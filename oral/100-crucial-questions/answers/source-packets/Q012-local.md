### Oxford's answer architecture

Oxford builds Q12 in this order, and this order is itself the exam-source teaching structure:

1. **Warning about the construct** — the *Introduction* (p. 253) opens by saying schizophrenia is "perhaps the most difficult to define and describe" of the major syndromes, that divergent concepts have been held in different countries, and that it remains the best example of psychiatry's unresolved problems of disease concept, classification and aetiology.
2. **Four symptom groupings** (p. 253–254): two classical (positive, negative) plus two more recently added (behavioural disorganization, cognitive symptoms).
3. **Acute versus chronic syndrome** (p. 254): the clinical description is *organised on this axis*. Acute = positive symptoms dominate; chronic = negative symptoms dominate. Oxford explicitly warns (p. 254) that the acute/chronic distinction "is an oversimplification; all features of schizophrenia can occur, and co-occur, at any phase of the illness."
4. **Subtypes** (p. 256–257), then two research subclassifications (Liddle, Crow).
5. **Other aspects of the clinical syndrome** (p. 257–259): cognition, depression, neurological soft signs, olfactory dysfunction, pain insensitivity, modifying factors, prodrome.
6. **Diagnosis and classification** (p. 259–267): history first, then DSM-5, then ICD-10, then an explicit DSM/ICD difference list, then schizophrenia-like disorders, comorbidity and differential diagnosis.

### Clinical picture / psychopathology

**The four groupings (p. 253–254, verbatim structure, paraphrased content):**

- **Positive symptoms** = delusions and hallucinations. Oxford states that *particular* types of delusion and hallucination — "called first-rank symptoms" — "carry greater weight in the diagnosis and help distinguish schizophrenia from other psychotic disorders."
- **Negative symptoms** = a loss of normal functioning, "often listed as 'four As'": **alogia** (decreased spontaneous speech), **avolition** (decreased motivation), **affective flattening** (lack of emotional expressivity, *but not depression*), **anhedonia**. Oxford explicitly notes these are "related to but distinct from Bleuler's 'four As'" (Box 11.5).
- **Behavioural disorganization** = formal thought disorder (abnormalities in the *flow and sequence* of thoughts — "in the past this was often considered as a positive symptom"), plus inappropriate affect and bizarre behaviour.
- **Cognitive symptoms** = attentional and memory impairments, "now often considered as a separate symptom category."

**Acute schizophrenia (p. 254–255).** Positive symptoms predominate and are often florid; behavioural disorganization, especially formal thought disorder, is also prominent; negative symptoms are less common and cognitive deficits less apparent, "partly because they are masked by the positive symptoms."

- *Appearance/behaviour*: may be entirely normal; or changed in non-specifically odd ways (preoccupation with health, appearance, religion); social withdrawal; unexplained smiling/laughing; perplexity; restlessness; sudden unexpected variability of behaviour.
- *Speech / formal thought disorder*: early vagueness that makes meaning hard to grasp; difficulty with abstraction; preoccupation with vague pseudoscientific or mystical ideas. **Loosening of association** between expressed ideas, detectable as illogical thinking (**"knight's move" thinking**) or **talking past the point (vorbeireden)**. Severest form: structure and coherence lost, utterances jumbled — **word salad** or **verbigeration**. Ordinary words used unusually (**metonyms/paraphrases**); coined words (**neologisms**). Disorders of the *form (stream)* of thought: **pressure of thought, poverty of thought, thought blocking, thought withdrawal** — "some of these constitute first-rank symptoms."
- *Hallucinations*: **auditory hallucinations are among the most frequent symptoms**; noises, music, single words, brief phrases or whole conversations; may be unobtrusive or severely distressing; some are command voices. **Gedankenlautwerden** (hearing one's own thoughts spoken aloud as one thinks them) and **écho de la pensée** (immediately afterwards); third-person voices discussing the patient or commenting on his actions — "these are first-rank symptoms." Visual hallucinations are less frequent and usually occur with other hallucinations. Tactile, olfactory, gustatory and somatic hallucinations occur in some patients and "are often interpreted in a delusional way" (Oxford's example: hallucinatory lower-abdominal sensations attributed to sexual interference by a persecutor).
- *Delusions*: "almost invariable in acute schizophrenia", although **primary delusions are infrequent and difficult to identify with certainty**. Delusions may arise from a **primary delusional mood (Wahnstimmung)**. **Persecutory delusions are common but are NOT specific to schizophrenia** (they also characterise delusional disorders and occur in all psychoses). "Less common, but of **greater diagnostic value**, are delusions of **reference** and of **control (passivity)**, and delusions about the **possession of thought**" — insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting.
- *Insight*: "almost always impaired"; most patients ascribe their experiences to the malevolent actions of other people rather than to illness.
- *Orientation*: usually normal (may be hard to test if there is florid thought disorder or preoccupation).
- *Mood*, three kinds: (i) anxiety, depression, irritability or euphoria — Oxford's caution: "if such features are sufficiently prominent and sustained, the possibility of schizoaffective disorder or other affective psychosis should be considered"; (ii) **blunting/flattening of affect**; (iii) **incongruity of affect**.
- Oxford closes the acute section by emphasising **variability**: few patients have all the symptoms, some already show 'chronic' features at first presentation, and "the overall **pattern and duration** of features are also taken into account before making a diagnosis."

**Chronic schizophrenia (p. 256).** Characterised by the negative symptoms of underactivity, lack of drive, social withdrawal and emotional apathy — "sometimes called a 'defect state' or **deficit syndrome**". Most striking feature is **diminished volition**. Inactivity for long periods or aimless repeated activity; social withdrawal; deteriorating social behaviour; poor self-care; hoarding and cluttered/dirty surroundings; breaking social conventions. Speech shows thought disorder of the same kinds as in the acute syndrome. Affect blunted, and when emotion is shown it is incongruous or shallow. **Hallucinations and delusions occur but are "by no means universal", and tend to be held with little emotional response** (Oxford's example: convinced of persecution but showing neither fear nor anger). Movement disorders — **stereotypies, mannerisms and other catatonic symptoms, and dyskinesias** — the latter "primarily but not entirely due to antipsychotic medication." **Cognitive impairment is common, if not universal**, and together with negative symptoms drives the low functioning and poor outcome; but "the cognitive deficits are rarely of sufficient magnitude to be apparent unless detailed cognitive testing is undertaken, and this is rarely the case in clinical practice." Positive symptoms may recur/exacerbate at any stage, in response to life events or discontinuation of medication.

**Cognitive features (p. 257–258)** — Oxford's fullest statement, and directly on the Q12 board-fact:
- Framed against Kraepelin's original *dementia praecox*; cognition "was for many years a neglected component."
- **Deficits are seen across all domains of learning and memory, with disproportionate involvement of semantic memory, working memory, and attention.**
- **"The deficits average about one standard deviation below expected performance, and are present at the first episode; IQ is also reduced."**
- **Executive function and attention may be the core deficits**, being seen even in patients with otherwise intact cognition.
- **Social cognition** deficits (perception/storage/utilisation of information about others and ourselves), including **marked impairments in theory of mind**.
- Time course: **reduced IQ already present in childhood** in those who later develop schizophrenia; further impairment at illness onset, partially improving after the acute episode resolves; **most deficits appear to be trait features**, largely independent of other symptom domains; later in illness **risk of overt dementia is markedly increased**, but not attributable to Alzheimer's or another recognised neurodegenerative disorder; **impaired cognition is also seen in attenuated form in unaffected first-degree relatives.**
- Why cognition is emphasised: (1) a major determinant of poor functional outcome; (2) a target for drugs and for cognitive remediation; (3) increasingly conceptualised as central to the disorder and as underlying the psychotic symptoms.
- **MATRICS Battery** named as a method to standardise cognitive assessment, "however, this has yet to become widespread in clinical practice."
- Crucially: **"Failure to achieve this goal, together with uncertainty about the diagnostic specificity of the cognitive deficits, contributed to their omission from DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia, despite their importance in the origins and outcomes of the disorder."**

**Depressive symptoms (p. 258).** Common in any phase and prominent in the prodrome. **About 25% of patients exhibit persistent and significant depression, called postschizophrenic depression.** Comorbid depression worsens functional outcome. Three candidate explanations given (integral part of schizophrenia — supported by "about 50% of patients with acute schizophrenia experience significant depressive symptomatology, which improves as the psychosis remits"; a postpsychotic response to recovered insight; a medication side effect — rejected as sole explanation because depressive symptoms occur without antipsychotics). **Examiner-relevant caution stated explicitly:** "Depressive symptoms in schizophrenia should not be confused with negative symptoms nor for the parkinsonian side effects of antipsychotic medication. These distinctions are important, but can be difficult to make." **Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia** named as useful here.

**Neurological signs (p. 258).** "Soft signs" — do not localise; abnormalities of sensory integration, coordination and sequencing of complex motor acts; catatonic features and dyskinesias may be included. Present in unmedicated first-episode patients, more common in chronic illness; correlate with cognitive dysfunction and developmental anomaly; regarded as a manifestation of neurodevelopmental origin. **Oxford makes a practical examination recommendation: "A neurological examination that focuses upon the extrapyramidal system should be part of the routine assessment of a patient who presents with schizophrenia."**

**Olfactory dysfunction (p. 258–259)** — deficits in odour identification, sensitivity and memory; not attributable to medication or smoking; associated with cognitive and negative symptoms. **Pain insensitivity (p. 259)** — long noted, occasionally extreme, cause unknown.

**Factors that modify the clinical features (p. 259).** Social/cultural background alters symptom *content* (religious delusions less common than a century ago, replaced by cloning, HIV, terrorism). **Age**: adolescents/young adults more often show thought disorder, mood disturbance, passivity phenomena, thought insertion and withdrawal; with increasing age, paranoid symptomatology with more organised delusions. **Intelligence**: patients with intellectual disability usually present with a simple clinical picture, "sometimes referred to as **pfropfschizophrenie**"; highly intelligent people develop complex delusional systems and are better able to articulate — or conceal — their experiences. **Social stimulation**: understimulation is thought to increase negative symptoms, overstimulation precipitates positive symptoms; psychosocial treatment is designed to avoid both extremes.

**Prodrome (p. 259).** The period when psychosis is "brewing" — identifiable symptoms before diagnosable criteria are met; also called the **at-risk / high-risk state**. Insidious, shifting, largely **non-specific** symptoms: mild psychotic-like positive and negative symptoms, commonly depressive and anxiety symptoms; cognitive and social functioning also deteriorate. **CAARMS** named. **"Importantly, many people who are considered to be prodromal do not progress to overt psychosis; typically, less than 25% do so during a 2- to 3-year follow-up."** Longer duration of untreated psychosis → worse outcome, with four candidate explanations offered.

**Chapter 1 support (p. 16).** Oxford's Ch. 11 explicitly assumes the reader has read Chapters 1 and 3. Ch. 1 p. 16 supplies the definitional layer for the disorganisation domain: three characteristic kinds of **loosening of associations**, "all of which are seen most often in schizophrenia" — **talking past the point (Vorbeireden)**, **knight's move or derailment**, **verbigeration** (extreme form = **word salad**); plus **overinclusion**, and **neologisms** ("occur most often in chronic schizophrenia", and the interviewer should ask the patient what he means by a word before calling it a neologism). Motor symptoms and signs listed there — **tics, mannerisms, stereotypies, catatonia** — are stated to be "mainly observed in schizophrenia, particularly catatonic schizophrenia."

### Diagnosis / classification

**Framing (p. 259).** "This section is concerned mainly with the diagnostic criteria for and classification of schizophrenia as specified in **DSM-5 and ICD-10**." Oxford insists the current approach is only understandable with historical perspective.

**Historical layer (p. 260–261, Box 11.5)** — this is examinable classic material in a Greek viva:
- Griesinger's **Einheitpsychose** (unitary psychosis) vs Morel's classificatory position; Morel's **démence précoce** (1852); Kahlbaum's **catatonia** (1863); Hecker's **hebephrenia** (1871).
- **Kraepelin** — division of **dementia praecox** vs **manic–depressive psychosis**, derived from course and outcome; first described 1893 in the 4th edition of his textbook. Three original subtypes (**catatonic, hebephrenic, paranoid**), later a fourth (**simple**). Separated **paraphrenia** (later onset, free of the emotional/volitional changes). Oxford corrects the common belief that Kraepelin thought the outcome invariably deteriorating: **13% recovered completely and 17% were ultimately able to live and work without difficulty** in his series.
- **Bleuler** — coined **schizophrenia** ("splitting" of psychic functions); **fundamental vs accessory** symptoms; **Bleuler's four As: Associations** (thought disorder), **Affect** (blunting, flattening, incongruity), **Ambivalence** (lack of motivation), **Autism** (social withdrawal and failure of theory of mind). "It is interesting that, in Bleuler's view, some of the most frequent and striking symptoms were accessory (secondary) — for example, hallucinations, delusions, catatonia, and abnormal behaviours."
- **Kleist**, **Leonhard** (systematic vs non-systematic; **cycloid psychoses**; affect-laden paraphrenia, schizophasia, periodic catatonia), **Jaspers**' process vs reactive distinction, **Langfeldt**'s true schizophrenia vs **schizophreniform states** (Oxford's warning: "According to modern diagnostic criteria, most of Langfeldt's schizophreniform states would be classified as **mood disorders**. (Note also that in DSM-5 the term schizophreniform disorder is used in a different way.)"), Scandinavian **reactive/psychogenic psychosis**.
- **Why criteria exist (p. 260):** UK/continental Europe used Schneider's first-rank symptoms to identify a narrow group; the USA diagnosed on the basis of mental mechanisms and included a much wider group; first-admission rates were much higher in the USA. This prompted the **US–UK Diagnostic Project (Cooper et al., 1972)** and the **International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (IPSS, WHO 1973)**, which led to agreed operational criteria and ultimately to ICD-10 and DSM-5.
- **Reliability ≠ validity (p. 261):** the term now denotes a syndrome that can be diagnosed reliably, "which is essential for rational clinical practice. However … reliability is not in itself sufficient. Because the cause of schizophrenia is still largely unknown, the syndrome remains of uncertain validity."
- **Current nomenclature debates (p. 261):** whether the word "schizophrenia" should be abolished (as already in Japan and South Korea) to reduce stigma and be replaced by e.g. **psychosis spectrum syndrome** (van Os, 2016); and whether psychotic disorders should be **dimensional rather than categorical**.

**DSM-5 as taught by Oxford (p. 262–263).** "Schizophrenia is defined in terms of the **symptoms, course, and impaired functioning**." Criterion A cardinal acute symptoms = delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, disorganized or catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms; **at least two** present for **at least 1 month** (unless treated); plus **criterion B** impaired functioning; plus **criterion C** continuous signs for **at least 6 months**. "The other criteria address the boundaries of schizophrenia with mood disorders, organic and substance abuse disorders, and autistic spectrum disorders." Shorter presentations → **schizophreniform disorder** or **brief psychotic disorder**. After **at least 1 year** since onset of active-phase symptoms, DSM-5 allows classification by number of acute episodes and whether currently in episode / partial / full remission; current severity of each criterion-A item may be rated on a **5-point scale**. **Catatonia:** DSM-5 does not divide schizophrenia into the classical subtypes, but includes catatonia as a category diagnosable as **comorbid** with schizophrenia.

*Box 11.6 (p. 263) — Oxford's own **"Simplified description of criteria for schizophrenia in DSM-5"***, reproduced structurally: A. two or more, each present for at least 1 month (or less if successfully treated), **at least one of which must be 1, 2 or 3** — 1 delusions, 2 hallucinations, 3 disorganized speech, 4 grossly disorganized or catatonic behaviour, 5 negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression or avolition). B. impaired functioning in ≥1 domain for a significant portion of time since onset; childhood/adolescent onset → failure to achieve expected level. C. continuous signs ≥6 months, including ≥1 month of criterion-A symptoms. D. does not meet criteria for schizoaffective disorder or a mood disorder with psychotic features. E. **"not attributable to the psychological effects of a substance … or another medical condition."** F. if history of autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia is added only if prominent delusions or hallucinations are present for at least 1 month in addition to the other required symptoms.

**ICD-10 as taught by Oxford (p. 263–264).** "The ICD-10 criteria … **place more reliance than DSM-5 on specific types of psychotic symptoms, including first-rank symptoms**. The diagnosis can be made after a duration of only **1 month**, excluding prodromal symptoms, and hence can be applied to recent onset cases which in DSM-5 would be categorized as schizophreniform disorder. Unlike DSM-5, **ICD-10 subclassifies schizophrenia into the subtypes described earlier**." ICD-10 includes **schizotypal disorder** among the schizophrenic spectrum, whereas DSM-5 classifies it as a personality disorder. ICD-10 has a group of **acute and transient psychotic disorders** with acute onset and **complete recovery within 2–3 months**. Schizophrenia-like disorders occurring in recognised organic disorders or secondary to substance use are **classified separately in ICD-10** but **included in the schizophrenia chapter of DSM-5**.

*Box 11.7 (p. 264) — ICD-10 criteria*: "a minimum of **one very clear symptom** (and usually two or more if less clear-cut) belonging to any one of the groups **(a)–(d)**, **or** symptoms from **at least two** of the groups **(e)–(h)**, should have been clearly present for most of the time during a period of **1 month or more**":
(a) thought echo, thought insertion or withdrawal, thought broadcasting; (b) delusions of control, influence or passivity clearly referred to body/limb movements or specific thoughts, actions or sensations, **delusional perception**; (c) hallucinatory voices giving a running commentary, or discussing the patient among themselves, or other types of voices coming from some part of the body; (d) persistent delusions of other kinds that are culturally inappropriate and completely impossible; (e) persistent hallucinations in any modality accompanied by fleeting/half-formed delusions without clear affective content, or by persistent overvalued ideas, or occurring every day for weeks or months; (f) breaks or interpolations in the train of thought → incoherence, irrelevant speech or neologisms; (g) catatonic behaviour (excitement, posturing, waxy flexibility, negativism, mutism, stupor); (h) 'negative' symptoms — marked apathy, paucity of speech, blunting or incongruity of emotional responses, usually with social withdrawal and lowering of social performance, **"it must be clear that these are not due to depression or to neuroleptic medication"**; (i) a significant and consistent change in the overall quality of some aspects of personal behaviour (loss of interest, aimlessness, idleness, self-absorbed attitude, social withdrawal).
Note that **(a)–(c) are essentially the first-rank symptoms** and are exactly the groups from which a *single* very clear symptom suffices.

***Summary of key differences between DSM-5 and ICD-10* (p. 264) — Oxford's own four-bullet examinable list:**
1. **ICD-10 places weight on Schneider's first-rank symptoms; DSM-5 emphasizes course and functional impairment.**
2. **ICD-10 requires a duration of illness of 1 month; DSM-5 requires a duration of 6 months.**
3. **ICD-10 recognizes traditional subtypes of schizophrenia (e.g. paranoid schizophrenia); DSM-5 does not.**
4. **Schizotypal disorder is included in ICD-10, but is categorized as a personality disorder in DSM-5.**

**Table 11.2 (p. 262)** aligns the two systems side by side. ICD-10 schizophrenia subtypes listed: paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic, undifferentiated, residual, simple schizophrenia, postschizophrenic depression, other, unspecified. DSM-5 side: schizophrenia (no subtypes), schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, other specified / unspecified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder, substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder, psychotic disorder due to another medical condition.

**Duration boundaries taught by Oxford (p. 264–265):**
- **DSM-5 brief psychotic disorder** — at least one acute-phase positive symptom from Box 11.6; lasts **at least 1 day but not more than 1 month**, by which time recovery has occurred including full return to premorbid functioning; may or may not follow a stressor; drug- and medical-illness-induced psychoses excluded.
- **DSM-5 schizophreniform disorder** — meets criterion A, lasted **more than 1 month but less than the 6 months** required for schizophrenia. **"Social and occupational dysfunction are not required to make the diagnosis."**
- **ICD-10 acute and transient psychotic disorder** — acute onset, **complete recovery within 2–3 months is the rule**; subdivided into *acute polymorphic psychotic disorder with/without symptoms of schizophrenia* (synonyms given: **bouffée délirante**, **cycloid psychosis**), *acute schizophrenia-like psychotic episode* (meets the symptom criteria for schizophrenia but **lasts less than 1 month**), and *other acute psychotic episodes*. **"The term schizophreniform disorder is not used as a discrete category in ICD-10."**

**Schizoaffective boundary as Oxford frames it inside Q12 (p. 265)** — kept brief here because Q13 owns it: DSM-5 requires an uninterrupted period with a major depressive or manic episode concurrent with criterion-A symptoms, **and acute psychotic symptoms present for at least 2 weeks in the absence of prominent mood symptoms** (otherwise the diagnosis is a mood disorder with psychotic features), while the mood disturbance must be present for a substantial part of the illness. ICD-10 is similar but specifies that both definite schizophrenic and definite affective symptoms must be **equally prominent and present simultaneously or within a few days of each other**; Oxford flags this as "an important point" and warns the label should not be applied merely for an isolated symptom or an insufficiently detailed assessment. ICD-10 also recognises **postschizophrenic depression** — prominent depressive symptoms for **at least 2 weeks** while some symptoms of schizophrenia (positive or negative) still remain. Oxford notes the nosological status of schizoaffective disorder is questionable, with **neither genetic nor neurobiological separation** from schizophrenia or bipolar disorder.

**Comorbidity (p. 266) — exact Oxford figures:** depression **50%**; lifetime prevalence of **any substance abuse 47%**, **alcohol abuse 21%**; **PTSD 29%**, **OCD 23%**, **panic disorder 15%**.

**Differential diagnosis (p. 266–267, Box 11.8)** — Oxford is explicit that the boundary between schizophrenia and schizophrenia-like disorders "is blurred and to some extent arbitrary", and that the organic/non-organic distinction "is also not only unhelpful … but becomes increasingly blurred as potential causes of some cases of 'schizophrenia' are discovered, such as **copy number variants and antineuronal antibodies**." Box 11.8 groups: (i) cases meeting some but not all criteria — brief psychotic disorder, delusional disorder, schizotypal disorder; (ii) cases with affective and psychotic symptoms — schizoaffective disorder, psychotic depression, bipolar disorder; (iii) schizophrenia-like disorders attributable to a specific cause — due to psychoactive substance use, organic schizophrenia-like disorder; (iv) other — autism and related disorders, OCD, body dysmorphic disorder, schizoid personality disorder, paranoid personality disorder.
Practical points Oxford adds: acute schizophrenia can be mistaken for **delirium** — look for clouding of consciousness and disorientation; secondary/organic examples given are **temporal lobe epilepsy (complex partial seizures), general paralysis of the insane, metachromatic leukodystrophy**; **"Visual, olfactory, and gustatory hallucinations are said to be suggestive of an organic aetiology"**; **one London study found 10 of 268 cases (3.7%) of 'first-episode schizophrenia' had an organic cause other than substance misuse (Johnstone et al., 1987)**; steroids and dopamine agonists can cause florid psychotic states; psychostimulants, phencyclidine and alcohol should always be considered, with urine or hair testing and temporal association helpful — but "the high prevalence of recreational drug use in young adults means that a clear distinction between a drug-induced psychosis and schizophrenia is not always possible."

**Epidemiological anchors that border the syndrome description (p. 267–268).** Low incidence, high prevalence. Lifetime prevalence **about 5 per 1000**; lifetime morbid risk **7.2 per 1000** (medians; means 5.5 and 11.9) — Oxford comments that the mean 11.9 "is more consistent with the frequent statement that about **1 in 100** people develop schizophrenia, but the value of **7 in 1000** is statistically more appropriate." Usual onset between **15 and 54 years, most commonly in the mid-twenties**; two peaks, one at **20** and a second at **33**; men have a single peak in their early twenties, women a broader range with a second peak in the fourth decade, but "the gender difference in mean age of onset is smaller and less robust than often stated." **Male:female ratio 1.4:1.**

### Classic examination concepts

**Schneider's first-rank symptoms — the exam source's exact treatment (Boxes 11.2 and 11.3, p. 255).**

*Box 11.2* — attributes them to **Kurt Schneider (1887–1967)**, with the deliberate warning "not to be confused with **Carl Schneider**, who categorized types of thought disorder." Their purpose: to make the diagnosis more reliable by identifying symptoms characteristic of schizophrenia and rarely found in other disorders. Oxford stresses that, **unlike Bleuler's fundamental symptoms, Schneider's symptoms were not supposed to have any central psychopathological role** — merely diagnostic value. Oxford then quotes Schneider (1959) directly, including "When any one of these modes of experience is undeniably present and no basic somatic illness can be found, we may make the diagnosis of schizophrenia. … Symptoms of first rank importance do not always have to be present for a diagnosis to be made."
Oxford's own appraisal, verbatim-in-substance: **"Studies have shown that first-rank symptoms are probably the most sensitive and specific means of making a diagnosis of schizophrenia. However, they are not always present in schizophrenia, and occur in 10–20% of people with other psychotic disorders. Moreover, in schizophrenia they do not predict outcome. For these and other reasons, first-rank symptoms were removed from the diagnostic criteria in DSM-5, and are expected to be omitted from ICD-11 (Tandon et al., 2013)."**

*Box 11.3 — the exam-source list, exactly eight items in this order:*
1. Hearing thoughts spoken aloud
2. Third-person hallucinations
3. Auditory hallucinations in the form of a 'running commentary'
4. Somatic (bodily, tactile) hallucinations
5. Thought withdrawal or insertion
6. Thought broadcasting
7. Delusional perception
8. Feelings or actions experienced as made or influenced by external agents (passivity)

**Subtypes as Oxford teaches them (p. 256–257).** "Schizophrenia is conventionally divided into several subtypes, based upon the predominant clinical features, especially during the acute phase(s)":
- **Paranoid** — "the commonest form"; persecutory delusions, often systematized, and persecutory auditory hallucinations; thought disorder and affective, catatonic and negative symptoms not prominent; **personality relatively well preserved**.
- **Hebephrenic (= disorganized)** — thought disorder and affective symptoms prominent; variable mood, silly and unpredictable behaviour; delusions and hallucinations fleeting and unsystematized; mannerisms common; rambling incoherent speech; **negative symptoms occur early and contribute to a poor prognosis**.
- **Catatonic** — motor symptoms most striking; activity varying between excitement and stupor; sometimes a dream-like (**oneiroid**) state. **"Formerly common, catatonic schizophrenia is now very rare, at least in industrialized countries"** — candidate reasons: change in the nature of the illness, improvements in treatment, or past misdiagnosis of organic syndromes with catatonic symptoms. Oxford records that catatonia has been argued to be a distinct syndrome (Fink et al., 2010), "and this is reflected to a degree in DSM-5."
- **Simple** — insidious odd behaviour, social withdrawal, declining work performance, no apparent positive symptoms. Oxford explicitly deprecates it: limited utility and "its history of abuse — for example, in the detention of political dissidents in the former Soviet Union (**'sluggish schizophrenia'**) — its use is now rare, and should be avoided."
- **Undifferentiated** — does not fit readily into any subtype, or equally prominent features of more than one.
- **Residual** — a stage of chronic schizophrenia when, **for at least a year**, there have been persistent negative symptoms but no recurrence of positive symptoms.
- Oxford's verdict: **"Despite their widespread use, research has shown that these subsyndromes of schizophrenia are not reliable, stable over time, nor associated with clear differences in pathophysiology nor prognosis. For these reasons, they have been removed from DSM-5."**

**Liddle's three subsyndromes (p. 257, Table 11.1)** — offered as biologically motivated alternatives to the classical subtypes, and mapping onto the three symptom groupings:
| Syndrome | Symptoms | rCBF correlates | Impaired psychological performance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reality distortion | delusions, hallucinations | left medial temporal lobe, cingulate cortex | disorders of self-monitoring |
| Disorganization | formal thought disorder, inappropriate affect, bizarre behaviour | anterior cingulate, right ventral frontal cortex, bilateral parietal regions | tests of selective attention |
| Psychomotor poverty | flat affect, poverty of speech, decreased spontaneous movement | underactivity of frontal cortex (**'hypofrontality'**) | word generation tasks, planning tests |
Oxford: "The most reproducible finding is the link between psychomotor poverty, impaired performance on frontal lobe tasks, and decreased frontal blood flow."

**Crow's type I / type II (p. 257).** Type I — acute onset, mainly positive symptoms, preserved social functioning during remissions, good antipsychotic response, associated with dopamine overactivity. Type II — insidious onset, mainly negative symptoms, poor outcome and poor drug response, no evidence of dopamine overactivity but structural brain changes (especially ventricular enlargement). Oxford's appraisal: **"Subsequent research has not strongly supported the biological subtypes and correlations predicted by the model."**

**German/French/Greek-viva terminology that Oxford itself supplies:** Einheitpsychose, démence précoce, dementia praecox, hebephrenia, catatonia, paraphrenia, Wahnstimmung, Gedankenlautwerden, écho de la pensée, Vorbeireden, knight's move, word salad, verbigeration, metonym, paraphrase, neologism, pfropfschizophrenie, oneiroid, bouffée délirante, cycloid psychosis, sluggish schizophrenia, defect state / deficit syndrome, soft signs, hypofrontality.

### Potentially edition-bound points

Flagged here **without modernising them** (Phase 1 discipline); adjudication in §5 and §7.

1. **The entire ICD side of the chapter is ICD-10.** Table 11.2, Box 11.7 and the four-bullet difference summary are all ICD-10.
2. **Box 11.2's forward-looking sentence**: first-rank symptoms "are **expected to be omitted** from ICD-11". This is a 2017 prediction, not a statement of fact about a published ICD-11.
3. **Box 11.2's appraisal sentence**: "first-rank symptoms are **probably the most sensitive and specific** means of making a diagnosis of schizophrenia" — a strong claim sitting beside the same box's admission that they occur in 10–20% of other psychotic disorders.
4. **Subtypes are still taught as live ICD-10 categories** ("Schizophrenia is conventionally divided into several subtypes"), even though Oxford records their removal from DSM-5 and their poor reliability.
5. **Negative symptoms are given as "four As"** with no fifth domain and no explicit primary/persistent-versus-secondary framework (although Oxford does supply the practical negative-vs-depression-vs-parkinsonism caution on p. 258 and ICD-10 criterion (h) makes the same point).
6. **Box 11.6 is expressly a "simplified description"** of DSM-5, not the criterion text, and it is DSM-**5** (2013), not DSM-5-TR (2022).
7. **Cognition is presented as "other aspects of the clinical syndrome"**, explicitly outside the diagnostic criteria.
8. **Catatonia is still presented as a schizophrenia subtype** in the subtype list, with the DSM-5 comorbid-specifier position noted only as a partial correction.
9. The **prevalence framing** ("1 in 100 … but 7 in 1000 is statistically more appropriate") is an Oxford editorial position, not a criterion.

Source: `Books\DSM-5-TR.pdf`. Criteria read in full at **PDF pp. 223–225 = printed pp. 113–115**; surrounding *Diagnostic Features* text read at **PDF pp. 225–226 = printed pp. 115–116**; chapter-level *Key Features That Define the Psychotic Disorders* and *Disorders in This Chapter* read at **PDF pp. 208–211 = printed pp. 101–104**. | **Substance / medical exclusion (E)** | The disturbance is **not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance** (e.g. a drug of abuse, a medication) **or another medical condition**. *(p. 114)* | Note the word is **physiological**. |

(b) **With catatonia** (coded additionally as **F06.1**, referring to the catatonia criteria on p. 135). | **Insight** | *Associated Features* (p. 116): lack of insight is described as **anosognosia** — "a symptom of schizophrenia itself rather than a coping strategy", the **most common predictor of non-adherence**, predicting higher relapse, more involuntary treatment, poorer psychosocial functioning, aggression and poorer course. | |
| **Five psychosis domains (chapter level)** | Schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders are "defined by abnormalities in one or more of the following **five domains**: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking (speech), grossly disorganized or abnormal motor behavior (including catatonia), and negative symptoms." *(p. 101)* | |
| **Bizarre delusions** | Defined at p. 101 (clearly implausible, not understandable to same-culture peers, not derived from ordinary life experience); delusions expressing loss of control over mind or body — **thought withdrawal, thought insertion, delusions of control** — "are generally considered to be bizarre." **No criterion anywhere gives bizarre delusions a special one-symptom status.** | This is the DSM-5 change away from the old DSM-IV bizarre-delusion shortcut, verified by the absence of any such clause in Criterion A. |
| **Catatonia's status** | Chapter text (p. 102): "Although catatonia has historically been associated with schizophrenia, **catatonic symptoms are nonspecific** and may occur in other mental disorders … and in medical conditions." Catatonia is handled as a specifier / separate diagnostic set (p. 103–104, criteria at p. 135). | |
| **Schneiderian first-rank symptoms** | The **only** mention of "Schneiderian" in the whole manual is in the **dissociative identity disorder** differential diagnosis (**p. 336**), where DID symptoms are said to resemble "some of the Schneiderian first-rank symptoms **formerly considered indicative of schizophrenia** (e.g. thought broadcasting, thought insertion, thought withdrawal, hearing voices keeping up a running commentary about the individual)." | DSM-5-TR's own word is **"formerly"**. There is no first-rank concept anywhere in the schizophrenia criteria or text. |

**Duration boundaries verified locally (DSM-5-TR):**
- **Brief psychotic disorder** — "lasts **more than 1 day and remits by 1 month**" *(p. 103)*.
- **Delusional disorder** — one or more delusions **for 1 month or longer**, Criterion A for schizophrenia never met, functioning not markedly impaired apart from the delusion's ramifications *(p. 105)*.
- **Schizophreniform disorder** — total duration including prodromal, active and residual phases **at least 1 month but less than 6 months**; "intermediate between … brief psychotic disorder … and schizophrenia"; **no functional-decline requirement**; diagnosed either when an episode lasted 1–6 months and the person has recovered, or **"(provisional)"** when still symptomatic before 6 months; **if the disturbance persists beyond 6 months, the diagnosis should be changed to schizophrenia** *(pp. 111–113)*. About **one-third** of those initially diagnosed schizophreniform (provisional) recover within the 6-month period; the majority of the remaining two-thirds eventually receive schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder *(p. 113)*.
- **Schizophrenia** — "lasts for at least 6 months and includes at least 1 month of active-phase symptoms" *(p. 104)*.
- **Schizoaffective disorder** (boundary only; Q13 owns it) — "a mood episode and the active-phase symptoms of schizophrenia occur together and were preceded or are followed by **at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without prominent mood symptoms**" *(p. 104)*.

## 4. Targeted supplementary local-textbook findings

Only two supplementary books were opened, each against a stated unresolved need.

### New Oxford Textbook of Psychiatry, 3rd ed. (2020)

- **Reason opened:** two identified needs — (i) whether a 2020 specialist Oxford text supersedes the 2017 Shorter Oxford framing of first-rank symptoms and of the symptom-dimension structure; (ii) whether any local source could supply **published ICD-11** schizophrenia requirements, since Oxford 2017 only predicts them.
- **Sections/pages:**
  - Ch. **57, "The core dimensions of schizophrenia"** (Nancy C. Andreasen), printed **pp. 565–569** = PDF **600–604**, including **Table 57.2** (varied content in delusions, p. 567) and **Table 57.3 "Schneider's first-rank symptoms"** (p. 568).
  - Ch. **7, "DSM-5 and ICD-11 classifications"** (Regier, Goldberg, Üstün, Reed), **Table 7.1** metastructure of DSM-5 and ICD-11 groupings, printed **p. 54** = PDF **89**.
- **Material additions / clarifications:**
  - **Three dimensions, not four.** Andreasen organises the phenotype as **psychoticism** (hallucinations, delusions), **disorganization** (bizarre speech and behaviour, incongruous affect) and **negative symptoms** (alogia, affective blunting, anhedonia, avolition), operationalised by her **SAPS** and **SANS** (p. 566). Cognition is **not** a fourth dimension here — attentional impairment is folded into the negative dimension.
  - **A "new five As"** (p. 568): DSM-5's two named negative symptoms (affective blunting/diminished emotional expression, avolition) plus **alogia, anhedonia and attentional impairment** — "Taken together, they may be considered to represent a new 'five As'."
  - **Table 57.3 supplies a nine-row first-rank list** with glosses: thought echo; voices commenting; voices conversing; somatic hallucination; thought withdrawal or insertion; thought broadcasting; delusional perception (defined as "a true perception, to which the person attributes a false meaning"); **delusions of passivity**; **delusions of control** — i.e. it splits passivity and control into two rows where Oxford Shorter's Box 11.3 has a single combined item.
  - **Appraisal of FRS is weaker than Oxford Shorter's** (p. 567): first-rank hallucinations and delusions "commonly occur in schizophrenic patients **but are also found in patients with psychoses due to mood disorders, medical illness, or the effect of substances**." No sensitivity/specificity claim is made.
  - **Historical synthesis useful for a viva** (p. 565): "DSM-5 represents a convergence of various points of view, with its Kraepelinian emphasis on course, specific delusions and hallucinations thought important by Kurt Schneider, and acknowledgement of the importance of Bleuler's fundamental symptoms."
  - **"Schizophrenia should be thought of as a diagnosis of exclusion because the consequences of the diagnosis are severe and limit therapeutic options. There are no definitive tests for schizophrenia, so the diagnosis rests on historical and clinical information."** (p. 566) — a sharper statement of the longitudinal/exclusion principle than Oxford Shorter makes anywhere.
  - **Thought disorder is transdiagnostic** (p. 567): derailment, poverty of speech, poverty of content, tangential replies "have all been found to occur in both schizophrenia and mood disorders"; manic patients show tangentiality, derailment and illogicality; "with the possible exception of clanging in mania, none appears to be disorder-specific."
  - **Course structure** (p. 569): prodrome (months to years) → active phase (at which point diagnosis can be made) → residual phase resembling the prodrome, with episodic acute exacerbations; "Schizophrenia may reach a plateau of severity at about 5 years without further deterioration."
  - **ICD-11 (Ch. 7, Table 7.1):** confirms only the **grouping name** — DSM-5 "Schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders" ↔ ICD-11 "**Schizophrenia spectrum and other primary psychotic disorders**", sourced to "World Health Organization, The ICD-11 Classification of Mental and Behavioural Disorders, Copyright (2018)". **No ICD-11 schizophrenia symptom threshold, duration rule, qualifier set or subtype statement is given anywhere in this book** (a full-text search for "first-rank" returns only pp. 42-area history, 565, 567 and 577 — none about ICD-11).
- **Did it change the proposed answer structure?** No. It **adds a competing dimensional scheme** (3 vs Oxford's 4) and a **competing negative "five As"**, both of which are adjudication items rather than structure changes.
- **Legacy-language caution:** p. 566 states that when duration is under 4 weeks the case "may be classified as either a brief psychotic disorder or a **psychotic disorder not otherwise specified**". "NOS" is DSM-IV terminology; DSM-5/DSM-5-TR use *other specified / unspecified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder*. This is a residual-legacy phrase in a 2020 book and should not be quoted as current nomenclature.

### Kaplan & Sadock's Synopsis of Psychiatry, 12th ed. (2021)

- **Reason opened:** one identified need — whether the classic American exam text frames the symptom groupings differently from Oxford (relevant to `bf.psychosis.cognitive_profile` and to what a Greek examiner may expect), and whether it corroborates or challenges Oxford's cognitive statements.
- **Sections/pages:** Ch. **5, "Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders"** — chapter opening PDF **1090**; *Cognition* subsection PDF **1096–1097**; *"The Symptoms of Schizophrenia Can be Divided into Three Groupings"* with **Table 5-1 (positive symptoms)** and **Table 5-2 (negative symptoms)** PDF **1099–1101**. ⚠ **Printed page numbers are not recoverable from this reflowed e-book PDF; do not cite printed pages for this source.**
- **Material additions / clarifications:**
  - **Three groupings: positive, negative and cognitive** — cognition *is* one of the three, unlike Andreasen's scheme and unlike Oxford's four. Negative symptoms are "sometimes also called **deficit symptoms**" and are "commonly associated with the progression of the illness." Cognitive symptoms "may be subtle, particularly early in the disease process, but are very impairing and **account for much of the disability** associated with this disorder", comprising impairments of **attention, working memory and executive functioning**.
  - **Cognition subsection (PDF 1096–1097)** corroborates Oxford closely: patients are usually oriented, and loss of orientation should prompt a search for a medical/neurologic disorder; MSE memory is usually grossly intact; **"In outpatients, cognitive impairment is a better predictor of the level of function than is the severity of psychotic symptoms"**; typical domains are **attention, executive function, working memory and episodic memory**; impairment is present at first episode and largely stable through the early illness; a small subgroup have true late-life dementia not due to Alzheimer disease; **impairments are present in attenuated form in non-psychotic relatives**; "these impairments cannot function as diagnostic tools" but have prognostic and treatment-planning value.
  - Insight framed as classically poor and linked to poor treatment adherence; reliability of the patient no worse than other psychiatric patients but collateral verification required.
  - Table 5-2 gives a granular negative-symptom taxonomy in SANS style: affective flattening (lack of vocal inflections, decreased spontaneous movement, paucity of expressive gesture, poor eye contact), **alogia** (poverty of speech, poverty of content of speech, blocking, increased latency of response), **avolition–apathy** (grooming and hygiene, impersistence at work or school, physical anergia), **anhedonia–asociality** (recreational interests, etc.).
- **Did it change the proposed answer structure?** No — **convergent**. It supplies a third variant of the symptom-grouping count (3 including cognition) and no material that Oxford + DSM-5-TR had not already established.

## 6. Exam-source facts/terminology to preserve

These should survive into the dossier even where the adjudicator later labels them historical or exam-specific.

**Oxford-specific framings**
1. Oxford's **four symptom groupings** (positive / negative / behavioural disorganization / cognitive) — the exam source's own architecture for "describe the clinical picture".
2. The **acute vs chronic** organising axis, **together with Oxford's own caveat** that it is an oversimplification and that all features can co-occur at any phase.
3. Oxford's **negative "four As"** — alogia, avolition, affective flattening (explicitly "not depression"), anhedonia — and the explicit warning that they are *related to but distinct from* **Bleuler's four As** (Associations, Affect, Ambivalence, Autism).
4. **Formal thought disorder was historically classed as a positive symptom** and is now placed under disorganisation.
5. **Delusional-value hierarchy**: persecutory delusions common but **not specific**; delusions of **reference**, of **control/passivity** and of **possession of thought** are "less common, but of **greater diagnostic value**".
6. **Primary delusions are infrequent and hard to identify with certainty**; **Wahnstimmung** (delusional mood) as their background.
7. **Gedankenlautwerden** vs **écho de la pensée**.
8. **Insight is almost always impaired**, usually attributed by the patient to others' malevolence.
9. The three kinds of mood alteration in acute schizophrenia (anxiety/depression/irritability/euphoria; blunting; incongruity), with the schizoaffective caution attached to the first.
10. **Deficit state / deficit syndrome**; diminished volition as the most striking chronic feature; delusions and hallucinations in chronic illness "held with little emotional response".
11. Cognitive anchors: **~1 SD below expected performance**, present **at first episode**, IQ reduced, **executive function and attention possibly core**, **social cognition/theory of mind** impaired, **childhood IQ already reduced**, largely **trait**, attenuated in **unaffected first-degree relatives**, **increased later risk of overt dementia not attributable to Alzheimer's**, **MATRICS**, and the reason cognition was **omitted from DSM-5 criteria**.
12. **Depression 50% in acute schizophrenia; ~25% postschizophrenic depression; Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia**; the negative-symptom / parkinsonism / depression three-way distinction.
13. **Neurological soft signs**, and Oxford's recommendation of a routine **extrapyramidal-focused neurological examination** at presentation.
14. **Pfropfschizophrenie**; the age and intelligence effects on presentation; the understimulation/overstimulation rule.
15. **Prodrome / at-risk state**, **CAARMS**, and **<25% conversion over 2–3 years**.
16. **Comorbidity figures**: depression 50%, any substance abuse 47%, alcohol 21%, PTSD 29%, OCD 23%, panic 15%.
17. **3.7% (10/268)** of "first-episode schizophrenia" had a non-substance organic cause (Johnstone et al., 1987); visual/olfactory/gustatory hallucinations as an organic pointer; steroids and dopamine agonists as prescribed-drug causes.
18. **Epidemiological anchors**: lifetime prevalence ~5/1000, morbid risk 7.2/1000 (Oxford's "1 in 100 vs 7 in 1000" comment), onset 15–54 with a mid-twenties mode and peaks at 20 and 33, **M:F 1.4:1**.

**Classic-history terminology likely to be probed in a Greek viva**
19. **Einheitpsychose** (Griesinger); **démence précoce** (Morel, 1852); **hebephrenia** (Hecker, 1871); **catatonia** (Kahlbaum, 1863).
20. **Kraepelin**: dementia praecox vs manic–depressive psychosis; four subtypes; **paraphrenia**; and Oxford's corrective figures **13% complete recovery, 17% able to live and work**.
21. **Bleuler**: coined *schizophrenia*; **fundamental vs accessory** symptoms; the **four As**; hallucinations/delusions/catatonia as *accessory*.
22. **Kurt Schneider** (1887–1967) — **not Carl Schneider**, who categorised thought disorder.
23. **Langfeldt** (true schizophrenia vs schizophreniform states; Oxford's note that most would now be **mood disorders**, and that DSM-5 uses "schizophreniform" differently); **Leonhard** (systematic/non-systematic; cycloid psychoses); **bouffée délirante**; **reactive/psychogenic psychosis**.
24. **US–UK Diagnostic Project (1972)** and the **International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (WHO, 1973)** as the reason operational criteria exist.
25. **Reliability without validity** — Oxford's central conceptual point about the schizophrenia construct.
26. Current nomenclature debates: abolition of the term (Japan, South Korea), **psychosis spectrum syndrome** (van Os, 2016), categorical vs dimensional.

**Exam-source lists to keep verbatim in structure**
27. **Box 11.3 — the eight first-rank symptoms in Oxford's order** (this is the enumeration the designated exam source expects).
28. **Box 11.7 — the ICD-10 (a)–(i) groups**, and the (a)–(d) *one clear symptom* vs (e)–(h) *two groups* rule.
29. **The four-bullet "Summary of key differences between DSM-5 and ICD-10"** (p. 264).
30. **Liddle's three subsyndromes with their rCBF and neuropsychological correlates** (Table 11.1), including **hypofrontality** ↔ psychomotor poverty.
31. **Crow type I / type II**, with Oxford's caveat that later research did not strongly support it.
32. The **six ICD-10 subtypes** with their distinguishing features — including **paranoid = commonest, personality relatively well preserved**; **hebephrenic = early negative symptoms, poor prognosis**; **catatonic = now very rare**; **residual = ≥1 year of negative symptoms without positive recurrence**; and Oxford's advice that **simple/latent schizophrenia should be avoided** (sluggish schizophrenia / Soviet political abuse).
