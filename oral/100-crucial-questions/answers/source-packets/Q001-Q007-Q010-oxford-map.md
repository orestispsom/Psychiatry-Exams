## 2. Shared material (extracted once)

### 2.1 Descriptive-psychopathology frame (Ch. 1, 2–4) → Q01, Q01a, Q01b, Q02
- **Descriptive psychopathology** = objective description of abnormal mental states, avoiding theory, limited to conscious experience and observable
  behaviour; "the fundamental professional skill of the psychiatrist". Classic authority **Jaspers, *Allgemeine Psychopathologie* (1913)**; modern
  text **Oyebode, *Sims' Symptoms in the Mind***.
- **Form vs content** — the key examinable distinction. Same content may be an auditory hallucination (psychosis) or an intrusive obsessional
  thought (OCD). *Form determines diagnosis; content matters for management and is what the patient wants to discuss.*
- **Subjective vs objective:** "As a rule, objective signs are accorded greater weight" — depression may be diagnosed on objective grounds despite
  denial of low mood.
- **Primary/secondary** used in two senses: temporal (preferred, no causal inference) and causal (arising from the pathological process vs as a
  reaction to a primary symptom).
- **Jaspers:** *Verstehen* (empathic understanding of the subjective experience) vs *Erklären* (explanation by external factors).
- **Single symptoms:** almost any symptom occurs in healthy people, including hallucinations. **Exception — an unequivocal, persistent delusion is
  itself evidence of disorder.** A single symptom mandates a thorough, repeated search, not a diagnosis — **Rosenhan (1973)**.
- Culture alters presentation (somatic depression in Asian populations), content (delusions of being cursed in sub-Saharan Africa) and meaning.

### 2.2 Structure of the assessment (Ch. 3, 35–60) → Q01, Q03, Q04, Q06, Q07
- **Three goals:** make a diagnosis; understand the context (life history, circumstances, personality — "why this disorder, in this person, at this
  time"); establish a therapeutic relationship. Process is **active, hypothesis-testing, iterative** — not a data sweep.
- **Box 3.2 history:** identifying data → informants → history of present condition → family history → personal history (Box 3.3: pregnancy/birth,
  development, childhood, education, occupations, relationships/sex, children, social circumstances, **substance use**, **forensic history**) →
  past psychiatric and medical history + **current medication incl. OTC/alternative remedies, allergies** → personality (Box 3.4).
- HPC: onset/duration/severity/fluctuation; **record expected symptoms that are absent** (e.g. "no suicidal ideation despite low mood"); temporal
  relation to physical illness and psychosocial problems; functional impairment; treatment and its effects.
- **Box 3.5 MSE headings:** **Appearance and behaviour → Speech → Mood → Thoughts → Perceptions → Cognitive function → Insight.** "Speech" = rate,
  quantity, volume, flow, latency, articulation; content deferred to "Thoughts". "Mood" conventionally carries anxiety **and suicidal thoughts**.
- **Informants** matter more than in the rest of medicine (unawareness, concealment, divergent personality accounts, carer burden). Consent normally
  required; exceptions include children and patients who are **mute or confused**.
- **Physical examination is integral** — for diagnostic signs (especially organic; **neurological and endocrine** systems above all), for drug
  side-effects, and for general health/nutrition/self-care. Every new inpatient gets a full examination.
- **Investigations:** "There is no one set of routine investigations applicable to every case", though FBC, renal, liver and thyroid function are
  conventional on admission.
- **Formulation (Box 3.11):** problem → differential diagnosis (for/against) → aetiology → treatment plan → prognosis. Aetiology = **predisposing /
  precipitating / perpetuating**, crossed with **bio / psycho / social**. **Box 3.8** topics: diagnosis, dysfunction, **risk to self and others**,
  effects on others, history, circumstances, personality, aetiology, treatment, prognosis, patient's understanding.
- **Interviewer safety, considered before every interview:** someone knows where/when/how long; help can be called; **neither patient nor
  obstruction between you and the exit**; potential weapons removed from sight. If risk is high or these cannot be met, **defer the interview**.

### 2.3 Emergency-modified assessment (Ch. 3, 54, 57) → Q03, Q04, Q06, Q07
- Interview must be **brief, focused, and produce a provisional diagnosis plus an immediate plan**. **The diagnoses usually in question are the
  psychoses (schizophrenia, mania, drug-induced) and delirium/other organic brain disorders.**
- Decide what must be answered *now* — the questions bearing on immediate management: **detain or not; investigations; medication; concurrent acute
  medical treatment.** Gather collateral first (accompanying persons, the patient's belongings, old notes, involved professionals); plan the
  location and manner of the interview for safety.
- **Emergency core dataset (8):** presenting problem (onset/course/severity) · other relevant symptoms · psychiatric and medical history · current
  medication · alcohol and drugs · recent stressors · social circumstances and support · **risk to self and others**.
- **Overactive patient:** limit questions, observe; in an emergency **some overactivity is a reaction to others' attempts to restrain**, and "a
  quiet but confident approach may calm the patient enough to allow more adequate examination." **Confused patient:** test cognition early,
  orientate and reassure, restart simplified, get an informant.
- **Mute/stuporose patient:** informant essential; **some stuporose patients switch abruptly from inactivity to violent overactivity — have help
  available**; allow time and several topics before declaring mutism; offer written communication; note whether eyes are open, track objects, or
  resist opening; **physical and neurological examination essential; specifically seek catatonic signs.**

### 2.4 Risk assessment, general (Ch. 3, 52–53; Ch. 18, 534–535) → Q04, Q05, Q05a, Q06
- Risk = harm to others (violence, neglect) **and** to self (suicide, DSH, neglect). **"All assessments, however brief, should include assessment of
  risk"**, with acute risks stated in the record. Failure to document risk assessment and management plan is "a common criticism of enquiries that
  follow homicides and suicides involving psychiatric patients." Risk assessment is **shared** with the team, **reviewed regularly**, and sometimes
  **disclosed to a person at risk**.
- **Two approaches to violence risk:** clinical and actuarial. Actuarial methods showed "low correlations between predicted and observed behaviour";
  newer instruments are better but **"may be more useful in predicting those *not* likely to be violent than those who are"** (Fazel 2012).
  Schedules **cannot replace thorough, repeated clinical assessment**.
- Oxford's explicit caution: most patients pose no risk and **are more likely to be victims than perpetrators**; assuming otherwise is unwarranted
  and stigmatizing. Citing Bohr — "Prediction is very difficult, especially about the future" — **even a complete risk assessment is only a weak
  guide**; many violent acts are by people with no history, and most with multiple risk factors never offend.

### 2.5 The "is this secondary?" rule (Ch. 14, 389) → Q01, Q03, Q10a
Secondary syndromes are **phenomenologically indistinguishable**; recognition depends on **associated features**. Four criteria:
(1) evidence of cerebral or physical disease known to be associated with that syndrome; (2) a **temporal relationship** (weeks to a few months);
(3) **recovery** when the cause is removed or improves; (4) **absence** of an alternative psychological explanation (no family history of mood
disorder, no relevant traits, no previous episodes, no recent life events).
**Table 14.6 causes** — *Psychosis*: temporal lobe disorders, Huntington's, basal ganglia lesions, dementias, endocrinopathies, metabolic disorders,
cerebral vasculitides, neurosyphilis, **limbic encephalitis**. *Mood disorder*: Alzheimer's, stroke, head trauma, Parkinson's, MS, Huntington's,
endocrinopathies, metabolic (**especially hypercalcaemia**), neurosyphilis. *Personality change*: frontotemporal dementias, frontal lesions,
Huntington's, focal basal ganglia lesions, neurosyphilis, prion disease, paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis. *OCD phenomena*: frontotemporal
dementias, complex partial seizures, basal ganglia disorders, Rett syndrome.

### 2.6 Delirium (Ch. 14, 351–354) → Q01a, Q03, Q07, Q10
- **Global impairment of consciousness (clouding)** → reduced alertness, attention, perception, cognition. Older terms: acute confusional state,
  acute brain failure, acute organic syndrome; **"delirium" preferred in both ICD-10 and DSM-5**.
- **Cardinal feature = disturbed consciousness**, but "the first clue is often one of its other features." Hyperactive and hypoactive forms, both
  with repetitive purposeless movements. Thinking slow, muddled, "dream-like"; transient poorly elaborated persecutory delusions and ideas of
  reference; **visual perception distorted — illusions, misinterpretations, visual hallucinations**; tactile/auditory hallucinations too;
  attention and registration particularly impaired; **amnesia for the episode on recovery**.
- **Box 14.2:** clouding · impaired attention · disorientation for time and place · impaired memory · psychotic-like symptoms · agitation,
  irritability, labile affect, word-finding difficulty · **rapid onset, 24-hour fluctuation, worse at night, reversed sleep–wake cycle**. Commonest
  screening tool: **Confusion Assessment Method**.
- **Box 14.1 predisposing:** dementia, previous delirium, functional and sensory impairment, cerebrovascular disease, alcohol misuse, older age.
  **Precipitating:** prescribed drugs (steroids, digoxin, diuretics), psychotropics (sedatives, opiates, hypnotics), **alcohol intoxication /
  withdrawal / DTs**, polypharmacy; septicaemia, infection (UTI, respiratory), hypoxia, organ failure, abnormal albumin, metabolic acidosis,
  hypo/hyperglycaemia, dehydration; post-ictal, head injury, space-occupying lesion, encephalitis; restraints, catheter, pain, sleep deprivation,
  constipation. In the elderly in medical settings: **drugs (especially polypharmacy) and infection**.
- **"Delirium is a medical emergency."** Clinical diagnosis; find and treat the cause; regular vital signs; **drugs should always be suspected until
  another cause is evidenced.** **Box 14.3:** FBC, U&E, renal, LFT, calcium, random glucose, blood cultures, ABG, syphilis serology; urinalysis,
  CXR, **drug screen**, cardiac enzymes, CT/MRI, EEG, LP. **Mainstay is non-pharmacological** (explanation, reorientation, reassurance, staff
  continuity, quiet single room, relatives visiting, adequate night lighting).
- **EEG is specifically useful in the differential diagnosis of stupor — a normal EEG suggests a dissociative state** — and in non-convulsive status.

## 3. Question-by-question map

### Q01 — Initial psychiatric assessment (+Q01a hallucination phenomenology, +Q01b formal thought disorder)

**Skeleton (Q01):** three goals → preparation (safety, collateral) → history by Box 3.2 → **MSE by the seven Box 3.5 headings** → physical
examination and targeted investigations → **risk assessment (always)** → **formulation** (Box 3.11; predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating ×
bio/psycho/social) → management and prognosis. Say the process is iterative and **adapted to setting** (§2.3).

**Q01a — stable definitions (Ch. 1, 6–8):**
- **Illusion** = *misperception of an external stimulus*; occurs when sensory stimulation is reduced and attention unfocused (the bush at dusk);
  commoner when **consciousness is reduced, as in delirium**, or when anxious. **"Illusions have no diagnostic significance, but need to be
  distinguished from hallucinations."**
- **Hallucination** = *a percept experienced in the absence of an external stimulus to the corresponding sense organ*; differs from illusion in
  being experienced as **originating in the outside world or within the body** rather than as imagined; **cannot be terminated at will**.
- Normal-comparison end: **imagery**, **eidetic imagery**, **pareidolia** (imagery persisting alongside a weak percept).
- **Pseudohallucination** — two meanings: (1) located **within the mind**, not external space (like imagery, but not dismissible by will);
  (2) apparently external but **seems unreal**. Oxford's practical stance: both hard to apply; decide only whether it is a **true** hallucination,
  since only that carries diagnostic weight — otherwise **describe** the experience rather than labelling it.
- **Non-morbid / non-psychiatric hallucinatory phenomena:** **hypnagogic** and **hypnopompic** (often hearing one's name — common in **narcolepsy**);
  hallucinations of the deceased in **bereavement**; **sensory deprivation**; **peripheral blindness or deafness**; visual-pathway disease;
  **epilepsy**; **Charles Bonnet syndrome**. **Autoscopic hallucination** in healthy people under sensory deprivation = **out-of-body experience**;
  after near-fatal accident/cardiac arrest = **near-death experience**.
- **Box 1.1 classification:** by **complexity** — *elementary* (bangs, whistles, flashes) vs *complex* (voices, music, faces, scenes); by
  **modality** — auditory, visual, olfactory/gustatory, somatic (tactile and deep); by **special features** — second-person, third-person,
  ***Gedankenlautwerden***, ***écho de la pensée***, **extracampine**, **autoscopic**, **reflex**, hypnagogic/hypnopompic. Also **lilliputian**,
  **haptic**, **formication**, **Doppelgänger**.
- **Diagnostic weighting:** only **clearly heard voices** (not noises or music) count; **third-person** voices and voices that **anticipate, echo or
  repeat** thoughts strongly suggest schizophrenia; **second-person** voices are non-specific — but **derogatory content accepted as justified**
  suggests severe depression, whereas in schizophrenia the patient **resents** it. **Visual hallucinations should always suggest an organic
  disorder** (content is diagnostically unhelpful); **autoscopic** likewise (e.g. temporal lobe epilepsy). **Taste/smell** — schizophrenia, severe
  depression, TLE, olfactory-pathway tumours. **Tactile/somatic** — schizophrenia if bizarre; **formication → cocaine**.
- **Bedside technique (Ch. 3, 49):** open with "Some people find that, when their nerves are upset, they have unusual experiences." For visual
  phenomena, probe the **"as if" quality** and ask whether it is seen **"out there, or in your mind's eye"**; distinguish from **dissociative
  experiences** (sense of a presence one can converse with — histrionic personality, some religious groups; little diagnostic significance).
- **Substance comparator: alcoholic hallucinosis** (Ch. 20, 574) — auditory hallucinations, usually insulting or threatening voices, **in clear
  consciousness**, distressing; **not due to acute withdrawal**, may persist months into abstinence; now coded as **substance-induced psychotic
  disorder**. Contrast **delirium tremens** (clouded consciousness).

**Q01b — formal thought disorder (Ch. 1, 15–16):** Oxford separates disturbance of **thoughts themselves** from disturbance of the **thinking
process**, the latter split into **stream** and **form**.
- **Stream:** *pressure of thought* (mania) vs *poverty of thought* (depression); either may occur in schizophrenia; recognized via language, hence
  *pressure/poverty of speech*.
- **Thought block:** sudden, striking, repeated interruption experienced as **abrupt complete emptying of the mind**; "strongly suggests
  schizophrenia", more so if the patient interprets it unusually (thoughts removed).
- **Form of thought (= formal thought disorder):** "Each kind has associations with a particular mental disorder, but none is strong enough to be
  diagnostic."
  - **Perseveration** — inappropriate repetition of the same thoughts/answers; **dementia and frontal lobe injury** (not limited to them).
  - **Flight of ideas** — rapid topic change with **logical sequence generally preserved**; links via **clang associations, puns, rhymes**;
    **characteristic of mania**.
  - **Loosening of associations** — muddled, illogical, tangential; **does not clarify on further questioning — the more the interviewer probes,
    the less he understands.** Three named forms, commonest in schizophrenia: **talking past the point (*Vorbeireden*)**; **knight's move /
    derailment**; **verbigeration** (senseless repetition of sounds/words/phrases; also with severe expressive aphasia) → extreme = **word salad**.
  - **Overinclusion**; **neologisms** (commonest in chronic schizophrenia — before calling a word a neologism, ask the patient to repeat, spell and
    define it, and exclude mispronunciation, limited education, dialect, technical terms, family "private words").
- **Differentials Oxford insists on:** loosened speech differs from that of **anxious** patients (coherent once at ease) and of people of **low
  intelligence** (clearer with simplified questions and more time). Flight of ideas vs loosening is hard when speech is rapid — **record a verbatim
  speech sample**.

**Flag for verification:** none of this phenomenology is guideline-sensitive. Verify separately (i) whether **"pseudohallucination"** is still a
usable clinical label (Oxford already leans against it); (ii) **DSM-5-TR / ICD-11** terminology for perceptual and thought-form categories —
Oxford is ICD-10-anchored.

### Q02 — Delusion vs overvalued idea vs obsession

**Skeleton:** define each **by form** → give the three discriminators (conviction, insight/resistance, understandability from background) → note
that diagnosis never rests on one symptom.

**Delusion (8):** "a belief that is **firmly held on inadequate grounds**, **not affected by rational argument or evidence to the contrary**, and
**not a conventional belief that the person might be expected to hold given their educational, cultural and religious background**." Characteristic
but non-definitional: marked effect on feeling and action — often it is the **behaviour** that brings the patient to attention. Exception:
**double orientation** (delusion with little behavioural effect; chronic schizophrenia).

**Box 1.2 problems with the definition (high-yield):**
- Some normal beliefs are equally impervious to argument (the spiritualist) — these are **overvalued ideas**.
- ***Partial delusion***: use only during recovery when a full delusion is known to have preceded, or at onset when known in retrospect that a full
  delusion followed. Partial delusions in isolation are **not diagnostically helpful — akin to pseudohallucinations**.
- **Primary (autochthonous)** delusions arise without preceding thought; others explain another abnormal experience.
- Culturally shared beliefs are also impervious to argument — check with an informant of the same culture/religion.
- ***Falsity is deliberately excluded from the definition***: a delusional belief **can be true or become true** (classic example: **pathological
  jealousy**). "It is not falsity that determines whether the belief is delusional, but the nature of the mental processes that led up to it."
  Improbable accounts of persecution by neighbours sometimes prove true — investigate before accepting an idea as delusional.

**Sub-typology (Box 1.3):** by fixity (complete/partial); by onset (**primary/autochthonous** vs **secondary**, which may **systematize**); other
delusional experiences — **delusional mood (*Wahnstimmung*)**, **delusional perception** (new significance attached to a *normal* percept — the
perception is normal, the interpretation delusional), **delusional memory** (two forms, Fish 1962); by theme — persecutory, reference, grandiose,
bizarre, guilt, nihilistic (→ **Cotard's**), hypochondriacal, religious, jealous, amorous (→ **De Clérambault's**), **control/passivity**,
**thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting**; by other features — **shared delusion (*folie à deux*)**, **mood congruence**.
**Box 1.4:** "paranoid" strictly means more than "persecutory" (Lewis 1970; Hippocrates used it of febrile delirium) — prefer **"persecutory"**;
"paranoid psychoses/states" → now **delusional disorders**.

**Obsession (13):** "recurrent persistent **thoughts, impulses, or images** that enter the mind despite efforts to exclude them." Two characteristic
features: the **subjective sense of a struggle — the patient resists**; and the conviction that to think something makes it likelier to happen.
**Recognized as his own** (vs thought insertion) and **regarded as untrue or senseless** (vs delusion). Forms (Box 1.5): **thoughts, ruminations,
doubts, impulses, obsessional phobias, obsessional slowness**; plus **compulsions/rituals** (checking, cleaning, counting, dressing). Six content
categories: dirt/contamination, aggression, orderliness, illness, sex, religion (incl. **"scruples"**).
*Caveats:* resistance **diminishes when obsessions are longstanding**, and very intense obsessions may be held with **less certainty that they are
false** — so the distinction from delusion "in isolation" can be difficult; resolve with a careful history of other features (compulsions, other
psychotic evidence). Distinguish also from normal intrusive thoughts in tired/stressed people — requires **dysfunction and persistence**.
Obsessional traits also occur in **anankastic personality disorder**.

**Overvalued idea (14):** **Wernicke (1900)**, reviewed by **McKenna (1984)**. "A **comprehensible and understandable idea which is pursued beyond
the bounds of reason**"; may dominate life for years and affect action.
- **vs delusion:** (1) **content and basis are understandable once the background is known** (the woman whose mother and sister both had cancer
  becoming convinced cancer is contagious), whereas delusions and their explanations tend to be bizarre; (2) the **theme is culturally common and
  acceptable** (body shape in anorexia). Also, there is **a small degree of insight and willingness to entertain alternatives**, though the person
  always returns to the belief.
- **vs obsession** (the easier distinction): **no intrusiveness, no senselessness, no resistance**.
- **vs normal religious belief:** the latter is shared by a wider group, arises from instruction, and is subject to periodic doubt.
- **Crucial nuance:** overvalued ideas are **defined by form, not content**, with **no inviolable relationship to any diagnosis** — some morbid
  jealousy is frankly delusional; in hypochondriasis or dysmorphophobia the belief often has the character of an **obsession or worry**. Clearest
  exemplar: **body shape and weight beliefs in anorexia nervosa**. Also cited: dysmorphophobia, hypochondriasis, paranoid PD, morbid jealousy.
- Oxford's discharge of the difficulty: unequivocal separation is sometimes impossible, "but this should rarely lead to practical problems,
  **because diagnosis depends on more than the presence or absence of a single symptom**."

**Eliciting (Ch. 3, 48–49):** a delusion **cannot be asked about directly**; approach via what might explain the other experiences described; test
conviction with an **enquiring, not argumentative** manner; then exclude cultural determination. Traps: **thought broadcasting** vs "people can tell
from my face"; **delusions of control** vs religious/philosophical beliefs about divine control, vs feeling "out of control" in anxiety, vs
**obeying command hallucinations**. Obsessions: "Do any thoughts keep coming into your mind, even though you try hard to stop them?" — and **before
recording a thought as obsessional, be certain the patient accepts it as his own.**

**Flag for verification:** DSM-5's **insight specifier for OCD ("with absent insight/delusional beliefs")** and for BDD is absent from Oxford's
framing; the poor-insight/delusional-obsession interface must be verified against current classification, as must ICD-11's handling of overvalued ideas.

### Q03 — Red flags for medical / neurological / medication / substance cause

**Skeleton:** secondary syndromes are **phenomenologically indistinguishable** and recognized by **associated features** → the four criteria (§2.5)
→ red flags grouped as history / mental state / examination / drugs → examination and investigations → causes (Table 14.6, Box 14.1).

- *History:* **acute onset**; onset after a **fall** (subdural haematoma); **fluctuation over 24 hours, worse at night, reversed sleep–wake cycle**;
  atypical age at first presentation; known cerebral or systemic disease of a linked kind; **close temporal relationship** (weeks to a few months);
  **absence of the usual psychological determinants**; **polypharmacy, recently started or stopped drugs, OTC and alternative remedies**; alcohol
  and drug use.
- *Mental state:* **any clouding of consciousness / impaired alertness or attention** — the defining discriminator, since the first question in a
  neuropsychiatric assessment is "whether there is clouding of consciousness"; disorientation; impaired attention and registration; **visual
  hallucinations**; **autoscopic hallucinations**; **olfactory/gustatory hallucinations**; **perseveration**; **word-finding difficulty, dysphasia,
  dysarthria**; illusions and misinterpretations; perplexity; **catatonic signs**. (Clouding also appears in Box 3.7 as a violence risk factor.)
- *Physical:* **neurological (incl. cerebrovascular) and endocrine** systems most often need detail; **peripheral stigmata of systemic disease and
  alcohol dependence**; named signs — **Argyll–Robertson pupil (neurosyphilis)**, **optic disc pallor (B12 deficiency)**, **cranial nerve
  involvement (neurosarcoidosis)**; **parkinsonism or ataxia observed as the patient walks in**, tardive dyskinesia, drug-induced "wooden" facies,
  goitre. **Box 3.6 neuropsychiatric examination:** dysarthria vs receptive vs expressive dysphasia and their localizing value (expressive =
  anterior, receptive = posterior; left hemisphere in right-handers); **constructional / dressing / ideomotor apraxia** (right posterior parietal;
  with sensory inattention and anosognosia); **agnosia**.
- *Substances:* Ch. 25 names the medical conditions to consider behind acute disturbance — **post-epileptic states, head injury, transient global
  amnesia, hypoglycaemia** — plus alcohol as a sedation hazard. **Alcoholic hallucinosis occurs in clear consciousness; delirium tremens does not.**
  **Formication → cocaine.** Distortion of body size/shape in **migraine, epileptic aura, after LSD**; depersonalization/derealization after **sleep
  deprivation, sensory deprivation, hallucinogens**.
- *Causes and work-up:* **Table 14.6** and **Boxes 14.1 / 14.3** (§2.5, §2.6).

**Flags for verification:** (i) the **investigation panel** — Oxford's Box 14.3 and its "no single routine set" stance predate current expectations;
**autoimmune encephalitis antibody testing (e.g. anti-NMDAR)** is not offered, though "limbic encephalitis" appears in Table 14.6 — the largest gap
for a modern answer. (ii) Delirium **epidemiology** (1–2% community, 8–17% ED, 18–35% on admission, 29–64% inpatient) and **25% mortality at
3 months** — 2014 vintage. (iii) Current delirium guidance on screening tools (Oxford names only CAM) and on **prophylactic low-dose antipsychotics
/ gabapentin / melatonin**, stated by Oxford to have "some efficacy" — treat as outdated.

### Q04 — Suicidal intent and immediate risk assessment

**Skeleton:** safe setting and time → explicit, staged, direct questioning (with the myth-busting statement) → reconstruct the act/plan (intent,
planning, precautions against discovery, access to and lethality of means, final acts) → current intent and hopelessness → current problems and
whether they persist → diagnosis and mental state → protective factors and support → immediate management decision, with the acute risk stated and
documented.

- **"An explicit assessment of suicide risk should be part of every mental state examination."** "There is no evidence to warrant" the fear that
  asking suggests it — someone who has already thought of suicide "is likely to feel better understood when the doctor raises the issue, and this
  feeling may reduce the risk."
- **Staged questions (Ch. 3, 47):** "Have you ever thought that life is not worth living?" → "Have you ever wished that you could die?" → "Have you
  ever considered any way in which you might end your life?" → direct questions about **current intent and plans**. Ch. 21 adds **specific plans and
  preparations (e.g. saving tablets)**.
- **"The most obvious warning sign is a direct statement of intent."** "It cannot be repeated too often that there is no truth in the idea that
  people who talk of suicide do not do it." Repeated threats get discounted as manipulation, yet some repeaters do kill themselves; **just before
  the act there may be a subtle change in the way they talk about dying — oblique hints replacing former open statements.**
- **Box 21.9 — factors suggesting high suicidal intent:** act carried out **in isolation**; **timed so intervention is unlikely**; **precautions
  against discovery**; **preparations in anticipation of death** (will, insurance); **preparations for the act** (buying means, hoarding tablets);
  communicating intent beforehand; **extensive premeditation**; **leaving a note**; **not alerting helpers afterwards**; admission of intent.
- **Lethality is judged twice** — actual dangerousness **and** the dangerousness the patient believed the method had ("some people wrongly believe
  that paracetamol overdoses are harmless, or that benzodiazepines are dangerous").
- **Homicidal ideation must be sought in severe depression:** such patients "occasionally have homicidal ideas — they may believe that it would be
  an act of mercy to kill other people, often their partner or a child… Such homicidal ideas should always be taken extremely seriously."
- **Immediate management (617):** admission vs day-patient vs outpatient decided on **intensity of intent × severity of illness × availability of
  social support**. If immediate risk is high, inpatient care unless there is an effective **crisis team** or reliable relatives who understand and
  can fulfil the responsibility. **"It is best if in doubt to play safe."** Refusal + essential admission → compulsory order. Outpatients get **a
  telephone number for help at any time** — "frustrated attempts to find help can be the last straw."
- **Box 21.2 (community):** full assessment of patient *and proposed carers*; adequate social support; **regular review of risk**; **less toxic
  drugs** in adequate dose; **small prescriptions**; relatives involved in **safe storage of tablets**; immediate access to extra help.
- **Box 21.3 (hospital):** safe environment, adequate trained staff, agreed observation policy. *Admission* — assess risk, agree observation level,
  **remove objects usable for suicide**, agree plans and a visitor policy. *During* — regular review, **clear handover between shifts**, agreed
  action if the patient leaves without notice. *Discharge* — aftercare planned in advance, **prescribe in adequate but non-dangerous amounts**,
  follow-up with an action plan for non-attendance. Ward design: no jumping points, **ligature points removed or boxed in**, dangerous possessions
  removed, one-to-one nursing if risk is high.
- **Peak inpatient risk: home leave, immediately before discharge, and the first few weeks after discharge.**

**Flag for verification — all predictive claims.** Oxford itself says prediction "has a low sensitivity and specificity", cites **Goldstein et al.
(1991)** whose model "failed to identify a single one of the 46 patients who later committed suicide", and notes that among inpatients the general
risk factors "are present in so many of those admitted that they **differentiate poorly between degrees of risk**." Use this as the epistemic frame,
not as licence for numeric stratification. Also verify: the current standing of **risk-stratification tools and low/medium/high categorization**
(contemporary guidance has moved against them); **safety planning** as a named intervention (absent from Oxford); local observation-level terminology.

### Q05 — Post-attempt recurrence prevention (+Q05a risk factors and risk by diagnosis)

**Skeleton (Q05):** medical stabilization → **every patient gets a psychosocial assessment, not only a medical one** → the five questions → judge
the three risks (immediate suicide, further DSH, unresolved problems) → agree a plan the patient will accept → treat the disorder vigorously →
restrict means → assertive, empathic follow-up.

- **Terminology (Box 21.5):** *attempted suicide*, *deliberate self-harm* (UK: **all episodes survived, regardless of intent**), *self-injury*,
  *parasuicide*, *non-suicidal self-injury* — usage differs by region and with respect to intent. Choose terms carefully in Greek.
- **Three aims:** immediate suicide risk; subsequent risk of further DSH; current medical and social problems. Conducted so the patient **reviews
  their problems constructively**, because many will not see a psychiatrist again. **If consciousness is still impaired, delay the interview.**
  Obtain collateral — accounts may differ substantially.
- **The five questions:** (1) intentions at the time; (2) do they now intend to die; (3) current problems; (4) is there a psychiatric disorder;
  (5) what helpful resources exist. Then: continuing suicide risk? risk of further non-fatal self-harm? what treatment, and will they agree?
- **Five sub-questions on intent:** planned or impulsive (**longer, more careful planning → greater risk of fatal repetition**); precautions against
  discovery (judge by **the patient's reasonable expectations**, not what actually happened); did they seek help afterwards (serious intent if not);
  was the method thought dangerous (**actual and anticipated**; did they take **all** available drugs?); was there a **"final act"** (note, will).
- **Problem domains:** intimate relationships; children and relatives; employment, finances, housing; legal problems; social isolation, bereavement,
  losses; physical health; alcohol and drugs. **"The more serious the problems that remain, the greater is the risk of a fatal repetition"** —
  particularly **loneliness or ill health**.
- **Resources:** capacity to solve problems (best guide = **their record of coping with past difficulties**), material resources, help others will
  actually provide.
- **Psychiatric disorder is the rule:** with standardized assessment it is detected in **about 90%** of hospital-presenting DSH patients; depressive
  disorder commonest in both sexes, then alcohol/drug misuse in men and anxiety disorders in women; comorbidity with personality disorder frequent.
  **"The presence of an obvious precipitating event does not rule out the presence of a psychiatric disorder."**
- **Motivation (Box 21.6):** to die; to escape unbearable anguish; to obtain relief; to change others' behaviour; to escape a situation; to show
  desperation; to get back at others; to get help. Historical terms: **Stengel and Cook (1958)** — "action from the human environment" → **"a cry
  for help"**, reframed by Williams and Pollock (2000) as **"a cry of pain"**. Oxford's rule: **weigh a common-sense evaluation of the actions above
  the patient's later account of motive.**
- **Box 21.11 — principles of care after self-harm (the recurrence-prevention core):** monitor for further suicidal/self-harm thoughts · identify
  crisis support · reach a **shared understanding of the meaning of the behaviour** · **treat psychiatric illness vigorously** · attend to substance
  misuse · problem-solving · enlist family and friends · encourage adaptive expression of emotion · **avoid prescribing quantities lethal in
  overdose** · **assertive follow-up in an empathic relationship** · affirm hope and self-care.
- **Special situations:** patients who **refuse assessment or self-discharge have very high repetition rates** — gather collateral before they leave,
  involve GP/CMHT, hold pending assessment for detention if risk is high (**Box 21.10** capacity sequence: presume capacity if alert and conscious;
  assess it; act in best interests under common law if incapable, e.g. persisting intoxication; capacity changes over time; if a competent patient
  still refuses and there are no grounds for compulsion, explain consequences, document fully, give an emergency number, contact the GP, and
  **attempt contact the next day**). **Frequent repeaters** usually have personality disorder and insoluble social problems — shared care plan
  rewarding constructive behaviour, continuity through one person, specialist personality-disorder service. **Repeated self-cutting** — behavioural
  analysis of the sequence; build confidence and self-esteem; **admission carries risks and is advised against unless absolutely necessary**
  (imitation on wards). **Mothers of young children** — association with child abuse; ask about feelings towards the children and their welfare.
  **Children/adolescents** → CAMHS, not the adult DSH service; **over-65s** → staff aware of the higher completed-suicide risk.

**Q05a — risk factors and risk by diagnosis:**
- **Box 21.1 (Hawton & van Heeringen 2009) — distal:** genetic loading; personality (**impulsivity, aggression**); restricted fetal growth/perinatal
  circumstances; early traumatic life events; neurobiological disturbance (serotonin, HPA hyperactivity). **Proximal:** psychiatric disorder;
  physical disorder; psychosocial crisis; **availability of means**; **exposure to models**.
- **General clinical list (616):** marked **hopelessness**; **previous attempts**; social isolation; older age; depressive disorder especially with
  severe mood change, insomnia, anorexia and weight loss; **alcohol dependence** especially with physical complications or severe social damage;
  drug dependence; **schizophrenia**, especially young men with recurrent severe illness, depression, intellectual deterioration or a previous
  attempt; chronic painful illness; **epilepsy**; abnormal personality.
- **Box 21.7 — repetition of attempted suicide:** previous attempt(s); personality disorder; alcohol/drug misuse; previous psychiatric treatment;
  unemployment; lower social class; criminal record; history of violence; age 25–54; single, divorced or separated.
- **Box 21.8 — suicide *after* self-harm:** older age; male sex; past psychiatric care; psychiatric disorder; social isolation; **repeated
  self-harm**; **avoiding discovery at the time**; **medically severe self-harm**; **strong suicidal intent**; substance misuse (especially in young
  people); hopelessness; poor physical health.
- **Method caveat:** "a non-dangerous method of self-harm does **not** necessarily indicate a low risk of subsequent suicide, partly because the
  patient may have incomplete knowledge of the dangerousness of the available methods."
- **Methodology terms:** **the psychological autopsy** vs epidemiological studies; **Durkheim (1897/1951)**.

**Flags for verification — every number below is 2017-vintage; do NOT state as fact:**
1. "**90%** of those who die by suicide have a mental disorder at the time"; psychiatric disorders account for "over **40%** of population risk".
2. Diagnosis-specific risks: **depression ~4%**; **bipolar 10–15%**; **alcohol dependence 7%**; **schizophrenia ~5%**; personality disorder in
   **40–50%** of suicides. These are the classically over-quoted figures, revised by later cohort methodology.
3. "**40–60%** of those who die by suicide made a previous attempt"; "**two-thirds** told someone of their intentions".
4. **Owens et al. (2002):** repetition **~1 in 6 within 1 year**, **~1 in 4 within 4 years**; suicide **1 in 200 to 1 in 40 within 1 year**,
   **~1 in 15 within 9+ years**; comparator "about 1 in 10,000 per annum in the UK".
5. "Around **5–10%** of DSH patients require psychiatric admission"; "about **one-third** have a disorder requiring treatment".
6. Occupational suicide rates (vets ×4; pharmacists and farmers ×2); Western European rate "one to two per 10,000 per year".
7. **Treatment-effect claims** (Hawton 2005 meta-analysis): problem-solving therapy, brief psychodynamic interpersonal therapy, **DBT for BPD
   (single trial)**, **emergency contact cards** ("may be detrimental for repeat attenders"), outreach, **postcards** (positive in Australia, not
   replicated in New Zealand); also low-dose risperidone/olanzapine "to reduce tension" in repeated self-cutting.
8. **Prevention measures (Box 21.4)** and their evidence: coal-gas detoxification, catalytic converters, paracetamol pack-size legislation, bridge
   barriers, pesticide restriction, the **Gotland GP-education study**.

### Q06 — Violence / aggression risk assessment

**Skeleton:** state the epistemic frame (§2.4) → structure by the **four categories** → **past violence is the single best predictor** → separate
static from modifiable factors → say what you do with the assessment (share, review, document, warn where indicated, build a management plan).

- **Box 3.7 (asterisk = most important in its category):**
  - *Personal:* **previous violence to others\***; antisocial, impulsive or irritable traits; male and young; recent life crisis; poor social
    network; divorced/separated; unemployed; social instability; **parent with a history of violence**.
  - *Illness-related:* **psychotic symptoms\***; **substance abuse\***; treatment-resistant; poor compliance; **recently stopped medication**.
  - *Mental state:* irritability, hostility, anger; suspiciousness; thoughts of violence; **threats to people to whom the patient has access\***;
    **planning of violence\***; persecutory delusions; **delusions of jealousy**; delusions of influence; **command hallucinations to harm others**;
    **suicidal ideas with severe depression**; **clouding of consciousness**; lack of insight.
  - *Situational:* confrontation and provocation; situations associated with previous violence; **ready availability of weapons\***.
- **"A history of violence is the best predictor of future violence"** (Ch. 18: "the most useful is a history of past violence", Buchanan 2008).
  Seek it from the patient **and** relatives, medical and social services records, and in some cases **the police**.
- **Highest-risk combination named by Oxford: psychosis + substance misuse + personality disorder.**
- **Situational factors are "extremely important"** — compare circumstances at the time of past violence with the present. **"Enquiries should
  always be made about the availability of weapons."** The Box 3.7 "suicidal ideas" entry refers to homicide (usually of family members) by patients
  with severe, usually psychotic, depression — ties to Q04.
- **Ch. 18 structured review** (Fig 18.3: mental disorder, pre-existing vulnerabilities, substance abuse, current state of mind, social/interpersonal
  stressors, situational triggers, versus social/interpersonal protectors). Four key questions: a **consistent pattern of behaviour**?
  **circumstances that provoked violence before and may recur**? evidence of **willingness to change**? likely **response to treatment**?
- **Box 18.9 — dangerousness:** *history* — previous violence, repeated impulsive behaviour, difficulty coping with stress, unwillingness to delay
  gratification, antisocial traits, lack of social support, **conduct disorder**; *the offence* — **bizarre violence, lack of provocation, lack of
  regret, continuing major denial**; *mental state* — **morbid jealousy**, paranoid beliefs **plus a wish to harm others**, deceptiveness, lack of
  self-control, threats to repeat, poor attitude to treatment; *circumstances* — provocation likely to recur, alcohol/drug misuse, social
  difficulties and lack of support.
- **Threats of homicide:** "the assessment is much the same as for suicide threats but **with a lower threshold for intervention**" — ask about
  intent, motivation and **the potential victim**, and assess the mental state fully. Outpatient support may suffice; admission if risk is high;
  **it may be necessary to warn potential victims or the police.** Discuss the evaluation with colleagues.
- **Diagnostic associations (519):** **morbid jealousy** — dangerous because the offence may be repeated with a new partner; **PTSD** — via
  substance misuse, irritability/poor affect regulation, rarely dissociative episodes resembling the trauma; **delirium** — confusion and
  disinhibition; **dementia and post-head-injury** disinhibition; **epilepsy** — no excess in prisoners on meta-analysis, and after adjusting for
  familial confounding **not** associated with violent crime; automatisms very rare; **violence is commoner post-ictally than ictally**;
  **intermittent explosive disorder** — Oxford notes none of these impulse-control conditions "has been established as a separate diagnostic entity".

**Flags for verification:** any claim that a structured tool (HCR-20, VRAG, START) *predicts* violence — Oxford names none and states actuarial
methods have been unhelpful individually; current terminology (**structured professional judgement**) and current rules on documentation,
information-sharing and duty-to-warn (jurisdiction-specific; interacts with the Greek-law work in Q08/Q09). The epidemiological claims about
diagnosis and violence, including the epilepsy findings, are single-study-based.

### Q07 — Severe agitation in the emergency setting

**Skeleton (principles only):** safety of patient, staff and others → **assess while managing**, prioritizing the medical differential →
**de-escalation first** → environmental measures → escalate through restrictive interventions with continuous reassessment → treat the underlying
cause → post-incident review.

- **Sequence, Figure 18.2 (NICE NG10 2015, reproduced in Oxford):** **PREDICTION** (risk assessment, searching) → **PREVENTION** (de-escalation,
  observation) → in emergency departments **seek expert help from the on-call mental health team** → **CONTINUED MANAGEMENT**: rapid
  tranquillization, seclusion, physical intervention — with **continuous risk reassessment and continued de-escalation throughout** →
  **POST-INCIDENT REVIEW**. **Prolonged physical intervention is contraindicated.** Seclusion "should be terminated when rapid tranquillization, if
  given, has taken effect"; physical intervention may be used "to enable rapid tranquillization to take effect".
- **Box 18.8 de-escalation:** **one staff member takes the primary communicating role**, assesses for safety, seeks clarification, negotiates
  **non-confrontationally**; staff use **emotional regulation and self-management** over their own verbal and non-verbal signals (**body posture,
  eye contact**); use **a designated low-stimulus area** — and **do not routinely use the seclusion room for this**, as it may be perceived as
  threatening.
- **Oxford's own points (533–534):** potentially dangerous people can often be calmed by **sympathetic discussion or reassurance, preferably from
  someone they know and trust**; **do not challenge the patient**, but it is also **inappropriate to reward violent or threatening behaviour by
  making concessions**; **"every effort should be made to allow the patient to withdraw from confrontation without loss of face"**; medication must
  be followed by **appropriate monitoring**.
- **Assessment half:** interviewer-safety preconditions (§2.2) and the emergency dataset (§2.3); remember that **some overactivity is a reaction to
  others' attempts at restraint**, and a quiet, confident approach may itself suffice.
- **The differential is the real exam target (Ch. 25, 734):** though mania and schizophrenia may be hard to separate early, both must be
  distinguished from **psychosis secondary to medical conditions** and from **aggressive outbursts in abnormal personality**. Named medical causes:
  **post-epileptic states, head injury, transient global amnesia, hypoglycaemia**; add **delirium** (§2.6) and intoxication/withdrawal.
  **Antipsychotics that lower seizure threshold need caution in post-epileptic states**; **antipsychotics should be avoided in delirium due to
  alcohol withdrawal and in epilepsy** (benzodiazepine is standard for DTs); caution in **Lewy body dementia** and liver failure. In delirium,
  medication is limited to **severe agitation endangering the patient** and distressing psychotic symptoms, started very low and titrated.
- **Post-incident review (534):** future care and medication (in personality disorder, emergency medication may be needed but **maintenance
  medication is usually best avoided**; reduce provoking factors; teach constructive ways of managing tension); **psychological support for patients
  and staff who were assaulted**; **whether to inform the police** (opinion has moved towards involving them more often); effect on the **whole
  patient group**; whether **ward policy** should change.

**Flags for verification — everything pharmacological and procedural:**
1. Oxford's regimens (734), **not to be presented as current practice** — *oral*: add **lorazepam 1–2 mg** or **promethazine 25–50 mg** if already
   on an antipsychotic; otherwise **olanzapine 10 mg / quetiapine 100–200 mg / risperidone 1–2 mg / haloperidol 5 mg** (last combined with
   promethazine 25 mg; licence requires **pre-treatment ECG**, noted as often impracticable). *IM*: lorazepam 2 mg, promethazine 50 mg, olanzapine
   10 mg, haloperidol 5 mg (with promethazine), **aripiprazole 9.75 mg**. Safety statements to re-verify: **IM olanzapine must not be given with
   parenteral benzodiazepines**; olanzapine cardiovascular contraindications; **check for respiratory depression, especially in the elderly and
   physically ill**; **flumazenil must be available**.
2. The precondition — **"Drug treatment in this situation should only be used if psychological or behavioural approaches cannot be employed or have
   failed"** — is a sound principle, but current **restrictive-intervention law and monitoring requirements** (post-RT physical observations,
   seclusion review intervals, restraint positions) are essentially absent from Oxford and must be verified.
3. Figure 18.2 derives from **NICE NG10 (2015)** — check for updates.

### Q10 — Catatonia (+Q10a catatonia with fever / autonomic instability / rigidity)

**Structural note:** Oxford 7th ed. has **no dedicated catatonia section**; material is distributed across Ch. 1, 3, 9, 11, 14, 22 and 25 and is
assembled below (see §5).

**Skeleton:** recognize the syndrome from motor/behavioural signs → confirm by examination manoeuvres → treat it as a **syndrome with many causes,
not a schizophrenia subtype** → investigate for medical/neurological cause → **benzodiazepine (lorazepam) first, ECT if ineffective** → escalate
urgently if febrile or autonomically unstable.

**Signs Oxford defines (Ch. 1, 16–17):**
- **Catatonia** itself, narrowly: "a state of **increased muscle tone** that affects extension and flexion and **is abolished by voluntary movement**."
- **Catalepsy (waxy flexibility, *flexibilitas cerea*)** — the term for the tonus: limbs remain where placed, tone uniformly increased; includes the
  **psychological pillow**. **Not to be confused with cataplexy** (narcolepsy).
- **Stupor** (psychiatric sense, p. 19) — **immobile, mute, unresponsive, but apparently fully conscious**: eyes usually open and following objects;
  if closed, **eye-opening is resisted**; reflexes normal; posture maintained. **"Stupor may occur in catatonia."** **Terminological trap: in
  neurology "stupor" implies impaired consciousness.**
- **Posturing**; **negativism** (doing the opposite of what is asked and actively resisting); **echopraxia**; ***Mitgehen*** (limbs move into any
  position with the slightest pressure); **ambitendence**; **grimacing**; ***Schnauzkrampf***. Also in the same list: **tics**, **mannerisms**
  (apparent functional significance, e.g. saluting), **stereotypies** (regular, no obvious significance) — apart from tics, seen mainly in
  **catatonic schizophrenia**. Related: **verbigeration**, **oneiroid state**.

**Examining the unresponsive patient (Ch. 3, 57)** — see §2.3; the operative instruction is that physical and neurological examination is essential
and **catatonic signs must be specifically sought**.

**Differential diagnosis of stupor/catatonia across the book:**
- **Depressive stupor** (Ch. 9, 196) — the extreme of psychomotor retardation, motionless and mute; now rare; Kraepelin's 1921 description quoted;
  recall on recovery usually intact (Kraepelin's impaired recall may reflect clouding from poor fluid intake). **"Patients in states of depressive
  stupor may exhibit catatonic motor disturbances."**
- **Catatonic schizophrenia** (Ch. 11, 256) — motor symptoms predominate, activity varying between **excitement and stupor**; may appear
  **oneiroid**. **"Formerly common, catatonic schizophrenia is now very rare, at least in industrialized countries"** — possibly a change in the
  illness, better treatment, or **past misdiagnosis of organic syndromes with catatonic symptoms**. Oxford records the argument (**Fink et al.
  2010**) that **catatonia is a distinct syndrome**, "reflected to a degree in DSM-5": **DSM-5 abolishes the classical subtypes and allows catatonia
  to be diagnosed as comorbid** with schizophrenia, and (Box 9.3) as a depressive-episode specifier "with catatonia". ICD-10 retains catatonic
  schizophrenia as a subtype and has **"organic catatonic disorder"** (Table 14.1).
- **Organic:** delirium (stuporose at its most severe); post-ictal and non-convulsive status; Table 14.6 causes. **A normal EEG in a stuporose
  patient suggests a dissociative state** (Ch. 14, 351).
- **Dissociative stupor** (Ch. 22, 656) — motionless, mute, unresponsive to stimulation, **but aware of surroundings**; rare.
- **Leonhard's *periodic catatonia*** (Ch. 11, 261) — non-systematic schizophrenia with regular remissions; akinetic symptoms sometimes interrupted
  by hyperkinetic ones.

**Treatment principles Oxford actually states:**
- **Box 25.13 (RCPsych 2005): "ECT may be indicated in patients with catatonia where treatment with a benzodiazepine (usually lorazepam) has proved
  ineffective."** The **benzodiazepine-first, ECT-second** sequence is in the book — without doses.
- **Box 25.12 (NICE 2003):** ECT only for rapid short-term improvement of severe symptoms after adequate trial of other options and/or when the
  condition is potentially life-threatening, in **(1) severe depressive illness, (2) catatonia, (3) prolonged or severe mania**. ECT is also
  treatment of choice in severe depression with **refusal of food and fluids** or high suicide risk, and may be considered for depression with
  **stupor** or marked retardation.

**Q10a — malignant catatonia vs NMS (Ch. 25, 731–732):**
- **Oxford makes the link explicitly:** "Before the introduction of antipsychotic drugs, a similar disorder was reported as a form of catatonia
  sometimes called **acute lethal catatonia**." That sentence is the quotable bridge.
- **NMS picture:** rapid onset **over 24–72 hours** of severe motor, mental and autonomic disturbance with **hyperpyrexia**. *Motor* —
  **generalized muscular hypertonicity**; throat/chest stiffness → dysphagia, dyspnoea. *Mental* — **akinetic mutism, stupor, or impaired
  consciousness**. *Autonomic* — unstable BP, tachycardia, excessive sweating, salivation, urinary incontinence. *Laboratory* — greatly elevated
  **CPK**, raised WCC. *Complications* — pneumonia, thromboembolism, cardiovascular collapse, renal failure.
- **Onset "often, but not invariably, during the first 10 days of treatment"**; especially **high-potency compounds** (haloperidol, fluphenazine most
  reported) but reported with **any** antipsychotic including clozapine; **combined lithium + antipsychotic** may predispose.
- **Differential given by Oxford: encephalitis, and in some countries heat stroke** — plus, per the above, malignant/lethal catatonia.
- **Treatment principles: symptomatic.** Priorities — **stop the drug, cool the patient, maintain fluid balance, treat intercurrent infection**.
  **"No drug treatment is certainly effective."** Diazepam for muscle stiffness; **dantrolene**; **bromocriptine** "also recommended"; very ill
  patients need **intensive care** with intubation and paralysis and management of renal failure.
- **Rechallenge:** some patients have safely received the same drug again; nonetheless restart cautiously with an atypical with weaker D2 blockade
  at low dose, and **at least 2 weeks should elapse**.

**Flags for verification — the weakest area of this source:**
1. **Diagnostic criteria.** Oxford's Ch. 1 definition is narrow and pre-DSM-5 in spirit; it does not reproduce the **DSM-5 catatonia specifier
   (12 features, ≥3 required)** nor mention the **Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale**.
2. **The lorazepam challenge test** — absent from Oxford; verify existence, dose, interpretation.
3. **All benzodiazepine dosing, route and titration for catatonia** — Oxford gives none.
4. **ECT parameters, urgency, and whether ECT is first-line in malignant catatonia** — Oxford's boxes are **NICE 2003 / RCPsych 2005**.
5. **NMS numbers:** **~0.2% incidence**; **~10% mortality** ("appears to have been declining"); duration "1–2 weeks after stopping an oral
   neuroleptic, two to three times longer after long-acting preparations".
6. **NMS treatment specifics** — dantrolene and bromocriptine indications/doses, the role of **benzodiazepines and ECT in malignant catatonia and
   NMS**, and modern monitoring (CK, renal function, temperature, autonomic parameters). "Diazepam for muscle stiffness" must not be presented as
   current practice.
7. **Serotonin syndrome** is not offered as a differential — must be added.
8. **Risk of antipsychotics precipitating NMS/malignant catatonia in a catatonic patient** — safety-critical, absent from Oxford.
9. **Anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis** as a cause of catatonia — absent (only "limbic encephalitis" in Table 14.6).

## 5. Stop / escalation notes

1. **Q10 / Q10a is the one place where Oxford 7th ed. alone is insufficient for a competent board answer.** No catatonia section; the working
   definition is narrow and pre-DSM-5; **no DSM-5 criteria set, no Bush–Francis scale, no lorazepam challenge, no benzodiazepine dosing, no explicit
   malignant-catatonia entity** (only the historical "acute lethal catatonia" aside inside the NMS section), **no serotonin-syndrome differential**,
   **no anti-NMDAR encephalitis**. A supplementary contemporary pass is required before this question can be answered.

   All drug detail is 2017 UK-licence-specific.

   antibody testing, no LP/EEG/MRI pattern.

   oral framing is Oxford's own — risk factors structure clinical judgement; they do not predict.
