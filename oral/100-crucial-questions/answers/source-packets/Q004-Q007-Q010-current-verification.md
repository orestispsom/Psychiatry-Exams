# Q04–Q07 & Q10 Current Verification Delta

Scope: targeted current verification only. This packet updates only the vulnerable claims identified in `Q001-Q007-Q010-oxford-map.md`; it does not reconstruct the questions and does not address Q01–Q03 or Q08–Q09.

## Q04. Suicidal intent and immediate suicide risk

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct assessment of suicidal intent | KEEP | Retain a direct, explicit assessment of current suicidal thoughts, intent, planning, access to means, recent suicidal behaviour, precautions against discovery, perceived lethality, hopelessness, acute stressors, mental state, substance use, supports and protective factors. The immediate purpose is to understand the current suicidal crisis and what is required to keep the person safe, not to produce a precise probability of future suicide. | NICE NG225 §§1.5–1.7; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024, Modules A–C |
| Low/medium/high global risk categories | DROP | Do **not** use global “low / medium / high” stratification to predict future suicide or repetition of self-harm, and do not use such categories to decide treatment or discharge. NICE explicitly recommends against this. A patient labelled “low risk” can still die by suicide, while many people with multiple risk factors will not. | NICE NG225 §§1.6.1–1.6.6; rationale on risk assessment and formulation |
| Suicide-risk scales as prediction tools | UPDATE | Scales and checklists may structure information gathering or screening in particular settings, but they should **not** be used to predict future suicide, to determine eligibility for care, or as a substitute for clinical assessment and formulation. Their principal limitation is poor individual-level predictive value in a low-base-rate outcome. | NICE NG225 §§1.6.1–1.6.6; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024 |
| Risk formulation versus prediction | ADD | The contemporary distinction is important: **prediction** asks “what is the probability this person will die by suicide?”, which clinical tools cannot answer reliably; **risk formulation** collaboratively explains why risk is elevated now, integrating historical vulnerabilities, current/changeable factors, foreseeable future stressors, protective factors, access to means and the patient’s own account, then links these to a management plan. Formulation is dynamic and should be revised as circumstances change. | NICE NG225 §1.5.10, §1.6.6 and glossary definition of risk formulation |
| Immediate disposition and management | UPDATE | Disposition should be based on immediate safety needs, ability to engage, mental state, intoxication/medical needs, available supports, safeguarding concerns, access to lethal means and whether a safe collaborative plan can be implemented—not on a score or risk category. If the person cannot currently remain safe, urgent psychiatric containment/admission and the applicable Greek legal framework may be required; if discharge is appropriate, it should follow psychosocial assessment, a concrete safety/aftercare plan and clear continuity arrangements. | NICE NG225 §§1.7, 1.9, 1.10; Greek legal framework handled separately in Q08–Q09 packet |
| Documentation and communication | KEEP | Record the clinically relevant suicidal thoughts/behaviour, dynamic drivers, protective factors, means access, formulation, immediate safety actions, who was involved, and the follow-up/disposition plan. Avoid documenting a bare “low risk” label without the reasoning and management response. | NICE NG225 §§1.5–1.7; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024 |

### Q04 adjudication note

Oxford’s detailed enquiry into intent, planning, discovery avoidance, means, current problems and protective factors remains useful. The main current correction is **epistemic**: the assessment is for formulation and management, not categorical prediction.

## Q05. After a suicide attempt: reducing recurrence

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychosocial assessment after every episode | KEEP | A proper psychosocial assessment should occur at the earliest opportunity after self-harm/suicidal behaviour and should not be deferred merely because physical treatment is still underway. The assessment should explore the function and context of the act, current mental state, needs, strengths, vulnerabilities and safety considerations. | NICE NG225 §§1.5.1–1.5.17 |
| Treat the underlying psychiatric disorder | KEEP | Diagnose and actively treat coexisting psychiatric and substance-use disorders; recurrence prevention is not a stand-alone “suicide drug” problem. NICE specifically advises against offering medication **solely** as a specific intervention to reduce self-harm, while still treating indicated underlying disorders appropriately. | NICE NG225 §§1.11.1–1.11.10 |
| Safety planning | ADD | A collaborative **safety plan** is now a high-value core intervention. It should identify warning signs/triggers, internal coping strategies, supportive people/settings, family/friend supports, crisis and out-of-hours contacts, and concrete steps to make the environment safer. The patient should hold the plan and it should be shared according to their wishes. | NICE NG225 §§1.11.7–1.11.8; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024 safety-plan resources |
| Means safety | UPDATE | Means restriction should be collaborative and specific to the person’s likely methods. At assessment, identify the means used or contemplated and work to remove or restrict access to lethal means where feasible. This is not merely generic advice to “remove dangerous objects”; it should be linked to the actual suicidal formulation. | NICE NG225 §§1.7.1, 1.11.7; VA/DoD 2024 lethal-means safety guidance |
| Rapid follow-up and continuity | ADD | If ongoing safety concerns remain, NICE recommends **initial aftercare within 48 hours** of the psychosocial assessment. Continuity of staff/care, clear responsibility for follow-up, accessible contact details and written communication between services are important recurrence-prevention measures. | NICE NG225 §§1.10.1–1.10.2, 1.12.1–1.12.4 |
| Structured psychological intervention | ADD | For adults who self-harm, NICE recommends a structured, person-centred **CBT-informed intervention** (for example CBT or problem-solving therapy) specifically tailored to self-harm, started as soon as possible and typically delivered over **4–10 sessions**, adjusted to need. | NICE NG225 §1.11.3 |
| Discharge after an attempt | UPDATE | Discharge from general hospital should follow psychosocial assessment, an agreed management plan, discharge planning with relevant agencies, and specified aftercare with clear communication to primary care. Avoid using a risk score as the discharge gate. | NICE NG225 §1.9.6 and §1.6 |

## Q05a. Major suicide risk factors and disorder-specific risk

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classic clinical risk factors | KEEP | Retain previous suicide attempt/self-harm, current suicidal intent/plan, hopelessness, severe or worsening psychiatric symptoms, substance misuse/intoxication, social isolation, acute interpersonal/financial/legal crises, chronic or painful physical illness, recent discharge or care transition, and access to lethal means as clinically relevant factors. They should inform formulation, not be treated as a scoring system. | NICE NG225 §§1.5.9–1.5.10; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024 |
| Static versus dynamic factors | ADD | Separate relatively static/background vulnerability (for example previous attempts, longstanding illness, trauma history) from **dynamic and future factors** that can change management now (current intent, agitation, intoxication, insomnia, recent loss, treatment disengagement, imminent stressor, means access) and from protective/mitigating factors. | NICE NG225 §1.5.10 |
| Exact lifetime percentages by diagnosis from Oxford | DROP | Do not retain Oxford’s exact figures such as depression ~4%, bipolar disorder 10–15%, alcohol dependence 7%, schizophrenia ~5%, or personality disorder in 40–50% of suicides as board facts. Such figures depend strongly on cohort, era, case definition and denominator and are poor tools for individual prediction. | NICE NG225 risk-formulation framework; VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024 |
| Rank ordering psychiatric diagnoses by “suicide risk” | DROP | Avoid a rigid hierarchy such as “bipolar > schizophrenia > depression” for a viva answer. Mood disorders, psychotic disorders, substance-use disorders and personality pathology are all associated with increased suicide risk, but diagnosis alone is less useful than the person’s current symptoms, past suicidal behaviour, comorbidity, functioning, acute stressors and treatment engagement. | VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024; NICE NG225 |
| Prior attempt as a high-yield fact | KEEP | A previous suicide attempt remains one of the strongest clinically useful historical indicators of future suicidal behaviour and must be explicitly elicited, but it still does not permit deterministic prediction. | VA/DoD Suicide Risk CPG 2024; NICE NG225 |

## Q06. Violence or aggression risk assessment

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structured clinical approach | KEEP | Use a multidisciplinary, individualised assessment. Clarify previous violence/aggression; current symptoms and mental state; substance use; threats, intent and access to weapons; victim/target issues; triggers and contexts; personality/impulsivity; cognitive/communication factors; social/environmental stressors; treatment adherence; and factors that have previously reduced escalation. | NICE NG10 §§1.2.8–1.2.14 |
| Past violence | KEEP | Previous violent/aggressive episodes remain a particularly important historical factor and should be explicitly considered. It is not sufficient on its own and should be integrated with current dynamic/contextual factors. | NICE NG10 §1.2.9 |
| Static versus dynamic/contextual factors | UPDATE | Retain the static/dynamic distinction but broaden it to include **situational and environmental** factors. Risk management should target modifiable symptoms, intoxication, interpersonal conflict, frustration, environmental restriction, communication problems and known “flashpoints,” not merely list demographic or diagnostic predictors. | NICE NG10 §§1.2.7–1.2.10 |
| Actuarial / SPJ tools | UPDATE | Structured tools can improve consistency and may outperform unstructured judgement for defined purposes, but they remain adjuncts. NICE specifically suggests tools such as the **Brøset Violence Checklist (BVC)** or **DASA-IV** in inpatient settings to monitor near-term aggression and help develop a management plan. They should not replace clinical formulation or be presented as precise long-term predictors. | NICE NG10 §1.2.11 |
| Categorical prediction language | UPDATE | Avoid statements that a patient “will” or “will not” become violent, and avoid treating a score as deterministic. Use calibrated language describing relevant factors, plausible scenarios, immediacy, potential severity, likely targets, and what interventions reduce risk. Reassess when mental state, environment or circumstances change. | NICE NG10 §§1.2.8–1.2.14 |
| Risk management plan | KEEP | Translate the assessment into an operational plan: known triggers, early warning signs, successful de-escalation strategies, observation/engagement needs, medication strategy where relevant, victim/target safety, substance management, and contingencies if escalation occurs. Review regularly. | NICE NG10 §§1.2.10–1.2.16 |
| Communication and documentation | UPDATE | Document the basis of the formulation and management plan rather than a bare risk label. On transfer/discharge, share relevant risk information with the receiving team and, where appropriate and lawful, carers/other agencies. Confidentiality may be overridden by serious safety obligations, but jurisdiction-specific disclosure law should not be imported from UK guidance into the Greek answer without separate legal verification. | NICE NG10 §§1.2.13–1.2.14, 1.6.3–1.6.4 |

### Q06 adjudication note

Oxford’s warning that prediction is intrinsically weak remains clinically sound. Current practice is **structured, scenario-based and management-focused**, not “clinical judgement versus actuarial score” as two competing standalone approaches.

## Q07. Severe agitation in the emergency setting

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| De-escalation first | KEEP | Verbal and non-verbal de-escalation, reduction of stimulation, appropriate personal space, a calm measured approach, avoidance of provocation and use of a quieter area are first-line whenever safely possible. Restrictive interventions are reserved for situations where less restrictive measures have failed or cannot safely be used. | NICE NG10 §§1.3.12–1.3.16, 1.4 |
| Assess medical/organic causes while managing behaviour | UPDATE | Severe agitation is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Concurrently assess for delirium, intoxication/withdrawal, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, head injury, seizures/post-ictal states, infection, metabolic disturbance, medication toxicity and other acute medical causes, while also considering mania/psychosis and behavioural/personality-related causes. Do not let sedation substitute for medical assessment. | NICE NG10 §§1.5.3–1.5.10; Oxford differential remains clinically sound |
| Rapid tranquillisation definition | ADD | Rapid tranquillisation is **urgent parenteral medication** when oral medication is not possible/appropriate and urgent sedation is required. It is an emergency containment intervention, not routine antipsychotic treatment and not a way to accelerate treatment of schizophrenia or mania. | NICE NG10 rapid-tranquillisation section |
| Adult rapid-tranquillisation drug choices | UPDATE | Current NICE guidance recommends either **IM lorazepam alone** or **IM haloperidol plus IM promethazine**. If there is insufficient information, the patient is antipsychotic-naïve, cardiovascular disease/QT prolongation is present, or no ECG is available, NICE favours IM lorazepam. Drug choice must also account for intoxication, physical illness, pregnancy, prior response, interactions and total medication exposure. | NICE NG10 §§1.4.37–1.4.43 |
| Exact rapid-tranquillisation doses | DROP | Do not carry Oxford’s 2017 dose table into the final answer as a universal current standard. NICE NG10 deliberately specifies the **agents and decision logic** but not a single universal dose in the recommendation itself; exact dosing should follow the current local protocol/product information. This avoids importing old UK licensing details into a Greek board answer. | NICE NG10 §§1.4.37–1.4.44 |
| Re-dosing | ADD | Prescribe the initial rapid-tranquillisation medication as a **single dose** and do not repeat it until the effect of that dose has been reviewed. | NICE NG10 §1.4.44 |
| Post-rapid-tranquillisation monitoring | ADD | After rapid tranquillisation, monitor adverse effects plus **pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, hydration and level of consciousness at least hourly** until there is no further physical-health concern. NICE recommends **15-minute** monitoring when the patient is asleep/sedated, has taken alcohol/illicit drugs, has a pre-existing physical-health problem, has been harmed during a restrictive intervention, or maximum-dose concerns apply. | NICE NG10 §1.4.45 |
| Restraint/seclusion principles | UPDATE | Restrictive interventions must be the **least restrictive option**, used for the shortest necessary time, never as punishment, humiliation or dominance, with dignity and physical safety protected. Mechanical restraint is a last-resort intervention in the limited situations covered by local law/policy. The legal authority for seclusion/restraint in Greece should not be borrowed from the UK Mental Health Act framework. | NICE NG10 §§1.1, 1.4.23–1.4.36; Greek legal questions separate |
| Post-incident review | ADD | After restrictive intervention/aggression, review what precipitated the event, what de-escalation was attempted, adverse effects/injuries, how future escalation could be prevented, and update the care/risk-management plan. | NICE NG10 post-incident review recommendations |

## Q10. Catatonia: recognition and treatment

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Catatonia as a syndrome, not schizophrenia subtype | UPDATE | Current DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 frameworks recognise catatonia across mood disorders, psychotic disorders, autism, substance/medication states and medical/neurological illness. “Catatonic schizophrenia” is historical classification language, not the current conceptual frame. | BAP Evidence-based Consensus Guideline 2023; ICD-11 catatonia framework |
| Diagnostic threshold | UPDATE | A practical current threshold is **3 or more catatonic signs**. The syndrome includes decreased activity (for example stupor, mutism), increased activity/agitation and abnormal psychomotor phenomena such as posturing, catalepsy/waxy flexibility, negativism, mannerisms, stereotypy, grimacing and echophenomena. The 2023 BAP guideline explicitly recommends a ≥3-sign threshold consistent with DSM-5-TR/ICD-11. | Rogers et al., BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Rating scale | ADD | Use a structured examination/rating instrument when helpful; the **Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS)** is widely used clinically. A scale supports detection/severity measurement but does not replace conversion to diagnostic criteria or aetiological assessment. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Search for underlying cause | KEEP | Catatonia should prompt active assessment for psychiatric, neurological, medical, toxic and withdrawal causes. Collateral history, physical/neurological examination and targeted investigations are essential; treatment for catatonia need not always wait for every investigation to return. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Lorazepam challenge | ADD | When diagnosis is uncertain, consider a **lorazepam challenge**. BAP recommends **1–2 mg IV or IM, or 2 mg orally**, with reassessment after approximately 5 minutes (IV), 15 minutes (IM) or 30 minutes (oral). A marked reduction in signs supports catatonia but is **not perfectly specific**. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023, challenge-test recommendations/Table 9 |
| Lorazepam treatment | ADD | Lorazepam is the preferred benzodiazepine. Dose is titrated to clinical response/tolerability and may need to exceed ordinary licensed anxiolytic doses; BAP considers an adequate trial complete when catatonia resolves, dose escalation is stopped by adverse effects, or at least **16 mg/day** has been reached. Do not stop abruptly; taper after improvement. This is specialist off-label practice, not a routine starting dose. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023, GABA-ergic treatment recommendations |
| Role of ECT | UPDATE | Benzodiazepines and/or **ECT are first-line treatments** depending on severity, underlying illness and clinical urgency. If benzodiazepines do not produce remission, ECT should be used. ECT should not be framed merely as a remote last resort after prolonged medication trials. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Medical supportive management | ADD | Prevent and treat complications of immobility and reduced intake: hydration/nutrition, aspiration risk, pressure injury, contractures, infection, urinary complications, rhabdomyolysis and **venous thromboembolism**. Severe cases may require medical/high-dependency care, VTE prophylaxis and enteral/IV support according to clinical status. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023; contemporary hospital-medicine reviews |

## Q10a. Fever, autonomic instability and rigidity

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malignant catatonia recognition | ADD | Catatonia plus **hyperthermia/fever, marked autonomic instability, rigidity or rapidly worsening systemic illness** should trigger concern for **malignant catatonia**, a life-threatening emergency. Do not wait for a prolonged diagnostic work-up before beginning treatment and supportive care. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| NMS differential | UPDATE | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome and malignant catatonia overlap substantially. Recent dopamine-antagonist exposure or dose escalation strongly supports NMS, but phenomenology can be difficult to separate and both require immediate recognition, withdrawal of dopamine antagonists and aggressive supportive care. Also consider serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, severe infection/CNS disease and other causes of hyperthermic rigidity. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023, NMS section |
| Antipsychotics in malignant catatonia | ADD | **Stop/withhold dopamine antagonists** in malignant catatonia. Introducing or escalating antipsychotics in a febrile autonomically unstable catatonic patient can worsen the syndrome or precipitate NMS and should not be used simply because the underlying diagnosis may be psychotic. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Malignant-catatonia lorazepam | ADD | BAP recommends starting lorazepam at **8 mg/day** (oral/IM/IV), titrating according to response and tolerability up to **24 mg/day** in malignant catatonia. These are specialist emergency-guideline doses and require close medical monitoring. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023, malignant-catatonia recommendations |
| Urgent ECT in malignant catatonia | ADD | If malignant catatonia shows only partial or no response to lorazepam within **48–72 hours**, BAP recommends urgent bilateral ECT; ECT may be considered earlier when the syndrome is rapidly life-threatening or benzodiazepines are unsuitable. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| NMS management | UPDATE | In suspected NMS, immediately discontinue dopamine antagonists, provide intensive supportive care (airway/ventilation, temperature control, fluids, renal/electrolyte/rhabdomyolysis monitoring and complication surveillance) and escalate level of care as required. Depending on severity, BAP supports lorazepam and consideration of bromocriptine/amantadine, dantrolene in severe NMS, and ECT for persistent/severe cases. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023, NMS recommendations |
| Restarting antipsychotics after NMS | ADD | After an NMS episode, BAP recommends delaying antipsychotic reintroduction for **at least 2 weeks after clinical resolution**, then restarting cautiously with close monitoring if antipsychotic treatment remains necessary. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |
| Old Oxford NMS incidence/mortality numbers | DROP | Do not make Oxford’s ~0.2% incidence or ~10% mortality figures board anchors. Rates vary by era, case definition, antipsychotic exposure and clinical setting; prompt recognition and management are more important to this answer than legacy percentages. | BAP Catatonia Guideline 2023 |

## Exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q04/Q05:** classic lists of suicide risk factors remain useful for enquiry, but current practice rejects turning them into a numerical or low/medium/high prediction model.
- **Q05a:** retain the concept that psychiatric illness and prior attempts materially increase risk; drop old disorder-specific lifetime percentages and rigid rank ordering.
- **Q06:** Oxford’s scepticism about violence prediction remains correct, but current practice is more structured: use collaborative formulation and selected validated tools as adjuncts for defined settings, especially near-term inpatient aggression.
- **Q07:** Oxford’s de-escalation-first principle remains current, but its 2017 medication dose table should not be treated as the modern universal protocol; current NICE monitoring requirements after rapid tranquillisation are a major addition.
- **Q10:** Oxford’s benzodiazepine → ECT sequence is directionally sound, but contemporary practice recognises catatonia as a transdiagnostic syndrome, uses a ≥3-sign framework/BFCRS, supports lorazepam challenge and higher-dose treatment, and treats malignant catatonia as an urgent benzodiazepine/ECT emergency with dopamine antagonists stopped.

## Unresolved issues

- **Rapid-tranquillisation dosing for Greece:** NICE provides the contemporary adult agent-selection logic but not a universal dose table, and local Greek hospital protocols/product information may differ. Exact RT doses should therefore not be frozen as a board fact from this verification packet without a Greece-specific prescribing-protocol check.
- **Greek legal rules for restraint/seclusion:** this packet verifies only clinical principles. Any exact legal authority, documentation mandate or review interval for restrictive interventions in Greece requires a separate Greek-law check if the Writer needs that level of detail.
- No unresolved issue blocks creation of the Q04–Q07/Q10 answer briefs.
