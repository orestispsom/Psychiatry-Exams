## Q59. Adult ADHD / Q59a. No clear childhood impairment

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Developmental onset | UPDATE | Both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 retain ADHD as a **neurodevelopmental disorder with symptoms beginning in childhood**. The useful current anchor is **some ADHD symptoms before age 12**; Oxford’s ICD-10 “before age 7” rule is obsolete as current ICD practice. | DSM-5-TR; ICD-11 CDDR/comparison literature |
| Childhood diagnosis versus childhood symptoms | UPDATE | A **documented childhood diagnosis is not required**. Adults without a childhood diagnosis can still meet criteria if a careful developmental history supports childhood-onset symptoms that persisted and are not better explained by another disorder. NICE explicitly provides a referral pathway for adults with no childhood ADHD diagnosis. | NICE NG87/QS39 |
| Childhood impairment | UPDATE | Do not require proof that the person was obviously impaired enough in childhood to have been referred or diagnosed. High structure, high ability, family scaffolding or limited demands may have masked functional impact. What must be demonstrated is a credible developmental ADHD phenotype, with current clinically important impairment. | DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 architecture; NICE NG87; adult ADHD consensus |
| Missing collateral / school records | UPDATE | Parent collateral, school reports and other historical records are **valuable but not universally mandatory or always obtainable**. If absent, diagnosis should not automatically be rejected; instead reconstruct development carefully from the patient’s longitudinal history, old records where available, siblings/partners/other informants, and consistency across life stages, while explicitly recognising greater diagnostic uncertainty. Rating scales alone are insufficient. | NICE NG87; adult ADHD consensus |
| “Adult-onset ADHD” | UPDATE | Current DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 do **not** recognise truly de novo adult-onset ADHD without childhood symptoms. Apparent late onset should trigger reassessment for previously missed/subthreshold childhood ADHD, changed environmental demands, mood/anxiety/bipolar disorder, sleep disorder, substance use, TBI or other explanations. Late **recognition** is common; true adult-onset ADHD remains contested rather than an established current diagnostic category. | DSM-5-TR; ICD-11; European adult ADHD consensus |
| Cross-situational evidence | KEEP | Symptoms should be evident across **multiple important settings** and cause functional impairment, although their visibility may vary with structure, novelty, interest and environmental demands. Adult assessment should examine occupational/educational, domestic, social/relationship and practical functioning. | NICE NG87; DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 |
| Adult pharmacotherapy sequence | UPDATE | At high level, NICE recommends **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate as first-line pharmacological treatment for adults** when medication is indicated. If one stimulant is ineffective after an adequate trial, switch to the other; **atomoxetine** is the main non-stimulant option after intolerance/nonresponse to both. Dexamfetamine may be considered when lisdexamfetamine works but its longer effect profile is problematic. Adult guanfacine is not a routine first-line NICE option. | NICE NG87 §§1.5, 1.7 |
| Major prescribing risks | KEEP | Before stimulants/non-stimulants, assess cardiovascular history, pulse/BP, weight, substance misuse/diversion risk, comorbidity and current medication. Routine ECG is not required in everyone, but cardiac symptoms/history or interacting cardiovascular medicines may warrant further evaluation. Avoid simplistic “stimulants are contraindicated in all anxiety/tics/substance-use histories”; risk is individualised. Acute psychosis/mania requires reassessment and usually withholding ADHD medication until stabilised. | NICE NG87 |

### Q59 adjudication note
The Writer should say **childhood symptoms, not childhood paperwork**. Absence of school reports is a limitation, not an automatic exclusion. Current systems still require developmental onset before 12; they do not endorse a clean adult-onset ADHD category.

## Q60. Adult autism / Q60a. Masking and camouflaging

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current diagnostic architecture | KEEP | Adult autism remains a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition defined by persistent differences/difficulties in **social communication/social interaction** together with **restricted, repetitive patterns, insistence on sameness/restricted interests and/or sensory differences**, present from the developmental period and causing clinically important functional impact. | ICD-11 CDDR; DSM-5-TR; NICE CG142 |
| Developmental history | KEEP | A comprehensive adult assessment should reconstruct early development where possible and examine functioning across education, employment, relationships, daily living and sensory/environmental demands. Informants or school records improve confidence but are not always available; current features plus a coherent developmental history remain central. | NICE CG142 |
| Diagnostic tools | UPDATE | AQ-10 or structured instruments may support identification/assessment, but **no questionnaire, CAT-Q, ADOS or other scale independently establishes or excludes autism**. Clinical synthesis and developmental context remain decisive. | NICE CG142 |
| Masking/camouflaging | ADD | Camouflaging refers to **suppressing, concealing or compensating for observable autistic features**, for example rehearsed scripts, imitation, forced eye contact, suppression of stimming or deliberate social-rule learning. It is a behaviour/strategy, **not a separate diagnosis or diagnostic criterion**. | Contemporary systematic/narrative reviews |
| Under-recognition | ADD | Camouflaging can reduce the autistic features visible during short structured encounters and may contribute to delayed diagnosis, particularly in adults and in some women/girls. However, it occurs across genders and the magnitude of sex/gender differences is not completely settled. The Writer should avoid turning “female autism = masking” into a stereotype. | 2023–2025 camouflaging reviews |
| CAT-Q | DROP | Do not make CAT-Q a board requirement. It can quantify self-reported camouflaging in research/selected clinical contexts but there is no current requirement to use it diagnostically and the construct itself lacks a single universally accepted operational definition. | Camouflaging reviews |

## Q61. Intellectual disability / Q61a. Minimally verbal adult with aggression and insomnia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classification | UPDATE | Current ICD-11 intellectual developmental disorder/intellectual disability diagnosis is based on **significant limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour originating during the developmental period**. Severity should be described primarily by adaptive/intellectual functioning and support needs, not by rigid IQ bands alone. Oxford’s old IQ-only severity bins should not be taught as the current model. | ICD-11 CDDR; ICD-11 implementation literature |
| Adaptive functioning | ADD | Practical, conceptual and social adaptive functioning is diagnostically central: communication, self-care, independent living, health/safety, social judgement, money/time and community functioning matter at least as much as a single IQ score. | ICD-11/DSM-5-TR architecture |
| Diagnostic overshadowing | KEEP | New aggression, sleep disruption, withdrawal or self-injury must **not** be attributed automatically to intellectual disability. Actively seek pain and medical illness, psychiatric disorder, communication frustration, environmental change and medication adverse effects. | NICE NG11 |
| “Medical cause first” | KEEP | In a minimally verbal adult with abrupt aggression/insomnia, first exclude common occult causes such as dental pain, constipation, urinary retention/UTI, infection, injury, reflux, seizures, sleep disorder and medication toxicity/withdrawal, using carers’ knowledge of baseline behaviour. | NICE NG11; clinical ID practice |
| Medication review | ADD | Review anticholinergic burden, activating/sedating drugs, antiseizure-drug behavioural effects, akathisia/EPS and recent medication changes. Behavioural change may be the only observable adverse-effect signal in a person who cannot describe symptoms. | NICE NG11 / medicines optimisation principles |
| Antipsychotics for behaviour that challenges | UPDATE | Do **not** use antipsychotics routinely simply because behaviour is difficult. NICE allows them only when psychological/environmental interventions are insufficient, treatment of a coexisting physical/mental disorder has not reduced the behaviour, or risk is very severe; they should be combined with other interventions, have an explicit target behaviour, use the minimum effective dose and be reviewed early. This is a useful prescribing principle, **not Greek statutory law**. | NICE NG11 |
| Review/stop principle | ADD | NICE recommends specialist review after **3–4 weeks** and stopping if there is no indication of response by **6 weeks**. This can be an examiner follow-up but should not be transformed into a universal international licensing rule. | NICE NG11 |

## Q62. Anorexia nervosa / Q62a. ARFID

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| AN diagnosis | UPDATE | Current AN diagnosis should be presented without obsolete fixed BMI cut-offs: **significantly low weight relative to the person/developmental context**, persistent restriction or behaviours preventing weight restoration, and core weight/shape psychopathology such as intense fear of weight gain or persistent behaviour interfering with weight gain plus disturbed experience/overvaluation of weight or shape. ICD-11 permits clinically relevant presentations without requiring the old ICD-10 BMI <17.5 rule. | ICD-11 CDDR; DSM-5-TR |
| Amenorrhoea | DROP | Amenorrhoea is **not required** for current diagnosis and should not survive as a criterion. | DSM-5/5-TR; ICD-11 |
| Treatment backbone | KEEP | Treatment requires **nutritional rehabilitation/weight restoration plus eating-disorder-focused psychological treatment** and physical monitoring. For adults NICE lists CBT-ED, MANTRA and SSCM among principal options. | NICE NG69 |
| Medication | KEEP | Medication is **not a primary stand-alone treatment for core anorexia nervosa**. Treat comorbidity when appropriate, but do not present antidepressants/antipsychotics as substitutes for nutritional and psychological treatment. | NICE NG69 §1.3.24 |
| ARFID architecture | ADD | ARFID is characterised by avoidance/restriction driven commonly by **sensory sensitivity**, **fear of aversive consequences** (for example choking/vomiting) and/or **low interest in eating/low appetite**, producing clinically important consequences such as weight loss/failure to grow, nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements/enteral feeding or marked psychosocial interference. | ICD-11/DSM-5-TR ARFID framework |
| ARFID versus AN | KEEP | The key distinction is that ARFID is **not driven by weight/shape overvaluation or fear of fatness**. A low-weight adult can therefore have ARFID, while a normal-weight patient can still have clinically serious ARFID. | ICD-11/DSM-5-TR |
| Adult ARFID treatment | ADD | Adult evidence is less mature than for AN/BN. Current practice is multidisciplinary: correct nutritional/medical deficits, identify the maintaining mechanism, and use behavioural/CBT-style exposure and flexibility-building where appropriate. Avoid presenting one named psychotherapy as universally established first-line in all adults. | Current ARFID clinical literature |

## Q63. Medically compromised anorexia / eating-disorder emergency

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current emergency framework | UPDATE | **MEED (2022) supersedes MARSIPAN/Junior MARSIPAN** as current Royal College of Psychiatrists emergency guidance. It uses a multi-domain traffic-light risk formulation rather than one BMI or one vital sign deciding safety. | RCPsych CR233 MEED |
| Red flags | KEEP | Retain **marked bradycardia, hypotension/postural instability, hypothermia, dehydration, syncope, ECG abnormality, electrolyte disturbance and hypoglycaemia** as high-risk findings. Also assess rapid weight loss, very low intake, weakness, infection, renal/hepatic dysfunction and inability to maintain hydration/nutrition. | MEED |
| Useful adult thresholds | UPDATE | A few thresholds remain useful as “red” examples, not stand-alone admission criteria: MEED flags **BMI <13 kg/m²**, **waking pulse <40/min**, **temperature <35.5°C** and **glucose <3.0 mmol/L** among high-risk adult findings. Oxford’s fixed **QTc >470 ms** should not be retained as a universal single threshold because QT interpretation is sex/context/drug/electrolyte dependent. | MEED summary/risk tool |
| Admission/escalation | KEEP | Urgent medical admission/escalation is warranted when there is physiological instability, serious biochemical/ECG abnormality, severe dehydration/hypoglycaemia, syncope, inability to take adequate nutrition safely, serious refeeding risk or other organ compromise. Psychiatric risk and inability to engage safely also matter. | MEED; NICE NG69 |
| Refeeding syndrome | KEEP | Refeeding can cause dangerous **phosphate, potassium and magnesium shifts, fluid imbalance, thiamine deficiency, arrhythmia, neurological deterioration and organ failure**. High-risk patients need planned refeeding in a setting capable of frequent clinical/biochemical monitoring and replacement. | MEED |
| Thiamine / electrolytes / glucose | UPDATE | Give **thiamine and appropriate multivitamin supplementation before/with refeeding in high-risk malnourished patients** and monitor/replete phosphate, potassium and magnesium closely; monitor glucose and fluid status. Do not teach blanket prophylactic phosphate for every patient as an immutable rule—replacement/prophylaxis should follow risk and local protocol. | MEED |
| Refeeding rate | UPDATE | Drop the old assumption that all severely malnourished patients should be started on extremely low calories for prolonged periods. Current MEED-era practice supports **risk-stratified, more assertive but closely monitored refeeding**, escalating promptly when biochemical/clinical monitoring is stable. Very high-risk patients may still need a cautious start, but prolonged underfeeding is itself harmful. | MEED; contemporary refeeding evidence |
| Single-number safety rules | DROP | Do not reduce medical risk to BMI alone or to Oxford’s old “BMI <13/HR <40/QTc >470 = admission” checklist. MEED explicitly combines nutrition, rate of loss, cardiovascular status, temperature, hydration, biochemistry, ECG, muscle strength, infection and psychiatric risk. | MEED |

## Q64. Bulimia nervosa

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | KEEP | BN remains recurrent binge eating with loss of control plus recurrent compensatory behaviours and overvaluation/undue influence of weight and shape. Distinguish it from **AN binge-eating/purging presentation**: if the person is significantly underweight and meets AN architecture, diagnose AN rather than BN. Do not use a rigid BMI 17.5 boundary. | ICD-11/DSM-5-TR |
| Psychological treatment | UPDATE | Current NICE sequencing begins with bulimia-focused guided self-help for many adults and moves to **individual CBT-ED** if unacceptable/ineffective; CBT-based eating-disorder treatment remains the core specialist psychotherapy. | NICE NG69 §1.5 |
| Fluoxetine | KEEP | **Fluoxetine 60 mg/day remains a legitimate prescribing/board fact for adults with bulimia nervosa** in current UK SmPCs, indicated as an adjunct to psychotherapy to reduce bingeing/purging. Medication should not be the sole treatment, and evidence for long-term efficacy is limited. | Current fluoxetine SmPC; NICE NG69 |
| Purging safety | KEEP | Recurrent vomiting/laxative or diuretic misuse can cause **hypokalaemia and other electrolyte disturbances, dehydration and arrhythmia/QT risk**. Physical assessment, electrolytes and ECG are indicated when purging is significant or symptoms/risk factors suggest cardiovascular compromise. | NICE NG69; prescribing/medical practice |

## Q65. Chronic insomnia / Q65a. Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBT-I | KEEP | **CBT-I is the preferred first-line long-term treatment for chronic insomnia**, using components such as stimulus control, sleep restriction/compression, cognitive work and relapse prevention. | AASM behavioural guideline; NICE TA922 context |
| Sleep hygiene | UPDATE | Sleep hygiene is supportive but **not an adequate stand-alone evidence-based treatment for chronic insomnia**. “Give sleep hygiene advice” is insufficient as the core answer. | AASM behavioural guideline |
| Hypnotics | KEEP | Hypnotic medication may be appropriate short term or in selected patients when CBT-I is unavailable, unsuitable, declined or insufficient, but chronic benzodiazepine/Z-drug prescribing should not be presented as routine default care. | NICE insomnia guidance/TA922; AASM |
| Daridorexant | ADD | **Daridorexant (Quviviq) is EU-authorised** for adults with insomnia symptoms present for at least 3 months with considerable daytime impairment. NICE recommends it when CBT-I has been tried without sufficient benefit or is unavailable/unsuitable, with treatment reviewed within 3 months and kept as short as possible. It is therefore a genuine current European option, not a US-only novelty. | EMA Quviviq EPAR; NICE TA922 |
| Orexin-antagonist caveat | ADD | Daridorexant is a dual orexin-receptor antagonist and is **contraindicated in narcolepsy**; strong CYP3A4 inhibitors are also a key prescribing restriction. No need to catalogue every DORA in the board answer. | EMA Quviviq EPAR |
| DSWPD distinction | KEEP | Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is a **circadian timing disorder**: sleep onset and waking are substantially delayed relative to required times, but sleep quality/duration are usually normal or near-normal when the person can follow the preferred delayed schedule. This distinguishes it from primary insomnia. | ICSD/AASM circadian framework |
| DSWPD treatment | UPDATE | Management centres on a stable schedule and **strategically timed melatonin**, often combined clinically with appropriately timed morning bright light and reduction of evening light. Timing is critical; “take melatonin at bedtime” is too crude. Evidence for adult light therapy is less robust than for melatonin, so avoid presenting one rigid protocol as universal. | AASM circadian guideline |

## Q66. Excessive daytime sleepiness / narcolepsy / Q66a. REM sleep behaviour disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| EDS differential | KEEP | Before diagnosing narcolepsy, exclude **insufficient sleep, obstructive sleep apnoea, circadian disorder, sedating medication/substances, depression, medical/neurological disease and other hypersomnolence disorders**. Sleep history and adequate prior sleep are essential before interpreting MSLT. | ICSD/AASM sleep-medicine framework |
| Narcolepsy type 1 | UPDATE | Narcolepsy type 1 is defined by chronic excessive sleepiness with **cataplexy plus characteristic PSG/MSLT findings and/or demonstrably deficient CSF orexin/hypocretin**. Cataplexy is emotion-triggered loss of muscle tone with preserved consciousness and is the highest-yield bedside discriminator. | ICSD-3-TR/AASM |
| Narcolepsy type 2 | ADD | Narcolepsy type 2 has persistent excessive sleepiness with characteristic MSLT/PSG findings **without cataplexy**, and CSF orexin is not low if measured. Secondary explanations and insufficient sleep must be excluded carefully. | ICSD-3-TR/AASM |
| PSG/MSLT | KEEP | Standard evaluation uses **overnight polysomnography followed by a properly performed MSLT** to document pathological sleepiness and sleep-onset REM periods, after ensuring adequate sleep and controlling confounders. The exact CSF hypocretin numerical threshold is not necessary for a short board answer unless specifically asked. | ICSD/AASM |
| Treatment | UPDATE | Current high-level options for daytime sleepiness include **modafinil**, pitolisant, solriamfetol and oxybate-based treatments depending on jurisdiction/phenotype; cataplexy may improve with oxybate, pitolisant and selected REM-suppressing antidepressants. The board answer should emphasise specialist sleep care, regular sleep scheduling and driving/occupational safety rather than a drug catalogue. | AASM treatment guideline; EMA product information where applicable |
| RBD diagnosis | KEEP | REM sleep behaviour disorder is **dream-enactment behaviour associated with REM sleep without normal muscle atonia**, confirmed with PSG when diagnostic certainty is needed and distinguished from nocturnal seizures, OSA-related behaviours and other parasomnias. | AASM RBD guideline |
| RBD safety | ADD | **Injury prevention is a first-line intervention**: remove dangerous bedside objects, pad sharp edges, consider floor protection and separate sleeping arrangements temporarily when behaviours pose serious partner/patient risk. | AASM RBD guideline 2023 |
| Synucleinopathy association | KEEP | Isolated RBD is strongly associated with later **alpha-synucleinopathies** such as Parkinson disease, DLB and MSA. This is an important follow-up point, but no exact conversion percentage is needed. | AASM/neurology consensus |
| RBD medication | UPDATE | **Immediate-release melatonin and clonazepam remain guideline-supported options**, but AASM recommendations are conditional and choice should consider age, falls, cognition, sleep apnoea and adverse effects. | AASM RBD guideline 2023 |
| Neuroprotection | DROP | There is **no established neuroprotective treatment proven to prevent synucleinopathy conversion** in isolated RBD. Do not imply that clonazepam/melatonin modifies neurodegenerative risk. | AASM/neurology guidance |

## Q67. Sleep stages / Q67a. Depression sleep architecture

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | KEEP | N1 is light NREM sleep with attenuation of alpha and predominance of **low-voltage mixed-frequency/theta activity**. | Standard sleep staging |
| N2 | KEEP | N2 is characterised by **sleep spindles and K-complexes**. | Standard sleep staging |
| N3 | KEEP | N3 is slow-wave sleep characterised by high-amplitude **delta/slow-wave activity**. | Standard sleep staging |
| REM | KEEP | REM shows low-voltage mixed-frequency EEG, rapid eye movements and **skeletal-muscle atonia** apart from expected exceptions. | Standard sleep staging |
| Depression architecture | KEEP | Classic research findings in major depression include **shortened REM latency, increased REM density and reduced slow-wave sleep**, but none is diagnostically specific and they are not required for clinical diagnosis. Drop Oxford’s exact “REM latency <60 min” as a universal diagnostic fact. | Sleep/depression literature |

## Q68. Personality disorder classification and formulation

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 architecture | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Current **ICD-11 replaced the old named categorical PD system with a primarily dimensional model**. First determine whether personality disorder is present, then rate **severity** and describe the characteristic trait domains. DSM-5-TR Section II still retains traditional categorical diagnoses, so the systems must be named rather than blended. | ICD-11 CDDR; WHO/ICD-11 PD literature |
| Severity | ADD | ICD-11 severity is **mild, moderate or severe personality disorder** (with a subthreshold Personality Difficulty code available). Severity reflects dysfunction in self/interpersonal functioning, pervasiveness, consequences and risk—not symptom counting. | ICD-11 PD framework |
| Trait domains | ADD | The five ICD-11 trait-domain qualifiers are **Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition and Anankastia**. Multiple domains may be applied; they describe style/phenotype rather than creating five new categorical diagnoses. | ICD-11 PD framework |
| Borderline pattern | ADD | ICD-11 includes an optional **Borderline Pattern qualifier** in addition to severity/trait specification, largely to preserve clinical continuity with the established borderline construct and its treatment evidence. | ICD-11 PD framework |
| Cluster model | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | DSM’s Cluster A/B/C vocabulary remains useful for DSM-based exams/communication but is **not the current ICD-11 architecture**. Do not describe ICD-11 as simply “DSM clusters with new names.” | ICD-11/DSM comparison |
| Formulation | KEEP | A competent answer should still formulate developmental history, attachment/relationships, trauma/adversity where relevant, temperament, coping, current interpersonal patterns, strengths, comorbidity and risk. Dimensional diagnosis should improve—not replace—individual formulation. | Current PD practice |

### Q68a. Avoidant personality pattern versus social anxiety disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distinction | UPDATE | The useful clinical distinction remains that an avoidant personality pattern is **pervasive across self-concept, relationships and interpersonal functioning**, whereas social anxiety disorder is an anxiety syndrome centred on feared scrutiny/negative evaluation. They commonly overlap and the distinction is not simply “one is more severe.” | DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 trait formulation; social-anxiety guidance |
| ICD-11 naming | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **Avoidant personality disorder is not a separate named ICD-11 PD category**. In ICD-11, a similar phenotype is described through PD severity plus prominent **Negative Affectivity and Detachment**. The named avoidant diagnosis remains DSM terminology. | ICD-11 PD framework |

### Q68b. OCPD/anankastic personality versus OCD

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distinction | KEEP | OCPD/anankastic personality is a pervasive pattern of perfectionism, rigidity, order/control and overconscientiousness; OCD requires **obsessions and/or compulsions**. “Ego-syntonic versus ego-dystonic” is a useful tendency, not an absolute discriminator—some people with OCD have poor insight and some with anankastic traits experience distress about their behaviour. | DSM-5-TR; ICD-11 OCRD/PD frameworks |
| ICD-11 naming | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | ICD-11 no longer has a separate **anankastic personality disorder/OCPD category**; the corresponding dimensional qualifier is **Anankastia**. OCD remains a distinct obsessive-compulsive and related disorder. | ICD-11 PD/OCRD framework |

## Q69. Borderline personality disorder / Q69a. BPD versus bipolar disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary treatment | KEEP | **Structured specialist psychotherapy is the primary treatment**, delivered within a coherent treatment framework with supervision and clear crisis planning. Evidence-based models include DBT, MBT and other structured therapies; no single model is mandatory for every patient. | NICE CG78/QS88; contemporary PD guidance |
| DBT/MBT | KEEP | NICE specifically supports comprehensive DBT when recurrent self-harm is a priority (its original wording referred to women), while evidence also supports MBT and other specialist programmes. The board point is structured evidence-based psychotherapy, not brand allegiance. | NICE CG78 |
| Medication for core BPD | UPDATE | **Do not prescribe medication as treatment for BPD itself or its core instability/self-harm symptoms.** Antipsychotics should not be used medium/long term for BPD. Treat clearly diagnosed comorbid disorders and review existing polypharmacy with an aim to stop ineffective/unnecessary drugs. | NICE CG78/QS88 |
| Crisis prescribing | KEEP | Short-term medication may occasionally be used for a clearly defined crisis target, but it should not replace psychological/crisis interventions; use one drug where possible, minimum effective dose, overdose/misuse safety and a clear stop/review plan. NICE’s crisis sedative recommendation is generally **≤1 week**. | NICE CG78 |
| BPD versus bipolar | UPDATE | BPD affective instability is typically **rapidly reactive to interpersonal/environmental events within a chronic pattern of identity/interpersonal dysregulation**, whereas bipolar disorder requires distinct sustained manic/hypomanic/depressive episodes representing a clear departure from baseline. Duration helps but is not sufficient alone: assess decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, increased goal-directed activity, episodicity, family history and inter-episode baseline. | Current bipolar/PD frameworks |
| Comorbidity | ADD | BPD and bipolar disorder can coexist. Do not force an either/or diagnosis merely because emotional reactivity is present; establish independently whether full bipolar episodes have occurred. | Current diagnostic guidance |

## Q70. Antisocial personality disorder versus psychopathy

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic status | KEEP | **ASPD is a formal DSM diagnosis** (and antisocial/dissocial phenotypes can be represented in ICD-11 dimensional PD). **Psychopathy is not an independent DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 diagnosis**; it is a personality/forensic construct. | DSM-5-TR; ICD-11 PD framework |
| Phenotype | KEEP | Psychopathy emphasises interpersonal/affective features such as superficial charm, manipulativeness, callousness, shallow affect and lack of empathy/remorse in addition to antisocial lifestyle/behaviour. ASPD criteria are more behaviourally weighted. | Forensic psychiatry literature |
| PCL-R | KEEP | The Hare **PCL-R** is a specialist forensic assessment instrument for psychopathic traits and risk-relevant formulation, not a routine general-psychiatry diagnostic test and not synonymous with ASPD. | Forensic assessment literature |
| Percent overlap | DROP | Drop Oxford’s fixed “25–30% of ASPD meet psychopathy” figure from the board answer; prevalence depends on setting, instrument threshold and population and does not improve the core distinction. | Current forensic literature |

## Q72. Paraphilic disorders and risk

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atypical interest versus disorder | UPDATE | ICD-11 deliberately distinguishes **atypical sexual interests from paraphilic disorders**. An unusual consensual interest is not a mental disorder merely because it is culturally uncommon. | ICD-11 sexual-disorders framework |
| Non-consenting focus | KEEP | ICD-11 paraphilic disorders principally prioritise sustained/intense arousal patterns focused on individuals who are **unwilling or unable to consent**, when the person has acted on the pattern or is markedly distressed by it. Offending behaviour alone does not prove an underlying paraphilic disorder; mania, intoxication, dementia and opportunistic offending remain differentials. | ICD-11 paraphilic-disorder framework |
| Consenting/solitary patterns | UPDATE | Atypical arousal involving consenting adults or solitary behaviour is diagnosable only when there is **marked distress about the arousal pattern that is not simply due to rejection/fear of rejection**, or the behaviour itself carries significant direct risk of injury/death. Social stigma alone must not be pathologised. | ICD-11 paraphilic-disorder framework |
| Risk assessment | KEEP | Assess consent capacity/status of potential victims, fantasies/urges/behaviour, access to potential victims, prior sexual/non-sexual offending, escalation, disinhibiting substances, comorbid mental disorder, self-control, protective factors and willingness to engage. Safeguarding/public-protection duties follow the actual jurisdiction and risk. | Forensic-sexology practice |
| Psychotherapy | KEEP | Specialist psychotherapy and relapse/risk-management work are central, tailored to offence risk, cognitive/behavioural mechanisms, intimacy, self-regulation and comorbidity. | WFSBP/forensic guidance |
| SSRIs | KEEP | SSRIs may be useful in selected lower-to-moderate-risk presentations, particularly with intrusive/compulsive sexual preoccupation or comorbid depression/OCD-spectrum symptoms; evidence is less robust than for mainstream depressive/OCD indications. | WFSBP 2020 |
| Antiandrogens/GnRH | KEEP | **Antiandrogenic treatment or GnRH analogues are specialist interventions for selected higher-risk/severe cases**, requiring informed consent/legal framework as applicable plus endocrine, metabolic, bone and cardiovascular monitoring. They should never be presented as routine treatment for consensual atypical sexuality. | WFSBP 2020 |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

1. **Q59 ADHD onset:** Oxford’s ICD-10 “before age 7” versus current DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 **symptoms before 12**. Childhood diagnosis/documentation is not required, but developmental symptoms are.
2. **Q61 intellectual disability:** old IQ-band severity versus current diagnosis/severity incorporating **adaptive functioning and support needs**.
3. **Q62 anorexia:** old fixed BMI <17.5/amenorrhoea framing versus current AN architecture without either as an obligatory universal criterion.
4. **Q63 medical AN:** MARSIPAN/very-low-calorie historical practice versus **MEED** multi-domain risk assessment and monitored, risk-stratified refeeding that avoids prolonged underfeeding.
5. **Q64 bulimia:** Oxford’s fluoxetine **60 mg/day** survives current prescribing verification, but only as an adjunct to psychotherapy rather than a stand-alone treatment.
6. **Q65 insomnia:** older “CBT-I then Z-drug” simplicity versus current European availability of **daridorexant**, while CBT-I remains standard first-line long-term care.
7. **Q68 personality disorders:** old categorical ICD-10/cluster-style model versus ICD-11 **severity + five trait domains**, with optional borderline pattern qualifier.
8. **Q68a/Q68b:** named Avoidant PD and OCPD remain DSM/legacy constructs; ICD-11 represents these patterns dimensionally (e.g. Negative Affectivity/Detachment and Anankastia).
9. **Q69 BPD:** medication-based symptom suppression/polypharmacy versus current guidance that **structured psychotherapy is primary and medication is not treatment for core BPD**.
10. **Q71 sexual dysfunction:** ICD-10 “non-organic sexual dysfunction” mental-disorder framing versus ICD-11 placement under **sexual health** and a biopsychosocial approach.
11. **Q72 paraphilias:** atypical consensual sexual interests are **not disorders merely because unusual**; ICD-11 focuses diagnosis on non-consent, non-stigma distress or substantial direct harm risk.
12. **Q73 gender:** ICD-10 mental-disorder categories versus ICD-11 **gender incongruence under sexual health**, explicitly non-pathologising gender diversity.

## Unresolved issues

1. **Greek gender-service pathway:** not verified and not required for the core learner-facing answer. Do not infer a national referral, reimbursement or legal pathway from WHO/WPATH guidance.
2. **ARFID psychotherapy hierarchy in adults:** evidence is developing and less mature than AN/BN. The packet intentionally avoids declaring one branded therapy universally first-line.
3. **Exact high-risk refeeding calorie prescription:** MEED supports risk-stratified monitored feeding and avoidance of prolonged underfeeding, but exact starting calories and electrolyte prophylaxis vary with medical risk and local acute-care protocol. The learner-facing answer should retain principles and a few red-risk thresholds rather than a full prescription.
4. **Adult DSWPD bright-light protocol:** timed melatonin has stronger formal guideline support; exact adult light intensity/timing schedules should not be frozen as a universal board rule.
5. No unresolved issue blocks creation of Q59–Q73 answer briefs.
