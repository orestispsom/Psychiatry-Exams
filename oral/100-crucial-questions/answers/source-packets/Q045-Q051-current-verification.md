# Q45–Q51 Current Verification Delta

Scope: targeted current verification only for the update-sensitive claims identified in `Q045-Q051-oxford-map.md`. This is not a reconstruction of the questions and not a learner-facing model answer.

## Q45. Delirium

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic architecture | KEEP | Retain the core syndrome: **acute/subacute onset over hours to days, fluctuating course, impaired attention with disturbance of awareness/other cognition**, attributable to an underlying medical/substance/medication cause or multiple causes. This remains compatible with current DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 framing. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; DSM-5-TR; NICE CG103 |
| Hypoactive delirium | KEEP | Hypoactive delirium is common, under-recognised and may present with reduced movement, slowed responses, withdrawal, lethargy or somnolence rather than agitation. It must not be mistaken for depression, fatigue or “quiet dementia.” | NICE CG103 §§1.3–1.5 |
| 4AT / CAM | UPDATE | **4AT now deserves a board fact.** NICE 2023 recommends the **4AT** when delirium indicators are present in general hospital/long-term-care settings. In critical care or post-anaesthetic recovery, use **CAM-ICU or ICDSC** instead. CAM remains valid historically/elsewhere, but current NICE no longer makes standard CAM the preferred general-setting tool. | NICE CG103 §1.6.1 (2023) |
| Non-pharmacological management | KEEP | Treat the cause(s), correct dehydration/hypoxia/metabolic disturbance, review medications, treat pain/constipation/urinary retention/infection, restore sleep–wake cues, reorient, provide glasses/hearing aids, reduce unnecessary moves/restraints, maintain mobilisation/nutrition, and involve familiar carers where useful. These measures are the treatment backbone. | NICE CG103 prevention/treatment sections |
| Role of antipsychotics | UPDATE | Antipsychotics do **not** treat the underlying delirium or shorten its cause-driven course reliably. Consider only for **severe distress or risk to self/others when de-escalation/non-drug measures are ineffective or inappropriate**. | NICE CG103; NICE QS63 |
| Haloperidol | UPDATE | If medication is required, NICE allows **short-term haloperidol, usually ≤1 week**, starting at the lowest appropriate dose and titrating cautiously. In frail older adults, MHRA advises baseline ECG/electrolyte correction where feasible, repeat cardiac/electrolyte monitoring as indicated, and close monitoring for EPS/dysphagia. **Parkinson disease and DLB are contraindications** in current UK product/safety guidance. | NICE CG103/QS63; MHRA Drug Safety Update 2021 |
| “Sundowning” as defining feature | DROP | Evening worsening can occur but is not required and should not be treated as a defining diagnostic criterion. The defining temporal feature is **acute onset with fluctuation**. | NICE CG103; DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 |

### Q45 adjudication note
Oxford’s phenomenology remains strong. The main current additions are the **4AT recommendation** and stricter framing of antipsychotics as short-term symptom-control only, with explicit haloperidol contraindication in PD/DLB.

## Q46. Assessment of suspected major neurocognitive disorder / dementia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terminology | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **“Major neurocognitive disorder” is DSM terminology.** ICD-11 retains **neurocognitive disorders** as the grouping and uses dementia diagnoses by aetiology; “dementia” remains clinically valid terminology. Do not imply that “dementia” has been abolished universally. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; DSM-5-TR |
| Core assessment | KEEP | History from the patient plus **collateral**, trajectory, behavioural/psychiatric symptoms, medication/anticholinergic burden, alcohol/substances, neurological/medical history, **ADLs/IADLs**, driving/safety, and assessment across cognitive domains remain core. Functional decline is essential for distinguishing dementia/major NCD from milder cognitive impairment. | NICE NG97 §§1.2.1–1.2.11 |
| Cognitive screening | KEEP | Use a validated brief cognitive instrument but **do not exclude dementia because a screening score is normal**. Neuropsychological testing is useful when impairment, aetiology or subtype remains unclear. | NICE NG97 §§1.2.3–1.2.11 |
| Baseline medical work-up | KEEP | Physical/neurological examination plus targeted blood/urine tests for reversible/contributing causes remain standard. Avoid presenting one rigid universal blood panel as mandatory in every patient; investigations are guided by presentation and local practice. | NICE NG97 §1.2.2 |
| Structural imaging | UPDATE | **Structural imaging is generally offered** to exclude reversible causes and assist subtype diagnosis unless dementia is well established and subtype clear. MRI is preferred when vascular disease/subtype detail matters; CT is acceptable when MRI is unavailable/contraindicated. Imaging supports, but does not itself make, the clinical diagnosis. | NICE NG97 §§1.2.13, 1.2.26 |
| CSF Alzheimer biomarkers | KEEP | In specialist assessment when AD remains uncertain and subtype clarification will change management, CSF amyloid/tau biomarkers remain established. NICE specifies CSF Aβ42 (or Aβ42/40) with total tau ± p-tau181 as an option alongside FDG-PET/SPECT. | NICE NG97 §§1.2.14–1.2.19 |
| Blood/plasma biomarkers | ADD | **Plasma biomarkers have moved into current specialist practice**, especially p-tau-based assays. The 2025 Alzheimer’s Association guideline supports sufficiently accurate blood biomarker tests as triage tools and, for validated assays meeting higher accuracy thresholds, potentially as confirmatory substitutes for CSF/PET **within specialised memory care**. They should **not be ordered before comprehensive clinical assessment** and have not replaced structural imaging or specialist interpretation universally. | Alzheimer’s Association BBM CPG 2025 |
| Biomarkers and anti-amyloid treatment | ADD | Because current anti-amyloid therapies require **confirmed amyloid pathology**, biomarker confirmation now has direct treatment implications in selected early-AD patients. This elevates the importance of specialist amyloid testing beyond purely academic subtype classification. | EMA Leqembi/Kisunla EPARs |
| Capacity/driving/safeguarding | KEEP | Address decision-making capacity, medication/financial safety, driving and safeguarding according to individual functional risk and jurisdiction. No new universal numerical threshold replaces clinical/legal assessment. | NICE NG97; local law/regulation as applicable |

## Q47. Alzheimer disease

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical diagnosis | KEEP | AD remains a **clinical syndrome plus aetiological diagnosis**: typically insidious, progressive cognitive decline, often initially amnestic but with recognised non-amnestic variants. Exclude delirium and alternative neurological/psychiatric causes and use collateral/functional history and cognitive testing. | NICE NG97; current AD criteria frameworks |
| Amyloid/tau biomarkers | UPDATE | Amyloid PET/CSF AD biomarkers—and now selected validated plasma assays in specialist settings—can increase diagnostic certainty. Biomarkers are especially consequential when the phenotype is atypical/uncertain or when considering disease-modifying anti-amyloid therapy. Do not present biomarker positivity alone as equivalent to the whole clinical diagnosis in a symptomatic patient. | NICE NG97; Alzheimer’s Association BBM CPG 2025 |
| Symptomatic treatment | KEEP | **Donepezil, rivastigmine or galantamine** remain standard options for mild–moderate AD. **Memantine** is an option in moderate AD when AChE inhibitors are contraindicated/not tolerated and in severe AD; it can be added to an AChE inhibitor in moderate/severe disease according to NICE. | NICE NG97 §§1.5.2–1.5.6 |
| Lecanemab EU status | ADD | **Leqembi (lecanemab) is EU-authorised** (marketing authorisation 15 Apr 2025) for **early AD: MCI or mild dementia due to AD**, with **confirmed amyloid pathology**, restricted to **APOE ε4 non-carriers or heterozygotes**. It requires specialist initiation, MRI access and ARIA risk monitoring. | EMA Leqembi EPAR, updated Jun 2026 |
| Donanemab EU status | ADD | **Kisunla (donanemab) is EU-authorised** (24 Sep 2025) for **early symptomatic AD (MCI or mild dementia)** with confirmed amyloid pathology, again restricted to **APOE ε4 non-carriers or heterozygotes**. MRI monitoring for ARIA is mandatory; EU treatment duration is limited in the product information. | EMA Kisunla EPAR, updated Feb 2026 |
| ARIA | ADD | For both anti-amyloid antibodies, **ARIA-E/ARIA-H (oedema and haemorrhagic imaging abnormalities)** is the defining safety issue. Board-level teaching should include that risk is higher in APOE ε4 homozygotes, explaining the narrower EU indication, and that MRI-based screening/monitoring is required. Do not turn Q47 into an infusion/MRI schedule memorisation exercise. | EMA Leqembi/Kisunla EPARs |
| “Disease modifying” | UPDATE | These agents **slow clinical decline modestly in selected early AD; they do not reverse established dementia or restore lost function**. They apply only to carefully selected biomarker-confirmed early disease, not moderate/severe AD. | EMA EPARs |
| Greek access | UNRESOLVED | EMA authorisation is confirmed, but **routine Greek availability, reimbursement and implementation pathway were not verified** from authoritative Greek sources in this pass. Do not equate EU authorisation with routine Greek prescribing access. | EMA + Greek EOF/MoH search |

## Q48. DLB versus Parkinson disease dementia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| DLB core clinical features | UPDATE | Current DLB Consortium criteria have **four core clinical features**: fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, **REM sleep behaviour disorder**, and spontaneous parkinsonism. Oxford’s older “core triad + supportive RBD” framing must be updated: **RBD is now core**. | McKeith et al., 4th DLB Consortium report 2017 |
| RBD | ADD | RBD may precede cognitive decline by years and should be actively sought from the patient/bed partner. PSG confirmation of REM sleep without atonia is an **indicative biomarker**, not required in every clinically clear case. | McKeith et al. 2017 |
| Probable/possible DLB | ADD | Probable DLB can be diagnosed with **≥2 core clinical features**, or **1 core feature + ≥1 indicative biomarker**. Possible DLB is 1 core feature without indicative biomarker, or an indicative biomarker without core features. Biomarkers alone do not establish probable DLB. | McKeith et al. 2017 |
| DAT imaging | KEEP | Reduced basal-ganglia dopamine-transporter uptake on SPECT/PET remains an **indicative biomarker** and is useful when parkinsonism/DLB diagnosis is uncertain. NICE recommends FP-CIT SPECT when DLB is suspected and diagnosis remains uncertain. | McKeith 2017; NICE NG97 §1.2.20 |
| Antipsychotic sensitivity | KEEP | Severe sensitivity to dopamine-blocking antipsychotics remains a major supportive feature and safety warning. Antipsychotics can markedly worsen parkinsonism and cause severe reactions in DLB/PDD. | McKeith 2017; NICE NG97 §1.7.4 |
| 1-year rule | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | The **1-year rule remains recommended operationally for research/classification**: DLB when dementia occurs before/concurrently with or within 1 year of parkinsonism; PDD when dementia develops in established PD after that interval. The DLB Consortium explicitly notes that in ordinary clinical practice the distinction is pragmatic and both belong to a Lewy-body-disease spectrum; the rule is operational rather than mechanistically validated. | McKeith et al. 2017; later criteria review |
| Cholinesterase inhibitors | KEEP | Donepezil or rivastigmine are recommended for mild–moderate DLB; memantine is considered when AChE inhibitors are not tolerated/contraindicated. PDD similarly has guideline support for cholinesterase inhibition, particularly rivastigmine. | NICE NG97 §§1.5.10–1.5.13; NICE NG71 |

## Q48a. Visual hallucinations in Parkinson disease

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| First steps | KEEP | First assess for **delirium, infection, medication toxicity, metabolic illness, sensory/visual impairment and other triggers**. If hallucinations are well tolerated and not distressing/risky, they need not automatically be medicated. | NICE NG71 §§1.5.12–1.5.15 |
| Rationalise PD medication | KEEP | Review and, with Parkinson-specialist input, **reduce provoking antiparkinsonian medication where feasible**, balancing psychosis against motor worsening and withdrawal risk. | NICE NG71 §1.5.15 |
| Quetiapine | KEEP | NICE says **consider quetiapine** for hallucinations/delusions in PD without cognitive impairment. Evidence is weaker than for clozapine and use is off-label in this indication in the UK, but it remains commonly used because motor worsening is limited relative to potent D2 blockers. | NICE NG71 §1.5.16 |
| Clozapine | KEEP | If standard treatment is ineffective, **clozapine is the evidence-based specialist option**, with mandatory haematological monitoring and substantially lower doses than schizophrenia treatment. | NICE NG71 §§1.5.17–1.5.18 |
| Pimavanserin | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Pimavanserin is an established **US-approved** Parkinson-disease-psychosis treatment, but **no current centrally authorised EU product/EPAR for Parkinson psychosis was identified**, and no Greek authorisation/availability was verified. It should therefore **not** be taught as routine European/Greek standard care. Mention only as an international/US option if useful. | EMA medicines/Union Register search Aug 2026; NICE NG71 does not include it |
| Avoid potent D2 blockade | KEEP | Olanzapine should not be used for PD psychosis per NICE, and phenothiazines/butyrophenones and other substantial D2 blockers can worsen motor symptoms. | NICE NG71 §§1.5.19–1.5.20 |

## Q48b. Dopamine-agonist–related impulse-control disorders

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | KEEP | Retain **pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping/spending and binge eating** as the classic ICD spectrum. Punding and dopamine dysregulation syndrome are useful examiner follow-ups but are not required to define an ICD. | NICE NG71 §§1.4.1–1.4.5 |
| Risk factor | KEEP | Dopamine agonists are the **major medication risk factor**, although ICDs can occur with other dopaminergic therapies. | NICE NG71 |
| Screening/collateral | UPDATE | Ask proactively at initiation and follow-up and involve family/carers where appropriate because behaviours may be concealed or minimised. NICE explicitly recommends discussing ICD risk and reviewing it regularly. | NICE NG71 §§1.3.8, 1.4.3–1.4.4 |
| Management | KEEP | First-line management is specialist-guided modification of dopaminergic therapy, **gradually reducing the dopamine agonist first** where clinically possible. | NICE NG71 §1.4.8 |
| DAWS | ADD | **Dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (DAWS)** is a clinically important complication of reduction/cessation and is a reason to taper gradually and monitor. Symptoms can be severe and non-motor; do not freeze one rigid taper schedule because reduction must be individualised to motor control and withdrawal symptoms. | NICE NG71 §1.4.8; specialist PD literature |
| CBT | ADD | If medication modification is insufficient, NICE recommends **specialist CBT targeted at impulse-control disorders**. | NICE NG71 §1.4.9 |

## Q49. Frontotemporal dementia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| bvFTD architecture | KEEP | Retain the International bvFTD phenotype: early **behavioural disinhibition, apathy/inertia, loss of empathy, perseverative/compulsive/stereotyped behaviour, hyperorality/dietary change**, plus executive dysfunction. | International FTD Consortium criteria; NICE NG97 §1.2.12 |
| Early memory preservation | UPDATE | Relative preservation of episodic memory and visuospatial function is a **common early pattern**, not an absolute rule. Significant episodic-memory impairment can occur in pathologically confirmed bvFTD. Do not exclude bvFTD solely because memory is impaired. | International bvFTD criteria and subsequent validation literature |
| Imaging | KEEP | Structural MRI supports the diagnosis and may show frontal/anterior temporal atrophy. When diagnosis is uncertain, NICE recommends **FDG-PET or perfusion SPECT**; normal imaging does not by itself rule out FTD. | NICE NG97 §§1.2.23–1.2.24 |
| AChE inhibitors / memantine | DROP | **Do not offer cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine for FTD** as routine cognitive treatment. NICE explicitly recommends against both. Oxford wording suggesting possible anti-dementia-drug use should be removed if present. | NICE NG97 §1.5.15 |
| Symptomatic medication | UPDATE | No drug is disease-modifying or reliably cognitive-enhancing in FTD. **SSRIs or trazodone may be used symptomatically** for disinhibition, compulsive behaviour, irritability or hyperorality where clinically appropriate, but evidence is limited and treatment is targeted to behaviour rather than “treating FTD.” | FTD consensus/review evidence; NICE does not endorse a specific agent |

## Q50. Vascular cognitive impairment / vascular dementia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terminology | UPDATE | **Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI)** is the broader modern construct, spanning mild vascular cognitive impairment through vascular dementia/major VCI. “Vascular dementia” remains valid for the dementia-level syndrome but should not be treated as the whole spectrum. | AHA/ASA VCI statement; JACC VCI expert panel |
| Course | UPDATE | **Stepwise decline is classic but not required.** VCI may be abrupt/post-stroke, stepwise, gradually progressive (especially small-vessel/subcortical disease), or mixed. A rigid stepwise-course requirement is outdated. | JACC VCI panel; AHA/ASA VCI statement |
| Cognitive/neurological pattern | KEEP | Executive dysfunction, reduced processing speed, attention deficits, gait/focal neurological signs and imaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease are useful clues, particularly in subcortical disease. Memory may be relatively less impaired than in typical AD but no single cognitive profile is diagnostic. | AHA/ASA; NICE NG97 |
| Imaging | KEEP | MRI is preferred when vascular dementia/VCI is suspected and subtype remains uncertain; CT is used when MRI is unavailable/contraindicated. Imaging burden must be clinically linked to the cognitive syndrome rather than treated as incidental proof. | NICE NG97 §1.2.26 |
| Mixed pathology | UPDATE | **AD + cerebrovascular pathology is common** and should not be forced into a false either/or diagnosis. Vascular disease can lower the threshold for expression of cognitive impairment in coexisting neurodegeneration. | AHA/ASA VCI statement; VASCOG/JACC frameworks |
| Treatment | KEEP | Management is primarily **vascular risk reduction and secondary stroke prevention**, smoking/exercise/diabetes/BP/lipid/AF management as indicated, rehabilitation and functional support. | AHA/ASA; contemporary VCI guidance |
| Cholinesterase inhibitors / memantine | UPDATE | For a clean UK/NICE board answer: **do not routinely offer AChE inhibitors or memantine for pure vascular dementia**; NICE considers them only when comorbid AD, PDD or DLB is suspected. Other guidelines find modest cognitive effects in selected VaD, so this is a genuine guideline difference; NICE’s simpler position is preferable for a European viva. | NICE NG97 §1.5.14; AHA/other VCI guidance |

## Q51. Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search for reversible drivers first | UPDATE | Before treating “BPSD,” perform a structured assessment for **delirium, pain, infection, constipation, urinary retention, medication adverse effects/anticholinergic burden, hunger/thirst, sensory impairment, sleep problems, inappropriate care, overstimulation/understimulation and other unmet needs/environmental triggers**. This should precede both drug and non-drug symptom treatment. | NICE NG97 §§1.7.1–1.7.2; QS184 |
| Non-pharmacological treatment | KEEP | Psychosocial/environmental approaches are first-line and should continue even if medication is added. Personalised activity, carer education/support, environmental modification and consistent care approaches are preferable to reflex medication. | NICE NG97 §§1.7.2, 1.7.8–1.7.9 |
| Antipsychotic threshold | UPDATE | Offer antipsychotics **only** when the person is at risk of harming self/others or has agitation, hallucinations or delusions causing **severe distress**, after causes and non-drug strategies have been addressed. Use lowest effective dose for shortest possible time. | NICE NG97 §§1.7.3–1.7.6 |
| Mortality/stroke risk | KEEP | Antipsychotics in dementia carry established **increased mortality and cerebrovascular-event risk**. Do not freeze Oxford’s old “1.5× mortality / 3× stroke” figures as universal board numbers; the safer board fact is the presence of clinically important excess mortality/stroke risk and the need for explicit risk–benefit discussion. | MHRA/NICE dementia antipsychotic safety guidance |
| DLB/PDD caution | KEEP | Antipsychotics can cause marked motor worsening and **severe sensitivity reactions in DLB/PDD**. This should be an explicit prescribing warning. | NICE NG97 §1.7.4; DLB Consortium |
| Risperidone licence | UPDATE | The harmonised European/UK indication is narrow: **short-term treatment (up to 6 weeks) of persistent aggression in moderate–severe Alzheimer dementia**, unresponsive to non-pharmacological approaches, when there is a risk of harm to self or others. Do not generalise this licence to all BPSD, all dementias or routine psychosis. | EMA Risperdal referral; NICE NG97 prescribing note |
| Haloperidol licence | UPDATE | Haloperidol has a narrower authorised role in persistent aggression/psychotic symptoms in moderate–severe Alzheimer and vascular dementia when non-drug measures fail and risk of harm exists, but its EPS/QT burden—and absolute unsuitability in DLB/PD—make it a high-caution option rather than a default BPSD drug. | NICE NG97; MHRA haloperidol safety update |
| Review/deprescribing | UPDATE | **Reassess antipsychotic need at least every 6 weeks** under NICE. Stop if there is no clear ongoing benefit, and continue psychosocial/environmental interventions during and after withdrawal. “6–12 weeks for everyone” is too crude; the specific risperidone licence is **up to 6 weeks**, while other antipsychotic use should be as short as possible with repeated reassessment. | NICE NG97 §§1.7.6–1.7.8 |
| Valproate for agitation | ADD | **Do not offer valproate solely for agitation/aggression in dementia** unless indicated for another condition. | NICE NG97 §1.7.10 |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q45:** Oxford/CAM-era teaching versus current NICE **4AT-first** assessment in general hospital/long-term-care settings; CAM-ICU/ICDSC remain preferred in critical care.
- **Q46:** “Dementia” versus **DSM major NCD** is a classification-system distinction, not a simple old/new replacement. ICD-11 still uses dementia terminology within neurocognitive disorders.
- **Q46/Q47 biomarkers:** Oxford-era structural imaging/CSF emphasis versus current specialist use of **validated plasma AD biomarkers**. Blood biomarkers are clinically important but do not replace comprehensive assessment universally.
- **Q47 disease modification:** older “no disease-modifying treatment” teaching is no longer literally current. **Lecanemab and donanemab are EU-authorised** for tightly selected biomarker-confirmed early AD, with major ARIA/MRI constraints and no implication of cure.
- **Q48:** RBD has moved from a supportive/suggestive DLB feature to a **core clinical feature** in current DLB Consortium criteria.
- **Q48 DLB/PDD:** retain the **1-year rule** as an operational classification convention, while noting that clinically DLB and PDD lie on a Lewy-body-disease spectrum.
- **Q48a:** pimavanserin may appear in US-oriented teaching but should **not** be presented as routine EU/Greek standard treatment without EU/Greek authorisation/access.
- **Q49:** older use of “anti-dementia drugs” in FTD should be removed; NICE explicitly says **do not offer AChE inhibitors or memantine** for FTD.
- **Q50:** “stepwise vascular dementia” is classic teaching, not a required modern phenotype; **VCI may be stepwise, abrupt or gradual**, and mixed AD/vascular pathology is common.
- **Q51:** avoid a generic “6–12 week antipsychotic course.” Current NICE says shortest possible time with **≥6-week reassessment**, while risperidone’s licensed Alzheimer-aggression indication is specifically **up to 6 weeks**.

## Unresolved issues

1. **Greek access to lecanemab/donanemab:** EU authorisation is confirmed, but routine Greek market availability, reimbursement, controlled-access implementation and prescribing pathway were not established from authoritative Greek sources during this pass. Do not imply routine Greek availability.
2. **Pimavanserin in Greece/EU:** no centrally authorised EU Parkinson-psychosis product/EPAR was identified and no Greek national authorisation was verified. Treat it as non-routine/non-verified European practice unless a later Greek regulatory check establishes otherwise.
3. **FTD symptomatic pharmacotherapy:** SSRIs/trazodone are reasonable symptom-targeted options, but evidence is substantially weaker than the negative guideline statement for AChE inhibitors/memantine. Do not turn them into a formal first-line “FTD drug regimen.”
4. No unresolved issue blocks creation of the Q45–Q51 answer briefs.
