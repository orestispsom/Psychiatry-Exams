## Q20. Major depressive disorder / depressive episode diagnosis and important differentials

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current depressive-episode threshold | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Current ICD-11 depressive episode requires **at least 5 of 10 symptoms**, present most of the day, nearly every day for **at least 2 weeks**, with at least one of **depressed mood or markedly diminished interest/pleasure**, clinically significant impairment, and appropriate exclusions. DSM-5-TR uses the familiar ≥5/9 major-depressive-episode architecture with the same 2-week/core-symptom principle. If the Oxford base retains the ICD-10 2-of-3-core symptom hierarchy, preserve that only as historical/exam-source framing and label ICD-11 separately. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; First et al., *World Psychiatry* 2021 |
| MDD versus current ICD terminology | UPDATE | “Major depressive disorder” is DSM terminology. ICD-11 diagnoses **single episode depressive disorder** or **recurrent depressive disorder** according to longitudinal course rather than using MDD as the primary label. For a viva, the syndrome is essentially the same depressive-episode construct, but classification names should not be mixed as if identical. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; First et al. 2021 |
| Bipolar exclusion/differential | KEEP | Before calling recurrent depression unipolar, actively seek lifetime mania/hypomania, including collateral history and treatment-emergent episodes. A convincing manic episode moves the diagnosis to bipolar I; hypomania plus major depression without mania supports bipolar II. Mixed manic symptoms, early/recurrent episodicity and family history may raise suspicion but are **not diagnostic by themselves**. | WHO Bipolar Disorder fact sheet 2025; ICD-11 bipolar framework; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Melancholic/atypical presentations | KEEP | Retain these as clinically useful patterns/specifiers if already present in the Oxford map, but do not let them replace the core depressive-episode diagnosis. Current ICD-11 supports specifier/qualifier-based characterisation including melancholic features; “atypical” phenomenology remains clinically descriptive but does not require a separate current-treatment algorithm in this short answer. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; ICD-11 mood-disorder implementation literature |
| Psychotic depression | UPDATE | Psychotic symptoms define a high-severity presentation requiring specialist assessment and materially affect treatment: current NICE guidance favours antidepressant plus antipsychotic treatment, with ECT considered when severe depression needs a rapid response or other treatments fail. Psychosis therefore belongs in both diagnosis/severity and treatment linkage, not merely as a descriptive subtype. | NICE NG222 §§1.12–1.13 |

### Q20a. PMDD versus premenstrual exacerbation (PME)

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| PMDD versus PME | UPDATE | The useful discriminator is the **cyclical on/off pattern**. PMDD/core premenstrual disorder has symptoms concentrated in the luteal/premenstrual phase with minimal or absent symptoms after menstruation and before ovulation. **PME** is an underlying psychiatric or medical disorder that remains present across the cycle but becomes significantly worse premenstrually. Persistent follicular symptoms argue for PME/comorbidity rather than pure PMDD. | ISPMD consensus; ACOG CPG No. 7 (2023) |
| Prospective charting | UPDATE | Do not diagnose PMDD confidently from retrospective recall alone when the distinction matters. Prospective daily symptom ratings are the validated method for demonstrating cyclicity; **two consecutive symptomatic cycles** is the standard confirmation period, with a third cycle useful if the first two disagree. The DRSP is a common validated tool. | ISPMD consensus; ACOG CPG No. 7 (2023) |
| Timing anchor | KEEP | High-yield timing is sufficient without criteria dumping: symptoms arise in the late luteal/premenstrual phase, improve within a few days after menses begins, and are minimal/absent in the post-menstrual follicular interval. The defining concept is recurrence linked to the menstrual cycle plus functional impact, not simply “feeling worse before periods.” | ICD-11 PMDD framework; ISPMD consensus |

## Q21. Treatment of a major depressive episode

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sequencing by severity and preference | UPDATE | Current guidelines do not support a rigid “mild = psychotherapy, moderate = drug, severe = drug” ladder. For less-severe depression, psychological/behavioural options are generally preferred and antidepressants are not routinely first-line unless preferred or clinically indicated. For more-severe depression, evidence-based psychotherapy and antidepressants are both first-line options; combined treatment becomes especially attractive with greater severity, chronicity, high risk or incomplete response. Shared decision-making, previous response, comorbidity, accessibility and patient preference matter. | NICE NG222 §§1.5–1.8; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Psychotherapy versus antidepressant versus combination | UPDATE | Structured psychotherapy and antidepressants are both effective. CANMAT favours psychotherapy when feasible for mild episodes and recommends combined antidepressant + psychotherapy for severe nonpsychotic depression with high safety risk; NICE ranks individual CBT plus an antidepressant among first-line choices for more-severe depression but does not mandate combination for every patient. Preserve this genuine difference in emphasis rather than inventing one universal sequence. | NICE NG222; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Psychotic depression | UPDATE | Refer to specialist mental health care; **consider antidepressant + antipsychotic** (NICE examples include olanzapine or quetiapine). Antidepressant monotherapy remains an option if the patient declines an antipsychotic. Continue the antipsychotic for a number of months after remission if tolerated, with stopping decided by/in consultation with specialist services. | NICE NG222 §§1.12.1–1.12.5 |
| ECT | KEEP | ECT remains a high-value option for **severe depression** when a rapid response is required because the condition is life-threatening (for example not eating/drinking), when other treatments have failed, or when the patient prefers ECT based on a previous effective course. It should move earlier in genuinely urgent psychotic/catatonic or life-threatening presentations rather than being described only as a remote last resort. | NICE NG222 §1.13; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Early review / adequate antidepressant trial | UPDATE | Review response and tolerability early rather than waiting passively for 6–8 weeks. NICE recommends reviewing lack/limited response after **4 weeks at a recognised therapeutic dose**, first checking adherence, psychosocial/medical contributors and diagnostic issues. CANMAT likewise treats absence of early improvement by about 4 weeks as a signal to optimise dose or switch depending on tolerability. A 4-week checkpoint is not the same as declaring definite treatment resistance. | NICE NG222 §1.9; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Continuation after remission | UPDATE | Do not stop antidepressants immediately after response. WHO’s current mhGAP recommendation is to continue effective antidepressant treatment for **at least 6 months after remission** in moderate-to-severe depression; NICE individualises longer relapse-prevention treatment according to recurrence risk and reviews long-term medication periodically. | WHO mhGAP 2023; NICE NG222 relapse-prevention section |
| Switching versus augmentation | KEEP | In a competent first-line viva, the decision is response-based: after checking pseudoresistance, **switch** if there is little/no benefit or poor tolerability; **augment/add** when there is a meaningful partial response to a well-tolerated antidepressant or recurrent difficulty with switching. Psychological treatment can also be added/switched. | NICE NG222 §1.9; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |

## Q22. Treatment-resistant depression

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Practical TRD definition | UPDATE | A conventional board definition remains depression that has not adequately improved after **at least two adequate antidepressant treatments** in the current episode. Current guidelines increasingly prefer a broader **difficult-to-treat depression (DTD)** formulation once burden persists across multiple reasonable treatments, because a binary “resistant/not resistant” label can obscure pseudoresistance, partial benefit and long-term management. Retain the two-failure TRD anchor, but do not let it replace reassessment. | NICE NG222/2026 surveillance; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Pseudoresistance | ADD | Before escalating, re-check the diagnosis (especially bipolarity), treatment adherence, adequate dose/duration, intolerable adverse effects, substance use, psychiatric comorbidity, relevant medical disorders/medications, psychosocial adversity and whether the chosen treatment was actually delivered competently. Apparent “TRD” without this reassessment is not a secure treatment-resistance diagnosis. | NICE NG222 §1.9; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Switching versus augmentation | KEEP | Switching is favoured after essentially no response or problematic adverse effects; augmentation is particularly useful after partial response to a tolerated antidepressant because it preserves existing benefit. Avoid reflexive polypharmacy; discuss additional adverse effects and interactions. | CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline; NICE NG222 |
| Atypical-antipsychotic augmentation | UPDATE | This is one of the strongest evidence-based pharmacological augmentation classes. NICE permits specialist augmentation with agents including aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone; CANMAT ranks **aripiprazole and brexpiprazole** as first-line adjunctive agents within its DTD hierarchy. The final Greek/European answer should mention the class and a well-established example rather than importing every North American ranking. | NICE NG222 §1.9; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Lithium augmentation | KEEP | Lithium remains an established augmentation strategy with important monitoring requirements. NICE includes it among further-line options; CANMAT retains it as a second-line adjunct because its evidence base is older/smaller and monitoring burden is substantial. This is a genuine guideline-ranking difference, not evidence that lithium is obsolete. | NICE NG222; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| ECT | KEEP | ECT remains the most important somatic treatment for severe, urgent or highly refractory depression, especially with psychosis, catatonia, severe suicidality or life-threatening refusal of food/fluids. | NICE NG222 §1.13; CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| rTMS | ADD | Repetitive TMS is an established non-invasive option for depression that has not responded adequately to standard treatment. NICE considers its safety acceptable and short-term efficacy adequate though variable; CANMAT places rTMS prominently for difficult-to-treat/TRD presentations. It belongs in Q22 as a major option, not an experimental footnote. | NICE HTG396 (current migrated guidance); CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |
| Esketamine / ketamine | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **Intranasal esketamine (Spravato) is EU-authorised** with an SSRI/SNRI for adults with TRD after failure of at least two antidepressants in the current moderate-to-severe episode, under supervised administration and monitoring. However, NICE continues **not to recommend** esketamine for NHS use because of clinical/economic uncertainty. IV racemic ketamine has evidence and international guideline support but is not an EU-authorised antidepressant indication. For a Greek board answer: esketamine deserves a brief “EU-licensed specialist option; local access/reimbursement varies” line, not routine first-line positioning. | EMA Spravato EPAR; NICE TA854 (reviewed 2024); CANMAT 2023 MDD guideline |

## Q23. Bipolar diagnosis and important alternatives

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bipolar I / II architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 now explicitly distinguishes **bipolar type I** (at least one manic episode) from **bipolar type II** (at least one hypomanic episode plus at least one depressive episode, with no history of mania), aligning the broad architecture with DSM-5-TR. This is a material change from ICD-10-era teaching. | WHO Bipolar Disorder fact sheet 2025; ICD-11 bipolar framework |
| Mania versus hypomania | KEEP | The clinically decisive distinction is severity/impairment, not simply symptom count: mania causes marked functional impairment, may require hospitalisation and may include psychosis; hypomania is a clear episodic change but does not produce the same marked impairment/psychosis. DSM retains **≥1 week for mania (or any duration if hospitalisation is required)** and **≥4 consecutive days for hypomania**. ICD-11 uses “several days” for hypomania rather than importing the DSM wording verbatim. | APA bipolar educational material; ICD-11 bipolar framework |
| Mixed features | UPDATE | Current systems have moved away from the old requirement for a full simultaneous “mixed episode.” Clinically important manic symptoms may coexist with depression and vice versa; mixed features increase diagnostic and treatment complexity and are a reason for caution with antidepressants. | DSM-5/5-TR mixed-features framework; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Bipolar versus unipolar depression | KEEP | No single depressive symptom establishes bipolarity. Diagnose longitudinally: actively elicit prior hypomania/mania, episodicity, collateral history, family history, treatment-emergent activation and mixed features. Do not relabel depression as bipolar solely because it is severe, psychotic, recurrent or antidepressant-resistant. | CANMAT/ISBD bipolar guidelines; WHO/ICD-11 framework |
| ADHD versus hypomania | UPDATE | ADHD is typically **trait-like and persistent from development**, while hypomania/mania represents a distinct episodic departure from baseline. Distractibility, talkativeness and activity overlap; decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, clear episodic mood/energy change and psychosis favour bipolar disorder. ADHD and bipolar disorder can coexist. | CANMAT/ISBD diagnostic guidance |
| Borderline personality affective instability versus bipolar episodes | UPDATE | Borderline affective instability is usually rapid, reactive to interpersonal/environmental events and embedded in a pervasive pattern of identity/interpersonal instability and impulsivity. Bipolar disorder requires sustained mood episodes representing a clear change from baseline. They can coexist; neither diagnosis should be made from “mood swings” alone. | CANMAT/ISBD diagnostic guidance |
| Substance/medication/medical causes | KEEP | A manic/hypomanic syndrome caused directly by substances, medications or another medical condition must be separated from primary bipolar disorder. Review stimulants, corticosteroids, antidepressants and other exposures, thyroid/neurological causes and intoxication/withdrawal according to presentation. | ICD-11 CDDR; DSM-5-TR architecture |
| Antidepressant-emergent mania/hypomania | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Current ICD-11, like DSM-5, permits a primary manic/hypomanic episode diagnosis when a full syndrome **persists after the antidepressant treatment is discontinued and its direct physiological effects have ceased/receded**. A brief activation confined to the drug’s direct effect should not automatically establish bipolar disorder. Older “any antidepressant-associated mania is substance-induced” teaching is therefore too simplistic. | ICD-11 bipolar framework; DSM-5-TR bipolar framework |

## Q24. Acute mania

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety/setting | KEEP | Assess risk to self/others, behavioural control, psychosis, substance use, medical causes, capacity and ability to adhere; severe mania frequently requires urgent specialist/inpatient management. Physical assessment and investigations should proceed as safely feasible without delaying essential containment. | NICE CG185; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| First-line pharmacological strategy | UPDATE | Major guidelines agree that an **antipsychotic, lithium or valproate/divalproex** are evidence-based antimanic agents, but hierarchy differs. NICE pragmatically starts with an antipsychotic (haloperidol, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone), switching antipsychotic before adding lithium; CANMAT/ISBD places lithium, several atypical antipsychotics and divalproex among first-line monotherapies and also endorses antipsychotic + lithium/divalproex combinations as first-line. Do not average these into a false universal algorithm. | NICE CG185 §§1.5; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Monotherapy versus combination | UPDATE | Monotherapy is reasonable for less severe mania and where tolerability/adherence favour simplicity. **Combination treatment** (antipsychotic plus lithium or divalproex) is appropriate early in more severe mania or when a faster/more robust response is needed and is supported as first-line by CANMAT/ISBD. NICE is more sequential. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; NICE CG185 |
| Antidepressants | KEEP | If mania/hypomania emerges, **stop/consider stopping antidepressants** and treat the manic syndrome. NICE explicitly advises considering cessation whether the antidepressant is used alone or with a mood stabiliser; CANMAT states antidepressants should be discontinued in acute mania. | NICE CG185 §§1.5.2, 1.5.7; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Benzodiazepines | KEEP | Benzodiazepines may be used briefly for severe agitation/insomnia while definitive antimanic treatment takes effect, but they are adjuncts, not core maintenance treatment and not a substitute for diagnosis/medical assessment. | Major bipolar/acute-agitation guidance |
| ECT | KEEP | ECT remains an option for **prolonged or severe mania** when other treatments fail or the condition is life-threatening; CANMAT also positions ECT for refractory mania. | NICE CG185 §1.5.13; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Valproate reproductive safety — women | UPDATE | EU regulation materially supersedes older routine use. For **bipolar disorder, valproate must not be used during pregnancy**. In girls/women able to become pregnant, it must not be used unless the Pregnancy Prevention Programme conditions are met and alternatives are ineffective/not tolerated. This must be a prominent safety qualifier whenever valproate appears in Q24/Q26. | EMA valproate pregnancy restrictions/PPP; current EU product information |
| Valproate reproductive safety — men | ADD | EMA/CMDh 2024 added precautionary measures for men because of a potential neurodevelopmental risk signal with paternal exposure in the 3 months before conception: initiation/supervision by a specialist, regular review, counselling about effective contraception for the man and female partner during treatment and for **at least 3 months after stopping**, and no sperm donation during that period. EOF communicated these measures in Greece. | EMA/CMDh 24 Jan 2024; EOF valproate DHPC 2024 |

## Q25. Bipolar depression

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core first-line agents | UPDATE | For a European-oriented answer, **quetiapine, lithium and lamotrigine** remain high-value core options, selected according to bipolar subtype, prior response, polarity, severity and maintenance needs. NICE specifically offers quetiapine or olanzapine-fluoxetine for moderate/severe bipolar depression when untreated, with olanzapine or lamotrigine alternatives; CANMAT/ISBD ranks quetiapine, lithium and lamotrigine among first-line bipolar-I depression options. | NICE CG185 §1.6; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Cariprazine / lurasidone | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | CANMAT/ISBD now includes **cariprazine** and lurasidone-based strategies among first-line bipolar-I depression options internationally. However, current EU EMA product information for **Reagila (cariprazine)** and **Latuda (lurasidone)** remains **schizophrenia-only**; neither has an EU bipolar-depression indication. They may be mentioned as international evidence, but should not be presented as routine EU/Greek licensed bipolar-depression treatments. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; EMA Reagila EPAR (updated 2026); EMA Latuda EPAR (updated 2026) |
| Antidepressant role | UPDATE | Antidepressants are not the backbone of bipolar-I depression treatment. CANMAT/ISBD allows adjunctive SSRIs/bupropion only as later-line options in selected patients and advises avoidance/caution with prior antidepressant-induced mania, mixed features or recent rapid cycling. Educate about switch warning signs and stop the antidepressant if mania emerges. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I | DROP | Do **not** recommend antidepressant monotherapy for bipolar-I depression. If an antidepressant is used, combine with an established mood-stabilising treatment and select patients carefully. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; WHO bipolar guidance |
| ECT | KEEP | ECT is appropriate for refractory bipolar depression and should be considered earlier when rapid response is needed because of severe suicidality, psychotic depression, catatonia or medical compromise from poor intake. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; ECT guidance |

## Q26. Long-term bipolar management

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lithium hierarchy | KEEP | Lithium remains a central first-line maintenance treatment and NICE explicitly calls it the most effective long-term pharmacological treatment for bipolar disorder. Selection must still incorporate previous response, tolerability, polarity and patient preference. Detailed serum/organ monitoring belongs to the later lithium question, not here. | NICE CG185 §§1.7.5–1.7.8; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Other maintenance agents / polarity | UPDATE | Quetiapine, lamotrigine, valproate/divalproex and selected antipsychotics have maintenance roles, with important phase specificity: **lamotrigine is useful for depressive relapse prevention but is not an acute antimanic drug**; antipsychotic choice is influenced by prior antimanic response and metabolic/tolerability burden. CANMAT recommends considering predominant polarity and the polarity of the most recent episode. | NICE CG185; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Continue what worked | KEEP | A treatment effective and tolerated in the acute phase is often a rational maintenance choice. Following mania treated with antipsychotic + lithium/divalproex, combination continuation can reduce early relapse, but long-term polypharmacy should be reviewed rather than continued automatically forever. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; NICE CG185 |
| Psychosocial maintenance | UPDATE | Medication is only one component. **Psychoeducation** has first-line guideline support; CBT and family-focused intervention have supportive evidence; regular sleep/social rhythms, relapse-signature recognition, crisis planning, adherence work and active management of substance use are core long-term care. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; NICE CG185 |
| Physical health | KEEP | Long-term care must include weight/BMI, cardiovascular and metabolic risk assessment and drug-specific monitoring, plus smoking/substance-use intervention. Avoid reproducing the later lithium-monitoring answer here. | NICE CG185 §§1.8, 1.10 |
| Duration | KEEP | Bipolar disorder is generally a recurrent/lifetime disorder; maintenance is commonly long term and often indefinite after recurrent/severe episodes. Do not give a single universal stopping date. Decisions reflect episode number/severity, psychosis, consequences, residual symptoms, relapse pattern, adverse effects and patient preference, and withdrawal should be gradual. | NICE CG185; CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update; WHO bipolar guidance |
| Lithium and suicide prevention | KEEP | Lithium has evidence suggesting reduction in suicide attempts/deaths and this is a useful additional reason to favour it in suitable patients, but avoid an exact percentage or claiming complete protection. | CANMAT/ISBD 2023 update |
| Valproate restrictions | UPDATE | Apply the same **EU pregnancy prohibition/PPP requirements** for women able to become pregnant and the **2024 paternal-exposure precautions** for men described under Q24. A maintenance answer that simply lists valproate without reproductive qualification is no longer acceptable current practice. | EMA valproate safety actions; EOF DHPC 2024 |

## Q27. Postpartum psychosis and postpartum depression

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timing and phenotype | KEEP | Postpartum psychosis usually has **sudden onset in the immediate postpartum period, commonly within the first 2 weeks**, with rapidly fluctuating affective, psychotic, behavioural and cognitive symptoms. Mania/mixed states, severe depression, insomnia, agitation, delusions/hallucinations and confusion/disorganisation may coexist. | NICE CG192 §1.5; Jairaj et al., *J Psychopharmacol* 2023 |
| Bipolar-spectrum relationship | UPDATE | Contemporary evidence strongly links postpartum psychosis to the **bipolar spectrum** rather than treating it as simply “schizophrenia after childbirth.” A previous postpartum psychosis or bipolar disorder is highly relevant to future risk and prophylaxis planning. Avoid freezing old recurrence percentages unless specifically needed. | Jairaj et al. 2023; NICE CG192 |
| Emergency status and safety | KEEP | Treat postpartum psychosis as a **psychiatric emergency**. Assess suicide/self-harm, infant harm/infanticidal thoughts, delusional beliefs involving the infant, ability to care safely, rapidly fluctuating risk and relevant medical/neurological differentials. NICE requires immediate specialist perinatal assessment **within 4 hours** when postpartum psychosis is suspected. | NICE CG192 §§1.5.11–1.5.12; Jairaj et al. 2023 |
| Admission / mother–baby unit | KEEP | Acute inpatient care is generally required. Where available and safe, admission should be to a **specialist mother-and-baby unit**; NICE states that women needing inpatient mental-health care within 12 months after childbirth should normally be admitted to an MBU unless there is a specific reason not to. | NICE CG192 §1.10.1; Jairaj et al. 2023 |
| Acute pharmacological treatment | UPDATE | Immediate pharmacological treatment is required; current evidence supports antipsychotic treatment with **lithium added/used where clinically appropriate**, with short-term benzodiazepine use for severe agitation/insomnia when necessary. Management should be individualised for medical status, breastfeeding and previous response rather than applying one fixed sequence. | Jairaj et al. 2023 treatment algorithm; NICE CG192 |
| ECT | KEEP | ECT should be considered early when a rapid response is needed, particularly with severe/refractory illness, catatonia, severe agitation or suicidal/infanticidal risk. It is not merely a last-resort historical treatment in postpartum psychosis. | Jairaj et al. 2023 |
| Relapse prevention / future pregnancy | UPDATE | Lithium has the strongest evidence for relapse prevention after postpartum psychosis and is an important prophylactic option for women with a previous episode. Future pregnancies require **preconception specialist perinatal planning** and an explicit antenatal/postpartum relapse-prevention plan; prophylaxis immediately after delivery may be appropriate in high-risk women. Do not freeze old recurrence percentages. | Jairaj et al. 2023; NICE CG192 §§1.4–1.5 |
| Breastfeeding and psychotropics | UPDATE | Breastfeeding decisions require drug-specific risk–benefit assessment that includes maternal relapse risk, infant exposure, prematurity/medical vulnerability and the harms of stopping effective treatment. NICE does **not** recommend breastfeeding while taking lithium, clozapine or carbamazepine and recommends infant monitoring when psychotropics are used. Do not present “breastfeeding-compatible” as a class property of antidepressants/antipsychotics; use current drug-specific prescribing/perinatal advice. | NICE CG192 §§1.4.4, 1.4.17, 1.9.8–1.9.11 |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q20 classification:** Oxford-era ICD-10 diagnostic architecture may remain examinable historically, but current ICD-11 depressive episodes use a ≥5-of-10, ≥2-week structure with depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure as the required core symptom. Do not call ICD-10 criteria “current ICD.”
- **Q22 esketamine:** EU-authorised for TRD after ≥2 antidepressant failures, but NICE continues not to recommend it for NHS use. “Licensed” and “guideline-recommended/available” are different claims.
- **Q23 bipolar classification:** ICD-11 now formally includes bipolar type II and allows antidepressant-emergent mania/hypomania to count when the full syndrome persists beyond the treatment’s direct effects; older ICD-10-era shorthand can mislead.
- **Q24/Q26 valproate:** historical routine use must be separated from current EU reproductive restrictions, including the 2024 precautionary measures for men.
- **Q25 newer bipolar-depression agents:** CANMAT/ISBD supports cariprazine/lurasidone strategies internationally, but current EMA labels for Reagila and Latuda are schizophrenia-only. Do not convert international evidence into an EU licence claim.
- **Q27a zuranolone:** older teaching will not mention it; as of 2026 it has an EU PPD indication. Brexanolone remains a US-centred intervention and should not be presented symmetrically in a European answer.
