## Q28. Panic disorder and agoraphobia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Panic disorder versus agoraphobia architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 treats **panic disorder and agoraphobia as separate diagnoses**. Both may be diagnosed when each disorder’s requirements are independently met. Panic attacks occurring only in agoraphobic situations do not by themselves justify panic disorder; they can be represented as panic attacks within agoraphobia. Do not carry forward the older “panic disorder with/without agoraphobia” architecture as current ICD classification. | Kogan et al. 2016 ICD-11 anxiety Working Group; Rebello et al. 2020 WHO field study; First et al. 2021 |
| Panic disorder course anchor | UPDATE | ICD-11 focuses on recurrent **unexpected** panic attacks plus persistent concern or behaviour change related to recurrence/consequences; it does not use a universal “6-month” duration requirement for panic disorder. Avoid importing agoraphobia/GAD duration rules into panic disorder. | ICD-11 anxiety/fear-related framework; Kogan et al. 2016 |
| Agoraphobia duration | KEEP | ICD-11 agoraphobia requires marked persistent fear/avoidance of situations in which escape may be difficult or help unavailable, causing distress/impairment, persisting for **at least several months**. Panic symptoms are common but not required for the diagnosis. | ICD-11 MMS 6B02; Kogan et al. 2016 |
| CBT/exposure | KEEP | CBT remains the central psychological treatment. For panic disorder with avoidance/agoraphobia, treatment includes cognitive work plus graduated exposure/interoceptive or situational exposure as clinically appropriate. | NICE CG113, panic-disorder recommendations |
| Pharmacotherapy | KEEP | SSRIs remain the core long-term pharmacological option; SNRIs also have evidence and licensing in some jurisdictions. NICE recommends an SSRI licensed for panic disorder; TCAs are later options. Benzodiazepines are not recommended as routine long-term treatment because of poorer long-term outcomes/dependence concerns. | NICE CG113 §§1.3.20–1.3.26 |

## Q29. Generalised anxiety disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 diagnostic architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 GAD is persistent anxiety on more days than not, expressed as excessive worry across multiple domains and/or general apprehensiveness (“free-floating anxiety”), with associated tension/autonomic/cognitive symptoms and clinically significant distress or impairment. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; First et al. 2021 |
| Six-month rule | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **Six months is DSM-specific, not a universal current rule.** ICD-11 requires symptoms for **at least several months**, more days than not. Any Oxford/DSM-style “6 months” line should therefore be labelled by classification system rather than presented as the ICD standard. | First et al. 2021; ICD-11 GAD description |
| Psychological treatment | KEEP | CBT is a first-line/high-intensity treatment; NICE also supports applied relaxation and lower-intensity CBT-based self-help within stepped care. Treatment choice should reflect impairment, prior treatment and preference. | NICE CG113 §§1.2.12–1.2.22 |
| SSRIs/SNRIs | KEEP | If medication is chosen, an **SSRI is first-line**; another SSRI or an SNRI is appropriate if the first is ineffective or poorly tolerated. | NICE CG113 §§1.2.23–1.2.24 |
| Pregabalin | UPDATE | Pregabalin is a guideline-supported alternative **when SSRIs/SNRIs cannot be tolerated**, rather than a co-equal first-line drug. Relevant safety/dependence/pregnancy issues should be considered, but controlled-drug scheduling does not need to become a board fact here. | NICE CG113 §1.2.25, current update notes |
| Benzodiazepines | KEEP | Do not use benzodiazepines as routine GAD treatment; NICE limits them to short-term use during crises. | NICE CG113 §1.2.26 |

## Q30. Social anxiety disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current ICD-11 diagnosis | KEEP | ICD-11 social anxiety disorder centres on marked fear/anxiety in one or more social situations because of anticipated negative evaluation, with avoidance or intense anxiety and clinically important distress/impairment, persisting for **at least several months**. | ICD-11 MMS 6B04; Kogan et al. 2016 |
| Performance-only versus generalised | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | DSM-5/DSM-5-TR replaced the old “generalized” subtype with a **performance-only specifier** for fear restricted to speaking/performing in public. ICD-11 does not use that DSM specifier as its core architecture; it simply permits social anxiety focused on one or more social situations. Do not present “generalized social phobia” as a current formal subtype. | DSM-5-TR architecture; Kogan et al. 2016 |
| Avoidant personality disorder differential | KEEP | Social anxiety is organised around fear of negative evaluation in social situations. Avoidant personality disorder is a broader, enduring personality pattern of social inhibition, inadequacy and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation across contexts. They may coexist; chronicity alone does not prove personality disorder. | NICE CG159 assessment framework; current DSM/ICD personality architecture |
| Autism differential | ADD | Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with persistent social-communication differences plus restricted/repetitive patterns or sensory/behavioural features; social avoidance may reflect communication demands, sensory load or prior adverse experiences rather than primarily fear of negative evaluation. Autism and social anxiety can coexist. | ICD-11 neurodevelopmental framework |
| Psychological treatment | KEEP | **Individual disorder-specific CBT** is the preferred first treatment in NICE, incorporating behavioural experiments/exposure, reduction of safety behaviours/self-focused attention and cognitive restructuring. | NICE CG159 §§1.3.2, 1.3.13–1.3.14 |
| Pharmacotherapy | KEEP | If the patient prefers medication or declines CBT, SSRIs are the principal pharmacological treatment; NICE specifically recommends escitalopram or sertraline, with alternative SSRI/SNRI options after nonresponse/intolerance. | NICE CG159 §§1.3.5–1.3.10 |
| Beta-blockers | DROP | Do not present beta-blockers as established treatment for social anxiety disorder. A 2025 systematic review found no robust benefit for beta-blockers in social phobia/anxiety disorders. They may sometimes be used off-label for **circumscribed performance-related autonomic symptoms**, but this is not a core guideline treatment and should be an optional caveat at most. | Archer et al., *J Affect Disord* 2025; NICE CG159 |

## Q31. Obsessive-compulsive disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classification / insight | UPDATE | ICD-11 places OCD within **obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (OCRD)**, separate from anxiety/fear-related disorders. Insight varies; when OCD-consistent beliefs are held with extreme fixity, ICD-11 uses a **poor-to-absent-insight qualifier** rather than automatically diagnosing delusional disorder in the absence of other psychotic features. | Stein et al. 2016 ICD-11 OCRD Working Group; Reed et al. 2019 |
| ERP | KEEP | CBT incorporating **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** remains the core evidence-based psychological treatment. Severity determines intensity; severe impairment commonly warrants combined ERP-based CBT plus an SSRI. | NICE CG31 §§1.5.1.1–1.5.1.4 |
| SSRI treatment | KEEP | SSRIs remain first-line pharmacotherapy. Response can be slower than in depression and treatment should not be declared unsuccessful after a brief trial. | NICE CG31 |
| Adequate SSRI trial | UPDATE | NICE treats **up to 12 weeks** as the key full-trial window before declaring inadequate response to SSRI monotherapy, after checking adherence, dose and substance interference. If a standard dose is ineffective but tolerated after **4–6 weeks**, gradual dose escalation may be considered. | NICE CG31 §§1.5.3.12–1.5.4.3 |
| “High-dose SSRI” | UPDATE | The useful principle is optimisation toward the **upper licensed/SmPC range when needed and tolerated**, not automatic supra-licensed dosing. Dose escalation should follow the relevant product information and monitoring; “OCD needs high-dose SSRIs” is too crude as a universal board statement. | NICE CG31 §§1.5.3.13–1.5.3.14 |
| Clomipramine | KEEP | Clomipramine remains a later-line evidence-based option, generally after at least one adequate SSRI has been ineffective or poorly tolerated, or where there has been previous benefit/patient preference. Its anticholinergic/cardiovascular and overdose toxicity burden makes it less suitable as routine first-line treatment. | NICE CG31 §§1.5.4.3–1.5.4.14 |
| Antipsychotic augmentation | KEEP | Antipsychotic augmentation is a **specialist resistant-OCD strategy**, not routine early treatment and not antipsychotic monotherapy. NICE considers it only after substantial failure of SSRI, ERP-based combined treatment and clomipramine pathways. | NICE CG31 §§1.5.4.5–1.5.4.10 |

### Q31a. Hoarding disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classification | UPDATE | Hoarding disorder is a **separate ICD-11 OCRD diagnosis**, not merely an OCD symptom/subtype. | WHO ICD-11 OCRD framework; Stein et al. 2016; Reed et al. 2019 |
| Core phenotype | KEEP | The core is persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of objective value, leading to accumulation that substantially compromises living spaces and causes distress/impairment. **Excessive acquisition is common but is not required in every case.** | Stein et al. 2016; ICD-11 hoarding framework |
| Insight | ADD | Insight can be limited or absent; poor insight does not convert the disorder into psychosis if the beliefs/behaviour remain congruent with hoarding phenomenology. | ICD-11 OCRD insight framework |
| Treatment | ADD | If treatment is mentioned, specialised CBT targeting discarding, acquisition, decision-making and avoidance is the clinically useful point. Do not assume standard OCD pharmacotherapy has equivalent evidence for primary hoarding disorder. | Contemporary hoarding consensus/review; ICD-11 distinction |

## Q32. Post-traumatic stress disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 PTSD architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 deliberately narrows PTSD to three core domains after extremely threatening/horrific trauma: **re-experiencing in the present**, **avoidance**, and **persistent sense of current threat** (hypervigilance/exaggerated startle), with functional impairment. It should not be taught as a long undifferentiated DSM-style symptom list. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR 2024; Maercker et al. 2013; ICD-11 implementation review 2023 |
| Complex PTSD | ADD | ICD-11 **Complex PTSD** requires the PTSD syndrome plus persistent **disturbances in self-organisation (DSO)** across three domains: affect dysregulation, persistent negative self-concept, and relationship/interpersonal difficulties. CPTSD is not simply “more severe PTSD” or synonymous with borderline personality disorder. | ICD-11 CDDR; Maercker et al.; NICE NG116 recognition section |
| Trauma-focused CBT | KEEP | Individual trauma-focused CBT is a first-line treatment for adults; examples include cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure therapy and prolonged exposure. | NICE NG116 §§1.6.15–1.6.17 |
| EMDR | KEEP | EMDR is also an established first-line trauma-focused treatment for adults, particularly after the first months following non-combat trauma; it should not be described as experimental. | NICE NG116 §§1.6.18–1.6.20 |
| Medication | UPDATE | Medication is **not preferred over trauma-focused psychotherapy** when psychotherapy is acceptable/available. If the adult prefers drug treatment, NICE supports an SSRI or venlafaxine; medication is not recommended routinely to prevent PTSD immediately after trauma. | NICE NG116 §§1.6.24–1.6.26 |
| Psychological debriefing | DROP | Routine single-session psychologically focused **debriefing should not be offered** to prevent or treat PTSD; evidence shows no benefit and possible harm. | NICE NG116 §1.6.5 and rationale |

## Q33. Acute stress disorder versus adjustment disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| DSM-5-TR Acute Stress Disorder | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | DSM-5-TR retains **Acute Stress Disorder (ASD)** as a mental disorder after qualifying trauma: **9 or more symptoms** drawn from intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance and arousal, lasting **3 days to 1 month** after trauma, with distress/impairment and exclusions. | DSM-5-TR; Merck Professional 2026 summary citing DSM-5-TR |
| ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | ICD-11 **does not classify Acute Stress Reaction as a mental disorder**. It is placed among factors influencing health/encounters with services as a transient normal-range response to exceptional stress, usually arising within hours/days and beginning to subside within about a week or after removal from threat. Do not call this “ICD-11 acute stress disorder.” | Maercker et al.; ICD-11 stress-disorder review 2023 |
| ICD-11 adjustment disorder | UPDATE | ICD-11 adjustment disorder is a maladaptive response to an identifiable stressor characterised particularly by **preoccupation with the stressor/consequences and failure to adapt**, causing functional impairment. It usually emerges within about **1 month** of the stressor and tends to resolve within **6 months after the stressor/consequences end**, unless the stressor persists. | ICD-11 stress framework; Maercker et al. |
| Viva distinction | ADD | Keep the systems separate: DSM ASD follows **trauma of sufficient severity** and occupies the 3-day-to-1-month post-trauma window; ICD acute stress reaction is a non-disorder transient response; adjustment disorder follows a broader identifiable psychosocial stressor and is defined by preoccupation/failure to adapt rather than a trauma-specific ASD syndrome. | DSM-5-TR; ICD-11 CDDR/stress framework |

## Q34. Dissociative disorders

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 organisation | UPDATE | ICD-11 substantially reorganises dissociative disorders and drops “conversion” from the grouping title. High-yield adult categories include **dissociative amnesia** (with fugue as a qualifier), **depersonalisation-derealisation disorder**, trance/possession trance disorders, **dissociative identity disorder and partial DID**, and **dissociative neurological symptom disorder**. | Reed et al. 2019; First et al. 2021 |
| Depersonalisation/derealisation | KEEP | Depersonalisation is experienced detachment/unreality regarding the self; derealisation concerns unreality/detachment from surroundings, with reality testing generally preserved. Persistent/recurrent clinically impairing symptoms constitute the disorder after exclusions. | ICD-11 dissociative framework |
| Dissociative amnesia | KEEP | Dissociative amnesia is inability to recall important autobiographical information inconsistent with ordinary forgetting, after excluding neurological, substance and other causes; dissociative fugue is no longer a separate ICD-11 disorder but a qualifier. | Reed et al. 2019 |
| DID | KEEP | DID belongs in the adult differential but does not need extensive criteria recital. Diagnosis requires clinically significant identity disruption/discontinuity with associated memory/agency phenomena and careful exclusion of cultural practices, substances, neurological disorders and deliberate fabrication. | ICD-11 dissociative framework |
| Functional neurological symptoms in ICD-11 | UPDATE | ICD-11 places **dissociative neurological symptom disorder** within the dissociative-disorders grouping; this is the ICD counterpart of DSM-5-TR functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder). The different placement reflects a genuine DSM/ICD conceptual split. | First et al. 2021; Reed et al. 2019 |
| Major differentials | KEEP | Neurological disease, epilepsy/syncope, delirium, substance/medication effects, sleep disorders, PTSD and other psychiatric disorders must be considered according to phenotype. Dissociation should not be diagnosed simply because routine tests are normal. | ICD-11 framework; FND guidance |

## Q35. Somatic symptom disorder versus illness anxiety disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| DSM-5-TR SSD | UPDATE | DSM-5-TR **somatic symptom disorder (SSD)** requires one or more distressing/disruptive somatic symptoms plus disproportionate/excessive thoughts, anxiety or behaviour related to them. The symptoms **do not have to be medically unexplained**; SSD can coexist with genuine medical disease. | APA DSM-5-TR Somatic Symptom chapter; APA Dictionary 2023 |
| DSM-5-TR IAD | KEEP | In **illness anxiety disorder (IAD)**, the dominant feature is preoccupation with having/acquiring serious illness while somatic symptoms are absent or mild, accompanied by high health anxiety and excessive checking or maladaptive avoidance. Significant symptom burden with excessive symptom-focused response points instead toward SSD. | DSM-5-TR; Merck Professional 2026 |
| ICD-11 bodily distress disorder | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | ICD-11 does not map neatly onto DSM SSD. **Bodily distress disorder (BDD)** is defined by distressing bodily symptoms plus excessive attention/preoccupation and impairment, also **without requiring absence of a medical explanation**. It replaces much of the old somatoform architecture. | Reed et al. 2019; Gureje & Reed 2016; ICD-11 field study 2023 |
| ICD-11 health anxiety/hypochondriasis | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | ICD-11 retains **hypochondriasis (health anxiety disorder)** but places it primarily within **OCRD** (cross-listed with anxiety/fear-related disorders), rather than bodily distress disorders. A prominent bodily symptom is not essential. Do not force it into one-to-one equivalence with DSM IAD despite substantial overlap. | Stein et al. 2016; Reed et al. 2019 |
| “Medically unexplained symptoms” | DROP | Do not teach SSD/BDD as diagnoses of “medically unexplained symptoms.” Current DSM and ICD deliberately shifted away from that requirement; the diagnostic target is the maladaptive distress/attention/cognition/behaviour and functional burden, after appropriate medical assessment. | APA DSM-5-TR; Reed et al. 2019 |
| Management | KEEP | High-yield management is continuity with a consistent clinician/team, validation without reinforcing catastrophic interpretations, proportionate rather than repetitive investigation, planned follow-up, treatment of psychiatric/medical comorbidity and a collaborative biopsychosocial formulation. New or changing red flags still require appropriate medical reassessment. | Current somatic-symptom clinical guidance/reviews |

## Q36. Functional neurological disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive diagnosis | UPDATE | FND is **not a diagnosis of exclusion**. Diagnosis should be supported by positive features showing internal inconsistency or incongruity with recognised neurological disease, interpreted in the clinical context. Normal tests alone are insufficient. | Stone et al. 2024; JNNP FND consensus recommendations |
| Positive signs | KEEP | Useful rule-in signs include **Hoover sign/hip-abductor sign** for functional weakness and **distractibility/entrainment/variability** for functional tremor. These demonstrate preserved capacity for normal movement under altered conditions and can also aid explanation to the patient. No single sign should be used mechanically outside the correct phenotype. | Nielsen et al. JNNP physiotherapy consensus; JNNP motor-FND review |
| Phenotypes | KEEP | Major presentations include functional weakness/gait or movement disorders, sensory symptoms, speech/communication symptoms and **functional seizures**. Functional seizures require careful semiology and, where uncertainty remains, video-EEG of typical events when feasible; coexisting epilepsy must be considered. | AAN Functional Seizures Guideline 2025/26; ICD-11 dissociative neurological symptom disorder |
| Psychological stress/trauma | DROP | Psychological trauma or a recent stressor is **not required for diagnosis**. Psychological factors may be relevant predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating factors in some patients but should not be forced into every formulation. | Nielsen et al.; DSM-5-TR FND architecture; Stone et al. 2024 |
| Multidisciplinary treatment | KEEP | Treatment is phenotype- and needs-based: clear diagnostic explanation, specialist **physiotherapy/rehabilitation** for motor disability, occupational/speech therapy where relevant, and psychological therapy when indicated for functional symptoms or comorbidities. Neurology and psychiatry/psychology should not treat FND as belonging exclusively to the other discipline. | JNNP physiotherapy/OT/speech consensus; Stone et al. 2024 |
| Physiotherapy principle | ADD | FND-informed physiotherapy aims to retrain automatic movement, redirect abnormal self-focused attention, demonstrate normal movement and reduce maladaptive movement/avoidance patterns rather than simply strengthening a supposedly damaged pathway. | Nielsen et al. JNNP consensus |

### Q36a. Explaining FND

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symptoms are real/involuntary | KEEP | State explicitly that the symptoms are **real, involuntary and potentially disabling**. Avoid language implying fabrication, weakness of character or “nothing is wrong.” | JNNP OT consensus; AAN Functional Seizures Guideline |
| Explain the positive diagnosis | ADD | Name FND directly and explain **why** the diagnosis was made, ideally demonstrating an applicable positive sign (for example Hoover sign or tremor entrainment) to show altered function and preserved capacity rather than relying on “your scan is normal.” | JNNP OT/physiotherapy consensus; Stone et al. |
| Reversibility/function | KEEP | Explain that the problem concerns how nervous-system functions are being controlled/integrated rather than evidence of irreversible structural destruction, while acknowledging that chronic symptoms may still be difficult to treat. Positive signs can demonstrate potential reversibility. | FND consensus recommendations |
| “All psychological” explanation | DROP | Do not tell the patient the symptoms are “all psychological,” caused by trauma, or due to stress unless that is specifically supported by the person’s history and formulation. FND is best communicated with a biopsychosocial/non-dualistic model. | Stone et al. 2024; Nielsen et al. |
| Software/hardware analogy | UPDATE | “Software versus hardware” can be a **useful optional analogy**, but it is not canonical and can oversimplify modern FND biology or imply absolutely normal brain structure. Prefer a functional-network/control explanation tailored to the patient; use the analogy only if it aids understanding. | FND communication literature; Perez et al. 2019 structural review |

### Q36b. Factitious disorder versus malingering versus FND

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| FND | KEEP | FND symptoms are **not intentionally produced**. Positive functional signs identify altered neurological function; they do not establish deception. | FND consensus/AAN guidance |
| Factitious disorder | UPDATE | Factitious disorder involves **intentional falsification or induction** of physical or psychological symptoms with deception, while presenting oneself (or another) as ill/impaired/injured, **in the absence of obvious external rewards**. “Assuming the sick role” is a useful traditional description but motivation does not have to be psychodynamically proven. | DSM-5-TR Somatic Symptom chapter; Merck Professional 2026 |
| Malingering | KEEP | Malingering is **intentional symptom production/exaggeration for identifiable external incentives** (for example financial/legal/occupational gain) and is **not itself a psychiatric disorder**. | APA patient/professional DSM-derived material; Merck Professional 2026 |
| Motivation/intent caution | ADD | Intent and motive often cannot be inferred reliably from inconsistency, unusual symptoms, secondary gain or clinician suspicion alone. FND, factitious disorder, malingering and genuine neurological disease can coexist in complex cases; accusations require evidence of deliberate deception rather than phenomenological oddity. | Contemporary FND/factitious guidance |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q28:** older panic-agoraphobia linkage versus current ICD-11 separation. Panic disorder and agoraphobia are independent diagnoses and can be comorbid.
- **Q29:** DSM retains a **6-month** GAD minimum; ICD-11 uses **at least several months**. Never call 6 months the universal current rule.
- **Q30:** DSM-5-TR has a **performance-only** social-anxiety specifier; the old “generalized” subtype is obsolete. ICD-11 does not use the DSM performance-only specifier as its classification backbone.
- **Q31/Q31a:** OCD and hoarding now sit in a dedicated ICD-11 OCRD grouping; hoarding disorder is independent rather than an OCD subtype.
- **Q32:** ICD-11 has **Complex PTSD** with DSO domains; DSM-5-TR does not contain a directly equivalent CPTSD diagnosis.
- **Q33:** DSM-5-TR has **Acute Stress Disorder (3 days–1 month)**; ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction is explicitly a **non-disorder** health-status factor. These must not be blended.
- **Q34/Q36:** DSM-5-TR places FND in Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders; ICD-11 places its counterpart, dissociative neurological symptom disorder, under Dissociative Disorders.
- **Q35:** DSM SSD/IAD and ICD-11 bodily distress disorder/hypochondriasis are overlapping but non-equivalent architectures. Neither current system requires symptoms to be medically unexplained.
