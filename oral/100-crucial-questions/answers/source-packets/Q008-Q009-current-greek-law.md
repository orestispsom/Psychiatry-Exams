# Q08–Q09 Current Greek-Law Verification

Scope: current Greek legal/professional verification only for informed consent (Q08) and involuntary psychiatric hospitalisation (Q09). This is not a model answer and does not import foreign capacity law.

## Q08. Valid informed consent in Greek psychiatric practice

| Point | Decision | Current Greek-law finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prior consent as the default rule | KEEP | A physician may not perform a medical act without the patient’s prior consent. The general Greek rule is therefore consent first, subject only to specific statutory exceptions. | Law 3418/2005 (Code of Medical Ethics), art. 12(1); Law 2619/1998 (Oviedo Convention), art. 5 |
| Information required before consent | UPDATE | Valid consent must follow **full, clear and comprehensible information**. The physician must explain the actual health condition, the content and expected result of the proposed act, foreseeable consequences and risks/complications, alternatives and expected recovery time, sufficiently for the patient to make an informed decision. The patient also has a right not to receive information and may designate another person to be informed. | Law 3418/2005 arts. 11(1)–(4), 12(2)(a); Law 2619/1998 art. 5 |
| Voluntariness | KEEP | Consent must not result from mistake, fraud or threat and must cover the specific medical act and the time at which it is carried out. The Oviedo Convention additionally makes explicit that consent must be free and may be withdrawn at any time. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12(2)(c)–(d); Law 2619/1998 art. 5 |
| “Capacity” versus legal validity of consent | UPDATE | Greek law uses the concept **ικανότητα για συναίνεση** (capacity/ability to consent) but does **not** codify the UK Mental Capacity Act four-part test. For a board answer, clinical assessment should establish whether the particular patient can understand sufficiently the health problem, proposed intervention, consequences/risks and relevant alternatives so that the consent is meaningful; however, this clinical judgement must be kept distinct from the legal rules identifying who may authorise treatment when the patient lacks capacity. Diagnosis, hospitalisation status or disagreement with the doctor does not by itself establish incapacity. | Law 3418/2005 arts. 11–12; art. 28(4)–(7); Law 2619/1998 arts. 5–7 |
| Adult patient who lacks ability to consent | UPDATE | If an adult patient lacks capacity to consent, the Code directs consent to the **judicial supporter (δικαστικός συμπαραστάτης), if one has been appointed**; if none exists, consent is given by the patient’s **relatives/close persons (οικείοι)**. The physician must still inform the patient as far as feasible and attempt to secure voluntary participation, cooperation and assent, particularly where the patient can understand elements of the decision. The Oviedo Convention similarly requires the person concerned to participate as far as possible and restricts substitute authorisation to interventions for the person’s direct benefit. | Law 3418/2005 arts. 11(4), 12(2)(bb); Law 2619/1998 art. 6 |
| Minors | KEEP | For minors, consent is provided by those exercising parental responsibility/custody, while the minor’s opinion must be taken into account when, in the physician’s judgement, age and intellectual/emotional maturity permit understanding of the condition, intervention and its consequences/risks. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12(2)(aa); Law 2619/1998 art. 6(2) |
| Emergency treatment without consent | KEEP | Consent is not required when appropriate consent cannot be obtained and there is an **immediate, absolute and urgent need** for medical care. The Code separately includes attempted suicide, and also permits necessary immediate intervention when authorised third parties refuse consent and delay would endanger the patient’s life or health. The Oviedo Convention independently permits medically necessary intervention in an emergency when appropriate consent cannot be obtained. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12(3)(a)–(c); Law 2619/1998 art. 8 |
| Psychiatry-specific autonomy | ADD | The psychiatric chapter of the Code is explicit that a person with a mental disorder remains a rights-bearing partner in treatment. The psychiatrist must respect dignity, autonomy, values and preferences, use interventions that restrict freedom as little as possible, and provide information about the nature of the condition, therapeutic procedures, alternatives and likely outcome. Mental disorder therefore does **not** itself cancel consent rights. | Law 3418/2005 art. 28(1), (3)–(7) |
| Treatment against the patient’s will | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Greek psychiatric ethics law states that the psychiatrist should not administer treatment against the patient’s will except where refusal endangers the patient’s life or those around them or seriously worsens the course of the mental disorder; where compulsory hospitalisation becomes necessary, the statutory involuntary-admission procedure must be followed. This must **not** be collapsed into the proposition that “involuntary admission automatically authorises every treatment”: admission, decisional capacity, consent to a specific intervention and emergency treatment are legally distinct questions. | Law 3418/2005 art. 28(8); Law 2071/1992 arts. 95–99; Law 2619/1998 art. 7 |

### Q08 legal cautions for the later adjudicator

- Do **not** import the UK statutory “understand–retain–use/weigh–communicate” formula as if it were Greek legislation. It can be used, if desired, only as a clinical reasoning aid after being clearly labelled as such; the Greek statutory wording is different.
- The Code’s fallback to “οικείοι” when no judicial supporter exists is broad and legally less precise than modern substitute-decision frameworks. It should be quoted cautiously rather than expanded into an invented hierarchy of relatives.
- Capacity is decision-specific in sound clinical practice, but the Greek Code does not provide a detailed statutory psychometric or four-limb test. The safest board formulation is a functional clinical assessment tied to the concrete decision, followed by the Greek legal rules on who may consent.
- Involuntary psychiatric status is not synonymous with global incapacity. The legal criteria for involuntary hospitalisation in Q09 are separate from the legal validity of consent to individual medical acts.

## Q09. Involuntary psychiatric hospitalisation in Greece

| Point | Decision | Current Greek-law finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing legal framework | KEEP | The substantive framework remains **Law 2071/1992, arts. 95–100**, still identified by the Ministry of Health and the Prosecutor of the Supreme Court as the operative law. The un enacted 2019 reform proposal must not be presented as current law. Later reforms have mainly changed service organisation and, importantly, the transport procedure. | Law 2071/1992 arts. 95–100; Ministry of Health mental-health legislation page; Prosecutor of Supreme Court Circular 8/2025 |
| Statutory criteria: Route I | KEEP | Involuntary hospitalisation is permitted where **all three** are present: (a) mental disorder; (b) inability to judge the interest of one’s own health; and (c) without hospitalisation, treatment would be excluded or the state of health would deteriorate. | Law 2071/1992 art. 95(2)(I) |
| Statutory criteria: Route II | KEEP | There is an **alternative** statutory route: the person has a mental disorder and hospitalisation is necessary to prevent acts of violence against self or others. This remains operative; the 2019 proposal to remove dangerousness was never enacted. | Law 2071/1992 art. 95(2)(II); Ministry of Health 2019 consultation notice (proposal only) |
| Social nonconformity | KEEP | Refusal or inability to conform to prevailing social, moral or political values is explicitly **not, by itself, a mental disorder**. This is a high-value rights safeguard. | Law 2071/1992 art. 95(3) |
| Who may initiate | KEEP | The procedure may be requested by the spouse, relatives in the direct line without degree limit, collateral relatives up to the second degree, or the person exercising care/guardianship as specified by law. If none is available, in an emergency the **Public Prosecutor of the Court of First Instance** for the place of residence/stay may initiate ex officio. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(1) |
| Initial medical reports | KEEP | Ordinarily the application to the prosecutor must be accompanied by **two reasoned written medical opinions**, from two psychiatrists or, if two cannot be found, one psychiatrist and one physician of a related specialty. The opinions must address the statutory conditions under art. 95(2); the examining doctors must not be related to the applicant or patient. Psychiatrists/child psychiatrists providing the reports are drawn from the statutory lists compiled by local medical associations. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(2)–(3) |
| When the person has not been examined | KEEP | If the prosecutor acts ex officio, or the application states that examination was impossible because the person refused examination, the prosecutor may order transfer to a **public psychiatric clinic for examination and preparation of the opinions**. Detention there for the necessary examinations may not exceed **48 hours**. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(5); Prosecutor of Supreme Court Circular 8/2025 |
| Prosecutor after concordant opinions | KEEP | Where formal requirements are met and both opinions support involuntary hospitalisation, the prosecutor orders transfer/admission to an appropriate Mental Health Unit; if the opinions conflict, the prosecutor may still order transfer and submits the matter for judicial determination. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(4) |
| Court with current jurisdiction | UPDATE | Although the unreconciled wording of Law 2071/1992 still says **Πολυμελές Πρωτοδικείο**, current civil-procedure law assigns involuntary-hospitalisation cases to the **Μονομελές Πρωτοδικείο**. This is a genuine statute-text mismatch that the Writer must not smooth over. Greek appellate case law expressly treats the Single-Member Court as the competent first-instance court following the amendment of CPC art. 740. | CPC art. 740 as amended by Law 2447/1996; Piraeus Court of Appeal 123/2024 |
| Prosecutor and court deadlines | UPDATE | The still-operative procedural timetable in art. 96 requires the prosecutor, **within 3 days of ordering transfer**, to submit the application for judicial determination. The court is to sit **within 10 days**. The patient must be summoned at least **48 hours** before the hearing and may appear with a lawyer and a psychiatrist as technical adviser. These statutory time limits remain part of the current framework, despite persistent practical-compliance concerns described in Greek oversight literature. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(6); Piraeus Court of Appeal 123/2024 confirms current judicial competence framework |
| Judicial decision | KEEP | Proceedings are under voluntary-jurisdiction procedure and in camera to protect private life. The judicial decision must be specifically reasoned. If the application is rejected, immediate discharge is ordered; if accepted, involuntary hospitalisation continues. The court may order examination by another psychiatrist when the existing opinions conflict, are unconvincing or are contradicted by the unit’s scientific director. | Law 2071/1992 art. 96(6)–(8) |
| Immediate information and legal safeguards | KEEP | Upon transfer to the Mental Health Unit, the patient must be informed immediately of rights, especially the right to challenge the decision; a record of this information is drawn up. The patient has the right to participate in the hearing with counsel and psychiatric technical adviser. Appeal and objection are available. During hospitalisation, dignity must be respected and restrictions on liberty must be determined only by health status and treatment needs. | Law 2071/1992 arts. 96(4), 96(6), 97, 98 |
| Duration and review | KEEP | Involuntary hospitalisation must end as soon as the art. 95 criteria cease. The ordinary statutory maximum remains **6 months**. After the first **3 months**, the scientific director and another psychiatrist submit a report to the prosecutor. The patient or eligible relatives/guardian may request discontinuation; if refused, a new request may be made after 3 months. Extension beyond 6 months is reserved for exceptional cases and requires the favourable opinion of a three-psychiatrist committee. | Law 2071/1992 art. 99 |
| Transport after 2022 reform | UPDATE | **Law 4931/2022 added art. 96A** and materially changed the transport model. Transfer following a prosecutor’s order is to occur as for any other patient, safely and with respect for dignity. The statutory model uses a mixed initial team including psychiatrist, nurse and police officer; onward transfer is by an appropriately equipped community mental-health vehicle with psychiatrist/nurse. Police accompaniment during transfer is exceptional and requires prosecutor involvement plus a psychiatrist’s written assessment that restrictive measures may be necessary because imminent self- or other-directed harm cannot be prevented by de-escalation. Restrictive measures are a last resort. | Law 4931/2022 art. 59 inserting Law 2071/1992 art. 96A; Ministry of Health 12 May 2022; Prosecutor of Supreme Court Circular 8/2025 |
| Effect of 2024 mental-health service reform | UPDATE | Law 5129/2024 reorganised the mental-health service system into national/regional networks and explicitly aims to reduce involuntary hospitalisations, but it did **not** repeal or replace the core admission criteria of arts. 95–99. The later service architecture should therefore not be mistaken for a new substantive involuntary-treatment statute. | Law 5129/2024 arts. 1–2; current consolidated Law 2071/1992 |

### Q09 current-law cautions for the later adjudicator

- The **2019 draft law is not current law**. Its proposed removal of dangerousness, community compulsory treatment and four-month maximum must not enter the board answer as enacted provisions.
- Current law contains a drafting/coordination problem: Law 2071/1992 art. 96 still names the **Multi-Member Court**, whereas CPC art. 740 gives jurisdiction to the **Single-Member Court**. The current answer should use Μονομελές Πρωτοδικείο and, if useful, briefly note the legacy wording mismatch.
- The **48-hour limit** applies to temporary stay for examination when the person had not been examinable beforehand; it is not the maximum duration of involuntary hospitalisation.
- The statutory **3-day prosecutor filing / 10-day court sitting / 48-hour summons** timetable is part of the law and should be stated exactly, while recognising that historic implementation failures do not repeal the deadlines.
- Involuntary **admission** and involuntary **treatment** should not be treated as identical legal acts. Consent to particular treatment remains governed by the Code of Medical Ethics, emergency exceptions and psychiatry-specific safeguards.

## Unresolved legal questions

1. **Detailed implementation of art. 96A transport nationwide.** The 2022 statute is in force and the Prosecutor of the Supreme Court reaffirmed the applicable transport rules in Circular 8/2025. However, operational availability of community-unit vehicles/teams is service-dependent. This does not change the legal board answer, but real-world local pathways may differ.
2. **Appeal-court composition after the shift to the Single-Member Court of First Instance.** Art. 97 of Law 2071/1992 retains legacy wording referring to a three-member appellate court, while later civil-procedure changes altered first-instance competence. The existence of appeal/objection is clear; the exact current appellate formation is not necessary for Q09 and should not be made a board fact without a dedicated procedural-law check.
3. **Meaning/order of “οικείοι” for substitute consent under art. 12(2)(bb) Law 3418/2005.** The statute does not provide a detailed priority hierarchy. Do not invent one.
