## Q52. Severe self-neglect / squalor / refusal of help

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical framing | UPDATE | Severe self-neglect/squalor is a **syndrome/problem presentation, not a diagnosis**. Assess personal care, nutrition/hydration, medication adherence, untreated disease, fire/environmental hazards, infestation, finances, dependants, social isolation and ability to obtain essential care. Avoid using “Diogenes syndrome” as if it specifies one aetiology. | Oxford base map; contemporary geriatric/self-neglect practice principles |
| Drivers | KEEP | Retain neurocognitive disorder, depression, psychosis, substance use, hoarding disorder, delirium and frontal/executive dysfunction as important causes/contributors. Multiple causes commonly coexist. | Oxford map; NICE dementia/delirium frameworks |
| Capacity / valid refusal in Greece | UPDATE | A capacitous adult may refuse an offered medical intervention after adequate information, even when clinicians consider the choice unwise. Greek law uses **ικανότητα για συναίνεση** but does **not** codify the UK Mental Capacity Act understand–retain–use/weigh–communicate test. Clinically, assess ability to understand the concrete problem, proposed intervention, material consequences/risks and alternatives sufficiently to make a meaningful decision; keep this distinct from the legal rules governing substitute consent. | Greek Law 3418/2005 arts. 11–12; Law 2619/1998 arts. 5–8; existing Q008–Q009 Greek-law verification |
| Decision-specific assessment | KEEP | Capacity should be assessed **for the specific decision at the relevant time** rather than inferred globally from diagnosis, age, eccentricity, poor hygiene, cognitive screening score or disagreement with professionals. Executive dysfunction may particularly impair appreciation/implementation of consequences and deserves active assessment. | Greek Code/Oviedo framework plus clinical capacity practice |
| Eccentric lifestyle versus incapacity | UPDATE | Severe clutter, unconventional living or refusal of services is not enough to prove incapacity. The key question is whether the person can understand/appreciate the consequences of the specific choice and act from a sufficiently intact decision-making process, not whether professionals approve of the lifestyle. | Greek autonomy/consent framework; Law 2071/1992 art. 95(3) analogue against social nonconformity as mental disorder |
| Home / multidisciplinary assessment | KEEP | Where safe and feasible, assess the person **in the home environment** and integrate psychiatry, primary/medical care, nursing, social work, occupational/environmental assessment and family/collateral information. The physical environment may reveal hazards and functional failures not visible in clinic. | Current community mental-health/geriatric practice; Law 2716/1999 community-care principles |
| Least-restrictive care | KEEP | Use persistent engagement, practical harm reduction, treatment of reversible drivers, support with food/medication/hygiene/repairs, and incremental environmental risk reduction. Coercive intervention should not substitute for engagement when a valid refusal can be made. | Greek psychiatric rights framework; Law 3418/2005 art. 28; Law 2716/1999 |
| Severe self-neglect and involuntary psychiatric admission | UPDATE | **Self-neglect alone is not an independent Greek statutory criterion for involuntary psychiatric admission.** Admission requires a mental disorder plus one of the existing Law 2071/1992 routes: (I) inability to judge the interest of one’s health and, without admission, treatment would be excluded or health would deteriorate; or (II) admission is necessary to prevent acts of violence against self/others. Thus severe self-neglect may contribute evidence to Route I when caused by mental disorder and accompanied by impaired judgement/health deterioration, but squalor itself is insufficient. | Law 2071/1992 art. 95; Ministry of Health legislation index; Q008–Q009 packet |
| Intervention despite refusal | UPDATE | For **medical treatment**, Greek law permits emergency intervention without consent when appropriate consent cannot be obtained and there is an immediate, absolute and urgent medical need; substitute authorisation rules apply when an adult lacks capacity. Involuntary psychiatric admission must follow its separate statutory procedure. These provisions do **not** create a general licence to force non-urgent social-care or home-cleaning interventions on a capacitous refusing adult. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12(2)–(3); Oviedo arts. 6, 8; Law 2071/1992 |
| Greek “adult safeguarding” | UNRESOLVED | Greece does not map cleanly onto the UK statutory “adult safeguarding” construct. Current official sources show fragmented health, disability, social-care, municipal/community and judicial-support mechanisms, but no single general adult-self-neglect statute was identified that authorises compulsory social intervention solely because an adult lives in squalor. Do not import UK safeguarding powers. | Greek Ministry/amea.gov.gr legislation review; Law 2716/1999; Law 4486/2017 |

### Q52 adjudication note
Oxford’s clinical distinction between eccentric choice and disorder-driven self-neglect is useful. Its imported UK four-part capacity formula must be removed as Greek law. The defensible Greek answer is: **assess the concrete decision functionally, respect valid refusal, treat emergencies under the emergency exception, and use involuntary psychiatric admission only when Law 2071/1992 criteria are met.**

## Q53. Autoimmune encephalitis red flags

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| When to suspect | ADD | The key board trigger is a **rapid/subacute (<3 months), polysyndromic psychiatric–neurological deterioration**, especially when a new psychiatric syndrome is accompanied by seizures, catatonia, cognitive decline, reduced consciousness, abnormal movements/dyskinesias, autonomic instability, language dysfunction or focal neurological signs. | Abboud et al., JNNP 2021; Graus-style AE framework |
| Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis | UPDATE | Anti-NMDAR encephalitis remains the prototypical “psychiatric-first” AE: acute behavioural/psychotic or affective change may precede seizures, catatonia, dyskinesias, speech reduction/mutism, altered consciousness, autonomic instability and hypoventilation. Do not frame it as “schizophrenia with antibodies.” | Abboud et al. 2021; Pollak et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2020 |
| Antipsychotic sensitivity | ADD | **Marked or paradoxical antipsychotic intolerance**, severe rigidity/catatonia or an NMS-like deterioration is a useful red flag in the appropriate rapidly progressive syndrome, but is not specific enough to diagnose AE by itself. | Pollak et al. 2020 autoimmune-psychosis consensus; AE symptomatic-management guidance |
| Other antibodies | ADD | One or two pattern anchors are enough: **LGI1** often produces faciobrachial dystonic seizures, memory/behavioural change and hyponatraemia; **CASPR2** can produce limbic encephalitis with peripheral nerve hyperexcitability/autonomic features. Avoid an antibody catalogue. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| MRI / EEG | UPDATE | Obtain brain **MRI and EEG**. MRI may be normal, including in anti-NMDAR encephalitis, so a normal MRI does not exclude AE. EEG helps identify diffuse/focal dysfunction and non-convulsive seizures/status; “extreme delta brush” is associated with anti-NMDAR encephalitis but is not required. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| CSF | ADD | **Lumbar puncture is central** unless contraindicated: cell count/protein/glucose, infectious studies (especially HSV/VZV as appropriate), oligoclonal bands/IgG indices and neuronal-antibody testing. CSF may be the only early inflammatory abnormality. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| Serum versus CSF antibodies | ADD | Test **both serum and CSF** when AE is suspected. CSF is more sensitive/specific for some clinically important antibodies, especially NMDAR; serum-only positivity can mislead. Conversely, some antibodies such as LGI1 may be more sensitive in serum. Interpret antibody results in the clinical syndrome rather than as stand-alone proof. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| Tumour search | KEEP | Search for malignancy according to phenotype/antibody and demographic risk. In anti-NMDAR encephalitis, targeted ovarian-teratoma assessment is especially important in appropriate patients. Tumour treatment/removal is part of disease treatment where relevant. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| First-line immunotherapy | ADD | Once infection is reasonably excluded and AE remains likely, first-line therapy is **high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG and/or plasma exchange**, with combination treatment considered early in severe disease. Do not wait for every antibody result if the clinical syndrome strongly supports AE. | Abboud et al. 2021 |
| Second-line | ADD | If there is inadequate improvement after appropriate first-line therapy, second-line immunotherapy—commonly **rituximab** for antibody-mediated disease, or cyclophosphamide in selected contexts—may be required at specialist level. | Abboud et al. 2021 acute/long-term recommendations |

## Q54. Epilepsy and psychiatry

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depression/anxiety/suicide | KEEP | Depression and anxiety are important comorbidities in epilepsy and suicide risk is increased. Screen actively, especially after diagnosis/treatment changes and with behavioural adverse effects. Do **not** retain Oxford’s old exact prevalence or “5–10-fold” suicide figures as board facts. | NHS SPS 2026; epilepsy psychiatry literature |
| Ictal/postictal/interictal psychosis | KEEP | The temporal framework remains clinically useful: ictal psychosis occurs during epileptic activity; **postictal psychosis** follows seizures/seizure clusters after recovery of the immediate postictal state; interictal psychosis lacks a close temporal link to a recent seizure. | Contemporary epilepsy-psychosis review 2024 |
| Lucid interval | UPDATE | The **lucid interval remains a high-yield hallmark** of postictal psychosis, but Oxford’s fixed “24–48 h” should be softened. Contemporary reports describe roughly **hours to several days** (e.g. 12–120 h), with no single universally accepted duration; onset is generally within a week after the seizure cluster. | Epilepsy and psychosis review 2024; contemporary PIP review |
| Levetiracetam | ADD | Levetiracetam can cause **irritability, aggression/hostility, depression, anxiety, agitation and psychotic symptoms**; suicidal ideation/behaviour is reported. New behavioural change after initiation or dose increase should prompt medication review rather than automatic attribution to the underlying psychiatric disorder. | Current levetiracetam SmPC/EMA product information |
| Clozapine | KEEP | Clozapine lowers the seizure threshold in a **dose-related** manner; risk is greater with rapid titration and pre-existing epilepsy. This is a prescribing caution, not an absolute contraindication to use in epilepsy. | Current clozapine SmPC |
| Bupropion | UPDATE | Bupropion has a clinically important dose-related seizure risk and current European/UK product information **contraindicates it in patients with a current seizure disorder or any history of seizures**. | Zyban SmPC 2026 |
| Antidepressants generally | UPDATE | Do not say “antidepressants are contraindicated in epilepsy.” **SSRIs are commonly preferred** because seizure risk is comparatively low; use licensed doses, start cautiously, monitor seizure frequency and review interactions. Strongly pro-convulsant TCAs such as clomipramine/amitriptyline deserve greater caution. | NHS SPS 2026 |
| Enzyme induction | KEEP | Strong enzyme-inducing ASMs (classically carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital/primidone) can **lower concentrations/effect of many psychotropics**; conversely psychotropics may alter ASM concentrations. The board fact is the interaction principle, not a large table. | Prescribing references / interaction principles |
| Functional/dissociative seizures | UPDATE | Current ILAE terminology favours **functional/dissociative seizures (FDS)** rather than relying on “psychogenic non-epileptic seizures.” They resemble epileptic seizures but are not intentionally produced and are managed through a positive neurological diagnosis plus multidisciplinary care; video-EEG remains important when diagnostic uncertainty exists. | ILAE Psychiatry Commission 2025; AAN functional-seizure guideline 2025/26 |
| Gastaut–Geschwind syndrome | DROP | Do not teach a fixed “temporal-lobe personality/Gastaut–Geschwind syndrome” as established contemporary nosology. Hypergraphia, religiosity or altered sexuality may occur in individual patients, but the historical syndrome is controversial and has poor specificity. | Contemporary epilepsy-neuropsychiatry literature |

## Q55. Traumatic brain injury

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychiatric sequelae | KEEP | Retain depression, irritability/aggression, emotional lability, apathy, executive dysfunction/personality change, substance misuse and suicide/self-harm risk as important neuropsychiatric sequelae. Symptoms require longitudinal assessment because neurological, psychiatric and environmental drivers overlap. | VA/DoD mTBI CPG; INCOG 2.0; neuropsychiatric TBI literature |
| Acute confusion versus later syndromes | UPDATE | During the acute post-injury phase, distinguish **post-traumatic amnesia/post-traumatic confusional state and delirium** from persistent depression, behavioural dyscontrol, executive syndromes or personality change. Acute agitation during PTA does not establish a chronic psychiatric diagnosis. | INCOG 2.0 PTA guideline 2023 |
| PTA role | KEEP | PTA remains a **clinically important severity/prognostic marker**, particularly in moderate–severe TBI, and should be measured prospectively with a validated PTA instrument when possible. | INCOG 2.0 Part I 2023 |
| Oxford PTA severity table | DROP | Do not freeze Oxford’s exact `<1 h / 1–24 h / 1–7 d / >7 d` four-bin table as a universal current severity classification. PTA duration is prognostically useful, but contemporary systems and injury-severity classifications use differing thresholds and combine PTA with GCS, loss of consciousness and imaging. | INCOG 2.0; contemporary TBI classification practice |
| Agitation during PTA | UPDATE | First-line management is **environmental/rehabilitative**: secure supervised setting, low stimulation, consistent staff, sleep–wake support, simple communication, reassurance, identification of triggers and minimising restraints. Avoid reflexive sedating polypharmacy. | INCOG 2.0 Part I 2023 |
| Antipsychotics/sedatives in PTA | UPDATE | Medication may be necessary for immediate dangerous agitation, but routine neuroleptic/sedative exposure should be minimised because it can worsen cognition, mobility and recovery; use the lowest effective burden and review frequently. | INCOG PTA guidance; rehabilitation practice |
| Post-TBI depression | KEEP | Treat genuine major depression rather than attributing all low drive to “organic change.” **SSRIs are commonly favoured** because of tolerability and relatively low seizure burden; psychotherapy/behavioural rehabilitation should be integrated where feasible. Monitor cognitive adverse effects, falls/sedation and seizures. | VA/DoD mTBI/MDD frameworks; epilepsy-prescribing guidance |
| Rehabilitation/family | ADD | Neuropsychiatric treatment should be embedded in **multidisciplinary rehabilitation**, with cognitive/executive strategies, environmental modification, communication support, return-to-role planning and family/carer education. | INCOG 2.0 Parts II–V |
| Medication sensitivity | KEEP | TBI can increase vulnerability to sedation, anticholinergic cognitive worsening, orthostasis and seizure-threshold-lowering effects. “Start low, go slow, reassess function” is safer than applying routine psychiatric doses without regard to neurological status. | Neurorehabilitation prescribing principles |

## Q57. Apathy versus depression

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core apathy definition | UPDATE | Current consensus defines apathy as a **quantitative reduction in goal-directed activity relative to the person’s previous functioning**, affecting behavioural/cognitive, emotional and/or social dimensions. This is more precise than simply “low motivation.” | Robert et al. 2018 international consensus |
| Depression distinction | KEEP | Apathy commonly lacks the pervasive sadness, guilt, worthlessness, hopelessness and suicidal ideation that define a depressive syndrome. The patient may show indifference/low initiation rather than painful negative affect. However, apathy and depression can coexist. | Apathy consensus/reviews |
| Anhedonia | UPDATE | **Anhedonia and apathy overlap but are not equivalent.** Anhedonia is diminished pleasure/interest; apathy is reduced goal-directed activity/initiation. A patient can have one without the other. | Contemporary apathy/depression reviews |
| Common neurological associations | KEEP | Apathy is especially common in disorders affecting frontal-subcortical circuits, including Parkinson disease, stroke, TBI and neurodegenerative disorders such as AD/FTD. Do not label all low motivation in medically ill older adults as apathy before excluding delirium, depression, fatigue, medication effects and environmental deprivation. | Consensus/review literature |
| Consensus duration/domains | ADD | If an examiner asks for an operational framework, the 2018 consensus requires persistent symptoms for **at least 4 weeks**, affecting **at least 2 of 3 domains** (behaviour/cognition, emotion, social interaction), with functional impairment and exclusion of better explanations. This is a useful follow-up, not necessary in the model oral opening. | Robert et al. 2018 |
| Scale | DROP | Do not make the Apathy Evaluation Scale or another rating scale a must-know board fact. Scales can quantify symptoms but do not improve the core viva distinction enough to justify space. | Consensus framework |

## Q58. Charles Bonnet syndrome

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core syndrome | KEEP | Charles Bonnet syndrome is characterised by **recurrent visual hallucinations in a person with visual impairment**, usually complex/formed (people, animals, faces, scenes or patterns), without delirium or a primary psychotic syndrome explaining them. | AAO Vision Rehabilitation PPP; updated CBS review 2025/26 |
| Insight | UPDATE | Preserved recognition that the images are unreal is a classic and diagnostically useful feature, but **perfect, immediate insight should not be treated as an absolute bedside rule**. Partial insight or initial uncertainty can occur, especially before explanation; persistent absent insight should prompt reconsideration of delirium, psychosis or neurodegenerative disease. | AAO PPP; CBS reviews including full/partial insight descriptions |
| “Exclusively visual” | KEEP | Hallucinations are characteristically visual. Prominent hallucinations in other modalities, delusions, cognitive fluctuation or altered consciousness should push the differential away from uncomplicated CBS. | AAO PPP; CBS diagnostic literature |
| Differential | KEEP | Exclude/consider **DLB/Parkinson disease, delirium, epilepsy, medication/substance effects, primary psychosis and other neurological disease**, especially if cognition is impaired, insight is poor, the phenomenology is atypical or there are neurological signs. | AAO PPP; updated CBS review |
| Treatment backbone | KEEP | **Education/reassurance and optimisation of vision/low-vision rehabilitation** are the treatment backbone. Explaining the syndrome reduces fear and prevents inappropriate antipsychotic labelling. Simple environmental/behavioural strategies may help individual patients. | AAO Vision Rehabilitation PPP; updated review |
| Pharmacotherapy | UPDATE | There is **no standard pharmacological treatment with robust evidence** for uncomplicated CBS. Case reports/series describe antipsychotics, anticonvulsants and other agents, but routine medication—especially routine antipsychotic treatment—should not be recommended. Treat the ocular cause and reassess atypical/distressing cases. | Updated CBS review 2025/26 |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q52/Q56 capacity:** Oxford’s UK four-limb statutory formulation may remain useful as a foreign clinical mnemonic, but it is **not Greek law**. Greek consent/refusal must be taught from Law 3418/2005 + Oviedo, with involuntary admission separately governed by Law 2071/1992.
- **Q52 self-neglect:** “severe squalor = grounds for compulsory admission” is wrong. In Greece, self-neglect matters only insofar as the statutory mental-disorder/admission criteria are actually met.
- **Q53 autoimmune encephalitis:** Oxford’s anti-NMDAR paragraph is too narrow for current practice. A psychiatric presentation with rapid progression plus neurological red flags requires EEG/MRI/**CSF** and paired serum/CSF antibody testing; serum-only testing is inadequate for several key antibodies.
- **Q54 postictal psychosis:** retain the **lucid interval** concept, but replace a rigid “24–48 h” fact with **hours to days, usually within 1 week**.
- **Q54 functional seizures:** “PNES” remains widely recognised, but current ILAE terminology favours **functional/dissociative seizures (FDS)** and avoids assuming a psychogenic cause in every patient.
- **Q54 Gastaut–Geschwind:** historical/contested teaching should not survive as an established personality syndrome.
- **Q55 PTA:** PTA duration remains important, but Oxford’s exact four-bin severity table should not be frozen as a universal current classification.
- **Q58 CBS:** “intact insight” is a classic diagnostic anchor, but learner-facing wording should allow **partial/initially uncertain insight** and use persistent absent insight as a red flag for another diagnosis rather than declaring CBS impossible from the first interview.

## Unresolved issues

1. **Greek adult self-neglect/safeguarding powers:** no single Greek statute equivalent to the UK adult-safeguarding framework was identified. Health/social/community and judicial mechanisms are fragmented. The safe board answer should not claim a generic compulsory “safeguarding” power over a capacitous adult living in squalor.
2. **Forced non-urgent social/environmental intervention:** the medical emergency and substitute-consent rules are clear, but this pass did not identify a general Greek legal power to compel non-urgent home cleaning/social-care entry purely for self-neglect. Any such local action should be presented as case-specific and legally/administratively coordinated, not as a standard psychiatric power.
3. **TBI pharmacotherapy:** evidence for specific drugs for agitation/aggression after TBI remains heterogeneous. This packet intentionally preserves only high-level rehabilitation/environmental and cautious prescribing principles rather than naming a universal first-line drug.
4. **CBS insight:** definitions vary across ophthalmology/neurology literature. Preserved insight remains the classic construct, but partial/initially uncertain insight is documented. Persistent absent insight requires broader diagnostic reassessment.
5. No unresolved issue blocks creation of Q52–Q58 answer briefs.
