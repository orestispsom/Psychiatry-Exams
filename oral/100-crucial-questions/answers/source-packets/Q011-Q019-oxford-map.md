## 2. Reusable chapter map

### Aetiology (269–279)
- **Table 11.3 scaffold:** genetic (SNPs, CNVs, rare variants) / early environment (maternal malnutrition, maternal infection, birth complications)
  / social (urban birth, migration, ethnic-minority status, early cannabis use); **hypotheses** = neurodevelopmental, gene–environment interaction,
  dopamine, glutamate, dysconnectivity, immune/inflammatory.
- Heritability **81%** (twin meta-analysis) but **64%** in population studies. Polygenic: "No genes are either necessary or sufficient, and they act
  as risk factors, not determinants." Three variant classes: **SNPs** (GWAS **108 loci**, ORs ~1.05), **CNVs** (rare, high-impact; 2.5% of patients vs
  0.9% of controls; **22q11 deletion / velocardiofacial syndrome, 1 in 4000 births, psychosis in ~30%**), **rare variants** (SETD1A, OR 32). Much
  heritability remains "missing".
- **Table 11.4 lifetime risk:** MZ twin 48%; two affected parents 46%; DZ twin 17%; child of one affected parent 17%; sibling 9%; half-sibling 6%;
  first cousin 2%; **baseline 1%**.
- Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder show **no independent familial transmission** (Lichtenstein 2009); risk of schizophrenia, **schizoaffective
  disorder** and schizotypal/paranoid PD is raised in first-degree relatives → **spectrum**. But there is **no clear excess of CNVs in bipolar
  disorder** — "this may be one of the points of separation in the aetiology of these disorders."
- **Table 11.6 relative risks:** life events 3.2; migrant 2.9; childhood trauma 2.8; tobacco 2.2; maternal malnutrition 2; birth complications 2;
  **cannabis 2**; urban birth 1.9; advanced paternal age 1.7; winter birth 1.1; maternal infection unquantified.
- **Cannabis (p. 276):** Andreasson 1987, >45,000 Swedish conscripts over 15 years — **RR 2.5, sixfold in heavy users**; later cohorts OR ~2;
  modified by genotype and strain; **associated with earlier age of onset**; causality and specificity "remain controversial". Tobacco likewise
  raises risk with earlier onset. **Diagnostic rule stated here:** "If substance use is considered to have directly caused or precipitated the
  psychosis, it is diagnosed as such, and not as schizophrenia."
- Box 11.12 psychodynamic/family causal theories (schizophrenogenic mother, marital skew/schism, double bind) are recorded as **historical and
  empirically unsupported**, but still influential among families and a source of unjustified guilt.

### Neurobiology (279–283)
- **Structural (Box 11.13):** ventricular volume **+30%**, brain volume **−2.6%**, intracranial volume **−2%** (implying early-childhood origin);
  largest effects **hippocampus and thalamus**; similar in antipsychotic-naïve patients; **no "organic" subtype** (unimodal shift); changes small,
  overlapping with controls, **not diagnostically specific**.
- **Neuropathology:** brain weight −2–3%; **no neurodegeneration or gliosis**; reduced synaptic/dendritic markers; parvalbumin interneurons and
  layer III pyramidal neurons. **Functional:** hypofrontality (Ingvar & Franzen 1974), strongest with psychomotor poverty; reduced cortical
  *efficiency* on fMRI working-memory tasks; **dysconnectivity**; P300 and P50 deficits, also in relatives.
- **Dopamine:** two convergent origins — amphetamine psychosis, and all antipsychotics being D2 antagonists with D2 affinity correlating best with
  clinical potency. Imaging: the main abnormality is **markedly increased striatal dopamine synthesis and release**; its extent **predicts
  antipsychotic response**; it **develops during the prodrome** and is **absent in remission**; it lies primarily in the **associative rather than the
  ventral striatum**. A separate **cortical dopamine deficit** relates to cognitive impairment. **Kapur's aberrant salience** links dopamine to
  phenomenology.
- **Glutamate (Box 11.14):** NMDA antagonists (phencyclidine, ketamine) induce or exacerbate the syndrome; co-agonists reduce some symptoms;
  altered glutamate markers and spectroscopy; risk genes affect glutamate signalling; anti-NMDA receptor antibodies in some cases.
  **Oxford's synthesis: glutamate is the more primary "trait" abnormality, dopamine downstream and "state"-related.**
- **Secondary layers:** GABA (reduced GAD, parvalbumin interneurons); serotonin (LSD as 5-HT2 agonist); inflammation (MHC/C4 signal; microglial PET
  activation **awaiting replication**, overall significance unclear).

### Neurodevelopmental model (283)
Clouston (1892) and Kraepelin; modern form Murray & Lewis (1987), Weinberger (1987); variants implicate the second trimester, adolescence, or both.
**Box 11.15:** structural changes at/before onset; limited progression afterwards; childhood motor/cognitive/social impairment; absence of gliosis
implying prenatal timing; most environmental risks prenatal/perinatal; **soft neurological signs at presentation**; minor physical anomalies and
dermatoglyphics; cavum septum pellucidum; risk genes affecting brain development; animal models.
**Oxford's critique — the examinable line:** "There are no serious challenges to the neurodevelopmental model … This partly reflects its vagueness,
which allows most findings to be interpreted as being consistent with it. Its weaknesses include a failure to readily explain the variable course of
the disorder or provide a convincing explanation for late-onset schizophrenia."

### Course and prognosis (284–286)
- **Manfred Bleuler 1974** (208 patients, 20 years): 20% complete remission, 35% good social adjustment, 24% severely disturbed, and **full recovery,
  when it occurs, usually within the first 2 years**. **Harrison 2001** (15 countries, 15/25 years): **one-sixth fully recovered**, with **late recovery
  in a significant minority**. **AESOP 10-year:** **23% unremitting**, **45% free of psychotic symptoms ≥2 years**, and **social outcomes often worse than
  symptomatic ones**. **Jääskeläinen 2013** (recovery = symptomatic *and* social/functional, sustained ≥2 years): **about 14%**. **IPSS/WHO:** better
  2-year outcome in India, Colombia and Nigeria, stable at 15/25 years, explanation **controversial**.
- **Box 11.16 poor-outcome factors.** *Demographic:* male; single; younger age at onset; family history of schizophrenia; comorbid substance abuse.
  *Clinical:* poor premorbid adjustment; insidious onset; **long duration of untreated psychosis**; hebephrenic (disorganized) subtype; negative
  symptoms; cognitive impairment; **absence of affective symptoms**; poor insight. *Other:* high expressed emotion in the family; poor adherence.
  Oxford's qualifier: "their modest predictive value is emphasized, and caution should always be exercised when asked to predict the outcome of
  individual cases."
- **Mortality:** all-cause RR historically 1.6, now typically **threefold** with a **widening gap**; men die ~**20 years** and women ~**15 years**
  prematurely; **~60% of excess early mortality is from unnatural causes** (suicide, accidents), the rest mainly cardiovascular; moderate antipsychotic
  use is associated with the **lowest** mortality, and even high doses with lower mortality than **no** treatment (Torniainen 2015).
- **Suicide:** "often quoted as 10% or more, but recent meta-analyses suggest a figure of about **5%**"; greatest **early** in the illness, with affective
  symptoms, prior attempts, recent discharge and number of admissions. Patients with pronounced **negative** symptoms are **less** likely to attempt
  suicide; suicide more often occurs in previously high-achieving patients who are relatively well but demoralised (p. 296).
- **Environment:** Wing & Brown 1970 — **poverty of the social milieu** correlates with **social withdrawal, blunting of affect and poverty of speech**.
  **High expressed emotion** (criticism, hostility, emotional overinvolvement) raises relapse, worst with contact >35 h/week; **92% relapse** with high
  EE + high contact + no medication (Vaughn & Leff 1976); family behavioural programmes reduce this (Leff 1985; Thorn Course).

### Treatment (286–291)
- **Guidelines as Oxford frames them:** NICE 2014a (short on detail, criticised for overemphasising psychological interventions and CBT); SIGN 2013
  (more medication-, TRS- and perinatal-focused); **Galletly 2016 Australasian** (most detailed; first-episode algorithm).
- **Efficacy:** median effect size vs placebo **0.44**; **about two-thirds** respond; **no clinically useful predictors** of individual response;
  antipsychotics treat **positive symptoms only**, with little or no clinically significant effect on negative or cognitive symptoms. **No substantial
  efficacy differences between antipsychotics except clozapine**, and none between typicals and atypicals — differences lie in side-effect profile
  (Table 11.7 ranks 15 drugs on efficacy, weight gain, EPS, prolactin, QTc and sedation).
- **Onset of action is NOT delayed** — improvement detectable within 24 hours; **if there has not been a 20% improvement in symptom score at 2 weeks,
  later response to that drug and dose is unlikely** (Samara 2016). Use the **lowest effective dose**, which is **lower in the first episode**.
- **Box 11.17, acute:** start at the lower end of the licensed range; **no loading doses ("rapid neuroleptization")** — use adjunctive benzodiazepines
  if sedation is needed; choose by patient preference, previous response and liability to serious side effects (especially extrapyramidal and
  metabolic); titrate within the licensed range; aim for an optimum dose with good adherence for **2 weeks**, then consider changing drug if no
  response; record indications, anticipated benefits, time course and discussions; consider psychological interventions whenever medication is
  introduced or changed.
- **Box 11.17, maintenance:** continue **a year, or longer**; ensure **dose, duration and adherence** are adequate **before switching**; withdraw
  **gradually** with mental-state monitoring; **continuous beats intermittent**; monitor adherence and side effects (including metabolic syndrome)
  regularly; consider depots where adherence is a problem; always pair with psychological interventions.
- **Relapse prevention:** 1-year relapse **65% off vs 27% on** medication, **NNT 3**; after a **first** episode, recurrence **77% off vs 3% on**
  (Zipursky 2014). Duration disagreement recorded explicitly: **NICE 1–2 years vs Galletly 2–5 years**.
- **Adherence:** non-adherence **up to 52%**, related to poor insight and negative attitudes to medication; **once-daily dosing gives best adherence**.
  **LAIs/depots:** relative risk reduction ~**30%**; used in ~**30%** of UK patients. **Box 11.21:** small test dose at the start (**not considered
  necessary for atypical preparations**); long half-lives mean several weeks to peak plasma levels, affecting titration and oral tapering;
  **olanzapine pamoate post-injection sedation/confusion/coma in about 2%**, recovery in 24–72 hours, requiring **≥3 hours of observation**.
- **Clozapine:** the **only proven drug intervention** for non-response/intolerance, effective in **about one-third** of such patients (Kane 1988);
  "no evidence that any other atypical antipsychotic shares this greater efficacy"; also credited with benefit for **suicide risk, aggression and
  substance misuse**, while "earlier suggestions that it might improve persistent **negative** symptoms have not been borne out". Adverse effects named
  in Ch. 11: **agranulocytosis** (driving the blood-monitoring burden), **weight gain, sedation, hypersalivation**, plus unspecified "rare but serious
  adverse events". It is "**the only antipsychotic for which it is often worth measuring plasma levels**" — 2017 figures: **minimum trough 200 µg/L**,
  higher **up to 500 µg/L or more** may increase response "but at the cost of more side effects". **Samara 2016** (blinded trials only) found clozapine
  superior to haloperidol and chlorpromazine but **not** to olanzapine or risperidone; Oxford leaves this unresolved.
- **Other agents:** antidepressants give clear but modest benefit against **depressive and negative** symptoms, especially SSRIs; mood stabilisers
  unproven, lithium benefit confined to **affective symptoms or schizoaffective disorder**; **benzodiazepines have no augmentation role but are useful
  acutely in rapid tranquillization**. **ECT** — traditional indications **catatonic stupor, severe comorbid depression, severe behavioural
  disturbance**; impression dramatic, data limited; possible role augmenting clozapine.
- **Psychosocial (Box 11.18):** family therapy/psychoeducation, CBT, cognitive remediation, art therapy, social skills training, illness-management
  skills, supported employment, integrated treatment for comorbid substance misuse; shared aims are enhancing interpersonal and social functioning
  including independent living, and attenuating symptom severity and comorbidity. **Family intervention lowers relapse (NNT 7) and improves compliance
  (NNT 6)**; **Box 11.19 elements:** education, improving communication, lowering expressed emotion, expanding social networks, adjusting expectations,
  reducing hours of daily contact. **Cognitive remediation** has good evidence for cognitive **and** functional gains; **exercise** improves symptoms,
  quality of life and functioning and counters weight gain. Oxford is sceptical about **CBT** (Jauhar 2014 — "only a very small effect"), arts
  therapies, social skills training, dynamic psychotherapy (overstimulation/relapse risk) and adherence therapy.

### Management (291–297)
- **Box 11.20 components:** therapeutic partnership with patient and carers; **early intervention for the first episode**; integrated multidisciplinary
  working across primary and secondary care; antipsychotics for treatment and maintenance with attention to side effects and adherence; **trial of
  clozapine for all patients who meet the criteria**; CBT; family interventions; cognitive remediation; assertive outreach; regular needs assessment;
  crisis resolution and home treatment as alternatives to admission; **maintain realistic therapeutic optimism**.
- **Acute illness (291–293):** admission usually needed for a first episode and severe relapses, not infrequently under the Mental Health Act — giving
  thorough assessment, a safe environment and family respite; home treatment possible for less severe episodes with adequate resources. **A drug-free
  observation period is "a counsel of perfection" and is rarely attempted now.** The main diagnostic difficulty is eliciting symptoms from a withdrawn
  or suspicious patient — **several interviews, informant history, nursing observation**. **Physical examination and routine investigations as soon as
  the mental state permits, for two stated reasons:** to exclude organic or drug-induced psychosis, and to detect medical comorbidity and provide
  **baseline data for metabolic monitoring**. **Metabolic syndrome is present in about 10% of unmedicated first-episode patients.** **Social assessment**
  in parallel (personality, functioning, work record, accommodation, leisure, relatives' attitudes) with OT/social work/nursing input.
  **Consent and family involvement ideally before initiating antipsychotics**; MHA safeguards if capacity is lacking.
- **Acute drug practice (292–293):** rapid tranquillization if acutely disturbed (deferred to Ch. 25); otherwise low-to-moderate oral doses.
  First-episode worked examples: **risperidone 1–4 mg/day, amisulpride 100–400 mg/day, aripiprazole 5–20 mg/day**; olanzapine "suggested to be avoided
  as first-line … because of its greater propensity to cause metabolic syndrome … however, it is widely used, and its sedative properties can be
  valuable". **Alert for acute dystonia early and parkinsonism later**; monitor temperature and BP in the elderly or physically ill. Improvement is
  gradual over weeks; **do not escalate dose to speed it** — for continuing agitation a short-term **benzodiazepine is better than increasing the
  antipsychotic**. Syrup/orodispersible tablets if swallowing is doubted; short-acting IM (zuclopenthixol acetate, olanzapine) **not recommended where
  antipsychotic tolerance is undetermined**; **anticholinergics only if EPS are troublesome, never routinely**.
- **After the acute episode (293–294):** maintenance normally **at least 1–2 years, longer after a second or subsequent episode**, within an integrated
  package (structured activity, education or work, continued CBT, regular community follow-up). **Care Programme Approach** plan: systematic needs
  assessment; treatment plan agreed by staff, patient and relatives; allocated **key worker**; regular review. Chronic-phase checklist: accommodation;
  occupation; activities of daily living; cognitive remediation/social skills training; comorbidity and substance misuse; physical health; medication
  optimisation "recognizing that any further response beyond that achieved earlier in the illness is unlikely"; carer support and psychoeducation
  (which reduce carer distress); **assertive outreach** for those at continuing relapse risk who disengage.
- **Failure to regain premorbid function despite resolved positive symptoms (p. 294):** "it is important to consider the cause. For example, it may be
  due to **negative symptoms, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairments, or the side effects of medication**. If a cause is identified, appropriate
  action should be taken; this may include interventions for depression, a change in medication, or referral for psychological treatment."
- **TRS (294–295):** "About **one in three** patients … also known as refractory schizophrenia"; consistent with "at least **30%** of patients do not
  respond to antipsychotics, or are intolerant of them". **Threshold:** clozapine "should be recommended to **all patients who do not respond to or
  cannot tolerate at least two other antipsychotics**". **Pseudoresistance:** "it is also worth trying to establish that the patient is refractory to
  treatment and not simply non-adherent; a recent study found that **half of patients considered treatment-resistant had low or undetectable
  antipsychotic plasma levels**" (McCutcheon 2015). Only a minority of suitable patients get a trial, often **many years** into the illness.
  **Box 11.22 assessment:** review diagnosis (e.g. organic psychosis); review comorbidities (substance misuse, physical health); review treatment
  history (drugs, doses, psychological therapies); rate symptoms with established scales (**BPRS, PANSS, CGI**); physical examination (e.g. metabolic
  syndrome); discuss the patient's views; check adherence (drug levels); consider routine bloods and further investigations (e.g. imaging).
  Beyond clozapine: add a high-D2-affinity antipsychotic such as **amisulpride** (or aripiprazole) — **"the only situation that justifies prescribing
  two antipsychotics at once"** — or a mood stabiliser, **especially lamotrigine**; ECT occasionally augments clozapine. But "overall there is limited
  evidence for any clinically or statistically significant clozapine augmentation strategy, nor for how to deal with treatment resistance if clozapine
  cannot be used."
- **Early intervention (295):** the consensus rests on three stated observations — **longer DUP predicts poorer prognosis**; **treatment response becomes
  slower and less frequent in subsequent episodes**; and **much of the deterioration in social function occurs in the first 2 years after diagnosis**.
  EIS teams were mandated in the UK 2000 NHS Plan and endorsed by NICE, **but** "the evidence that they are effective remains limited and actively
  debated", with **no convincing evidence that early intervention reduces DUP**.
- **Other (296–297):** substance misuse is "now more the rule than the exception", with worse course and poorer engagement. Violence: 10.7% of male and
  2.7% of female patients convicted of a violent offence within 5 years of diagnosis, comorbid substance misuse the main factor and risk reduced by
  medication — but **patients are much more at risk of being victims than perpetrators**; manage with a **calm, reassuring, consistent environment in
  which provocation is avoided**. Communication: stigma; explain aetiology to prevent parental guilt; use Table 11.4 for heritability questions "while
  emphasizing the probability that offspring and unaffected relatives will remain well"; portray recovery **realistically but optimistically**; the
  **Recovery Approach** criticises psychiatric pessimism.

### Q11 — First-episode psychosis (incl. Q11a cannabis)
**Spine.** (1) Safety and setting — admission usual for a first episode, often under the MHA. (2) Diagnosis built over repeated interviews plus
informant history and nursing observation. (3) Physical examination and baseline investigations for Oxford's two stated reasons. (4) Parallel social
and multidisciplinary assessment. (5) Consent, explanation and family involvement before medication. (6) Low-dose single oral antipsychotic;
benzodiazepine, not dose escalation, for agitation. (7) Early-intervention/DUP framing with Oxford's own scepticism attached.
**Keepers.** First-episode dose examples and the olanzapine caveat; metabolic syndrome ~10% pre-treatment; "counsel of perfection"; the three
early-intervention observations. **Q11a** turns on one sentence (p. 276): psychosis directly caused or precipitated by substance use "is diagnosed as
such, and not as schizophrenia" — supported by the cannabis epidemiology and the Q12-packet caveat that the distinction is often not cleanly possible.
**Verify currently.** (i) Whether olanzapine-avoidance in first episode still holds. (ii) The baseline investigation panel — Oxford names none.
(iii) Current first-episode dose ranges. (iv) Whether guidelines still back EIS. (v) Current substance-induced vs primary psychosis criteria.
**Boundaries.** Differential diagnosis and organic causes are Q12-packet material; maintenance and psychosocial packages are Q17; no TRS/clozapine.

### Q13 — Schizophrenia vs schizoaffective vs mood disorder with psychotic features
**Spine.** The distinction is longitudinal and the Q12 packet holds the operational rules. Only four later-chapter items sharpen it: **no independent
familial transmission** of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (spectrum extending to schizoaffective disorder and schizotypal/paranoid PD); the
**CNV asymmetry** Oxford calls a possible point of aetiological separation; **absence of affective symptoms as a poor-prognosis factor**, so affective
features point prognostically away from a pure schizophrenic course; and **lithium's benefit being confined to affective/schizoaffective presentations**.
**Verify currently.** (i) ICD-11 schizoaffective requirements — no local ICD-11 text, already an open Q12 item. (ii) Whether "no independent
transmission" still stands.
**Boundaries.** Diagnosis pages were not re-read and did not need to be; the Q12 packet remains primary for the DSM-5 2-week rule, the ICD-10
"equally prominent and simultaneous" rule, postschizophrenic depression, and the absence of genetic/neurobiological separation.

### Q14 — Neurobiology
**Spine.** (1) Multi-level, polygenic, gene–environment disorder with **no single explanatory model**. (2) Genetics — high heritability, polygenic,
three variant classes, no necessary or sufficient gene. (3) Environment concentrated prenatally/perinatally plus urbanicity, migration, trauma,
cannabis. (4) Structure and neuropathology consistent with a **neurodevelopmental** origin (no gliosis, no neurodegeneration). (5) Dopamine as the
best-established, therapeutically anchored abnormality; glutamate/NMDA hypofunction as the candidate upstream trait abnormality. (6) Circuit level —
hypofrontality, dysconnectivity, aberrant salience. (7) Limitation — findings small, overlapping, not diagnostically specific, and the
neurodevelopmental model's unfalsifiability is itself its weakness.
**Keepers.** Table 11.4 risk ladder (also Oxford's family-counselling material, p. 297); 22q11/VCFS; Johnstone 1976 CT study; PCP/ketamine model;
Kapur; Crow's lateralization hypothesis ("ingenious but not widely supported"); and the three load-bearing facts — heritability 81% with no necessary
gene, associative-striatal dopamine that predicts response and remits, glutamate trait vs dopamine state.
**Verify currently.** (i) GWAS locus count (108 is 2014). (ii) Inflammation/microglia findings, which Oxford says await replication. (iii) Cortical
dopamine deficit and NMDA-antibody findings. (iv) Progressive volume loss vs antipsychotic effect.
**Boundaries.** Not extracted: Table 11.5 CNV detail, DISC1, C4 mechanism, P50 genetics, epigenetics, GABA/5-HT specifics. Environmental risks are
shared with Q15 and Q11 — state each once.

### Q15 — Prognosis
**Spine.** (1) Outcome heterogeneous and individually unpredictable — Oxford says so twice. (2) Box 11.16 factors in Oxford's three groups.
(3) Recovery figures depend entirely on the definition (one-sixth vs ~14%). (4) Social outcome lags symptomatic outcome. (5) Mortality and suicide.
(6) Modifiable levers — DUP, adherence, substance misuse, expressed emotion, under/over-stimulation.
**Keepers.** M. Bleuler's "recovery usually within the first 2 years" against Harrison's late recovery in a significant minority; AESOP 23%/45%;
the IPSS developing-country advantage; poverty of social milieu; Vaughn & Leff's 92%; suicide ~5% not 10%; negative symptoms associated with *fewer*
suicide attempts; the 65%/27% and 77%/3% relapse figures used here as prognosis.
**Verify currently.** (i) Suicide-risk estimate. (ii) Mortality-gap data. (iii) Whether the developing-country advantage survives modern cohorts.
(iv) Whether DUP remains an independent predictor after adjustment.
**Boundaries.** No long epidemiology tables; incidence/prevalence/age-of-onset anchors are in the Q12 packet; physical-health *monitoring* is Q17.

### Q16 — Acute treatment
**Spine.** (1) Setting and safety. (2) Complete the assessment while treating. (3) Consent, explanation, family involvement. (4) One antipsychotic at
the low end of the licensed range, chosen on side-effect profile and patient preference, **no loading doses**. (5) Agitation managed with adjunctive
benzodiazepines, not dose escalation. (6) Response defined prospectively — the 2-week/20% checkpoint before switching. (7) Environmental and
psychological measures in parallel.
**Keepers.** "Rapid neuroleptization" as a named error; the 2-week/20% rule; effect size 0.44; two-thirds response; positive-symptom-only efficacy;
benzodiazepines useful acutely but useless as augmentation; the practical monitoring points; ECT for catatonic stupor, severe comorbid depression and
severe behavioural disturbance; the patient is "unlikely to be able to engage fruitfully in specific psychological therapies at this stage".
**Verify currently.** (i) Rapid-tranquillization protocol — Oxford defers entirely to Ch. 25. (ii) Whether 2 weeks is still the switch point.
(iii) Metabolic monitoring schedule. (iv) Whether the anticholinergic and short-acting IM cautions are unchanged. (v) Table 11.7's rankings (2013).
**Boundaries.** Clozapine appears only as the exception to "no efficacy differences"; maintenance, LAIs and psychosocial packages are Q17.

### Q17 — Long-term management (incl. Q17a)
**Spine.** (1) Maintenance antipsychotic treatment as the strongest relapse-prevention measure, quantified. (2) Duration is guideline-dependent and
Oxford flags the disagreement. (3) Adherence is the bottleneck; LAIs the structural answer. (4) Psychosocial interventions — family work first, then
cognitive remediation, CBT, supported employment, exercise. (5) Physical-health monitoring as a core psychiatric responsibility. (6) Negative and
cognitive symptoms drive functional outcome and are poorly served by drugs. (7) Care coordination, realistic therapeutic optimism, Recovery Approach.
**Q17a.** Oxford's differential is assembled from two places: p. 294 names **negative symptoms, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment and medication
side effects** as the causes to consider when a patient fails to regain premorbid function despite resolved positive symptoms, each with a matching
action; the fifth mimic, **environmental deprivation/understimulation**, comes from Wing & Brown (p. 285) and the Q12 packet's understimulation rule
(p. 259). Add that antipsychotics have **little or no effect on negative symptoms**, so persistence is expected rather than failure.
**Keepers.** 65%/27% NNT 3; 77%/3% after a first episode; LAI RRR ~30% and ~30% UK usage; non-adherence up to 52% with once-daily best; family
intervention NNT 7 relapse / NNT 6 compliance and Box 11.19's six elements; the NICE-vs-Australasian duration split.
**Verify currently.** (i) Maintenance duration after first and subsequent episodes. (ii) Physical-health monitoring schedule and targets. (iii) LAI
practice — test doses, post-injection observation. (iv) Status of CBT for psychosis given Oxford's scepticism. (v) Whether any agent is now recommended
for primary negative symptoms (Oxford's cariprazine note is a single 2017 trial).
**Boundaries.** Relapse statistics shared with Q15 — prognosis there, intervention here; TRS/clozapine excluded; the Q12 packet supplies the
negative-vs-depression-vs-parkinsonism caution and the Calgary Depression Scale.

### Q18 — Treatment-resistant schizophrenia
