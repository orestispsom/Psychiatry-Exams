# Q11–Q19 Current Verification Delta

Scope: targeted current verification only. This file updates the vulnerable points identified in `Q011-Q019-current-verification.md`; it does not reconstruct the questions or replace the Oxford/base-source map.

## A. Q11 + Q13

| Question | Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q11 | First-episode psychosis is not automatically schizophrenia | KEEP | A first episode/presentation of psychosis is a clinical state requiring longitudinal diagnosis. Current care pathways assess psychosis without presuming schizophrenia and retain affective, substance/medication-induced and medical/neurological alternatives until course and exclusions are established. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.1–1.3.4; Q12 current packet |
| Q11 | Substance/medication-induced versus primary psychosis | UPDATE | ICD-11 substance-induced psychotic disorders require a plausible temporal relationship to intoxication or withdrawal and symptoms beyond ordinary intoxication/withdrawal phenomena. A primary disorder is favoured when psychosis predates the substance exposure, persists for a substantial period after cessation/withdrawal, or there is evidence of prior independent psychotic episodes. Causation should not be inferred from a positive toxicology result alone. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR (2024); Reed et al. 2019 ICD-11 innovations; Bramness & Rognli 2024 ICD-11 commentary |
| Q11a | Cannabis-specific causal attribution and observation period | UPDATE | Cannabis-induced psychosis may arise during or soon after intoxication **or withdrawal**. The clinically useful discriminators are timing of heavy/relevant exposure, whether the exposure could plausibly produce psychosis, persistence despite abstinence, psychotic symptoms before cannabis use, prior non-cannabis psychotic episodes, collateral history and the wider longitudinal phenotype. Current ICD-11 wording uses persistence for a **“substantial period”** after cessation as evidence against a cannabis-induced diagnosis; it does not provide a universal fixed observation period that should be applied mechanically. | WHO ICD-11 CDDR (2024); current ICD-11 cannabis-induced psychotic disorder description as discussed in Bramness & Rognli 2024 |
| Q11 | Routine neuroimaging | KEEP | Routine structural neuroimaging is **not** recommended for every uncomplicated first episode of psychosis. History, examination and investigations should be directed toward identifying clinically suspected secondary causes; imaging is indicated when the presentation/examination gives a neurological or other organic reason. | NICE CG178 §1.3.3.2 and comprehensive assessment §1.3.3 |
| Q11 | High-value early-management update | UPDATE | Oxford’s implication that first-episode admission is usually required should not be universalised. Current NICE care prioritises **early-intervention-in-psychosis access without delay**, full multidisciplinary assessment, and crisis/home treatment where safe; admission depends on risk, severity and clinical need. First-episode treatment should combine oral antipsychotic medication with CBT and family intervention rather than treating psychosocial work as a later-phase add-on. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.1, 1.3.4 and 1.4.1 |
| Q13 | ICD-11 schizophrenia versus schizoaffective versus mood disorder with psychotic symptoms | UPDATE | ICD-11 schizoaffective disorder is **episode-based**: the diagnostic requirements for schizophrenia and for a moderate/severe depressive, manic or mixed episode are met concurrently for at least 1 month, with psychotic and mood syndromes beginning simultaneously or within a few days. Because ICD-11 focuses on the current episode, diagnosis can change across episodes. Mood disorder with psychotic symptoms remains appropriate when the psychotic presentation does not meet the full schizophrenia requirement during the mood episode. | First et al., *World Psychiatry* 2021 (ICD-11/DSM comparison) |
| Q13 | Must psychosis occur outside a mood episode? | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **Not in ICD-11.** ICD-11 does not require a period of psychosis in the absence of mood symptoms. **DSM-5/DSM-5-TR uses a different lifetime/longitudinal model:** schizoaffective disorder requires at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode at some point, while major mood episodes occupy the majority of the illness. Therefore the classic “2 weeks of psychosis without mood symptoms” rule is DSM-specific, not a universal schizoaffective criterion. | First et al., *World Psychiatry* 2021; APA DSM-5-TR update status already checked in Q12 packet |
| Q13 | Duration/longitudinal discriminator | UPDATE | Do not blend systems. ICD-11 schizoaffective disorder is a ≥1-month concurrent episode diagnosis; schizophrenia likewise has a 1-month symptom-duration threshold. DSM schizophrenia uses a ≥6-month overall disturbance framework and DSM schizoaffective diagnosis explicitly depends on lifetime mood–psychosis chronology. These differences can yield different diagnoses for the same longitudinal history. | First et al., *World Psychiatry* 2021; Q12 current packet |

## B. Q14–Q17

| Question | Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q14 | Polygenic architecture and gene–environment framing | UPDATE | The polygenic conclusion is stronger than in 2017: modern genomic work implicates **nearly 300 common variants and >20 rare variants**, reinforcing extensive polygenicity rather than single-gene causation. Genetic and environmental liabilities are both established; specific mechanistic gene–environment interactions should be described more cautiously than a settled unitary mechanism. | Sullivan, Yao & Hjerling-Leffler, *Nat Rev Neurosci* 2024 |
| Q14 | Neurodevelopmental model | KEEP | Schizophrenia remains appropriately framed as substantially neurodevelopmental: genetic findings converge on developmental/synaptic biology and clinical/cognitive abnormalities can precede first psychosis. This is a high-level organizing model, not a complete explanation of every case or course pattern. | Sullivan et al. 2024; Howes et al., *Nat Rev Neurol* 2024 |
| Q14 | Dopamine abnormality | UPDATE | Increased striatal dopamine synthesis/release remains one of the most robust group-level biological findings, particularly relevant to psychosis. Oxford’s stronger wording should be softened: it is **not universal**, varies by illness/treatment-resistance subgroup, and is not a diagnostic biomarker; claims that it simply disappears in remission or reliably predicts individual treatment response are too categorical for a board answer. | Howes, Bukala & Beck, *Nat Rev Neurol* 2024 |
| Q14 | Glutamate/NMDA hypothesis | UPDATE | Glutamatergic abnormalities and NMDA-hypofunction models remain well supported research frameworks, with converging pharmacological and imaging evidence, but they should not be presented as an established singular “primary trait” mechanism with dopamine merely downstream. Current models emphasize interacting transmitter and circuit abnormalities. | Howes et al., *Nat Rev Neurol* 2024 |
| Q14 | Structural/functional connectivity findings | KEEP | Structural, functional and connectivity abnormalities are reproducible at group level but heterogeneous, overlapping with controls and other disorders, and **not clinically diagnostic**. No MRI/fMRI pattern currently establishes schizophrenia in an individual patient. | Howes et al. 2024 |
| Q14 | Environmental exposures | KEEP | Cannabis use, migration/ethnic-minority status, urbanicity, childhood adversity and obstetric/developmental exposures remain replicated **risk associations**, with varying strength and generalisability. They are probabilistic and non-deterministic; observational association should not be converted into a simple individual causal statement. | Christou et al., *Epidemiol Psychiatr Sci* 2025 mapping review |
| Q15 | Robust prognostic factors | UPDATE | Retain a restrained set rather than Oxford’s long classic list. A 2023 meta-analysis found lower symptomatic remission associated with male sex, longer DUP, greater baseline symptom burden, poorer global functioning, more previous admissions and poorer adherence; **baseline functioning** was the strongest investigated predictor. Evidence was limited or absent for several traditional predictors, including age at onset and depressive symptoms. | Schnack et al., *Schizophrenia Research* 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis |
| Q15 | Duration of untreated psychosis | KEEP | Longer DUP remains associated with lower remission and poorer functional outcome, but it is safest to call DUP a **prognostic association**, not a proven independent causal determinant. Recent meta-analysis explicitly notes uncertainty about whether longer DUP contributes causally or partly marks a poorer-course phenotype/pathway to care. | Franco-Rubio et al. 2024 overview of meta-analyses; 2024/25 FEP DUP meta-analysis |
| Q15 | Symptomatic versus functional recovery | KEEP | Maintain the distinction. Symptomatic remission and functional recovery are separable outcomes; social/occupational functioning may remain impaired despite symptom reduction. Recovery percentages are highly definition- and follow-up-dependent, so Oxford’s single ~14% figure should not be used as a timeless board fact. | Franco-Rubio et al., *Schizophrenia Research* 2024 |
| Q15 | Mortality and suicide framing | UPDATE | Retain the qualitative message but modernise the numbers. Large meta-analysis found approximately **2.5-fold all-cause mortality versus mixed non-schizophrenia controls and about 2.9-fold versus the general population**, with 15–20 years of premature mortality and particularly high relative mortality from suicide and other non-natural causes. For the viva, “marked excess mortality from both natural disease and suicide/non-natural causes” is more robust than a fixed lifetime suicide percentage. | Correll et al., *World Psychiatry* 2022, 135 studies/4.5 million patients |
| Q15 | Sex/age-of-onset pattern as prognosis fact | UPDATE | Male sex retains some association with lower symptomatic remission, but **younger age at onset should not be treated as a strong independent prognosis rule**; recent meta-analysis found limited/no evidence for age at onset as a short-to-medium-term outcome predictor. Keep sex/age-of-onset epidemiology separate from prognosis. | Schnack et al. 2023 |
| Q16 | Initial antipsychotic choice/shared decision-making | KEEP | Choice should be made jointly with the patient, considering prior response where relevant and individual metabolic, extrapyramidal, cardiovascular/QT, prolactin/hormonal and subjective adverse-effect priorities. There is no universal “best” first antipsychotic for all patients. | NICE CG178 §1.3.5 |
| Q16 | Oral antipsychotic trial principles | UPDATE | Treat the antipsychotic as an explicit therapeutic trial: start at the **lower end of the licensed range**, titrate gradually, record target symptoms/adverse effects, and—if tolerated—assess an optimum-dose trial over **4–6 weeks**. Avoid loading/rapid neuroleptisation and routine antipsychotic polypharmacy. | NICE CG178 §1.3.6 |
| Q16 | Oxford 2-week/20% switching rule | UPDATE | Early improvement by 2 weeks has prognostic value, but a hard rule that lack of ≥20% improvement at 2 weeks should trigger switching is **too simplistic** for the final answer. Current NICE guidance uses a 4–6-week optimum-dose trial unless adverse effects, deterioration or another clinical reason requires earlier change. | NICE CG178 §1.3.6.3 |
| Q16 | Benzodiazepines / rapid tranquillisation | KEEP | Benzodiazepines are not routine augmentation for schizophrenia. Their role in Q16 is limited to short-term management of severe agitation/rapid tranquillisation according to an emergency protocol; escalating the maintenance antipsychotic solely to sedate an agitated patient is poor practice. | NICE CG178 plus NICE NG10 rapid-tranquillisation framework |
| Q16 | Psychosocial intervention in the acute phase | UPDATE | Oxford’s suggestion that acute patients are generally unlikely to engage fruitfully in specific psychological treatment is too broad. NICE recommends oral antipsychotic **with CBT and family intervention** for first episode and acute recurrence, and states psychological interventions can begin in the acute phase or later according to ability/need. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.4 and 1.4.2 |
| Q16 | First-episode dosing / polypharmacy | KEEP | Preserve “start low and titrate,” but avoid memorising Oxford’s old worked dose examples unless separately verified from each SPC. NICE starts at the lower end of the licensed range and explicitly advises against routine combined antipsychotics except briefly during switching. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.6.3, 1.3.6.10 |
| Q17 | Maintenance after first episode | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Current guidance does **not** support one universal duration. WHO’s 2023 update recommends maintenance antipsychotic treatment for a **minimum 7–12 months** after remission in adults after a first psychotic episode, balancing efficacy, adverse effects and preference. NICE warns of a high relapse risk if medication is stopped in the next **1–2 years**, recommends gradual withdrawal and ≥2 years of monitoring after withdrawal. The writer should present shared decision-making and relapse risk rather than a single dogmatic number. | WHO mhGAP 2023 update; NICE CG178 §1.4.6 |
| Q17 | Maintenance after recurrent illness | KEEP | Continued maintenance is generally favoured after response, with duration individualised according to number/severity of episodes, relapse consequences, residual symptoms, adherence, adverse effects and patient preference. APA recommends continuing antipsychotic medication after improvement; NICE rejects routine intermittent/targeted-only maintenance. | APA Schizophrenia Guideline 2020; NICE CG178 §1.5.5 |
| Q17 | LAI role | UPDATE | LAIs are **not only an adherence-enforcement tool**. They should be considered when the patient prefers an injectable formulation **or** when poor/covert/uncertain adherence is a clinical priority. Drug-specific initiation/test-dose/oral-overlap requirements come from the relevant SPC rather than a generic LAI rule. | NICE CG178 §§1.5.5.3–1.5.6; APA 2020 statement 10 |
| Q17 | Physical/metabolic monitoring | UPDATE | Baseline: weight, waist, pulse/BP, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement-disorder assessment, nutrition/activity; ECG when SPC or cardiovascular/inpatient indications apply. During treatment: weight weekly first 6 weeks, then 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; waist annually; BP/pulse, glucose/HbA1c and lipids at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually, plus systematic adverse-effect/adherence/overall-health review. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.6.1–1.3.6.5 |
| Q17 | Psychosocial interventions | UPDATE | Oxford’s sceptical CBT framing should not drive the current answer. Current major guidelines support **CBT for psychosis, family intervention/psychoeducation**, coordinated specialty/early psychosis care, and supported employment for people seeking work. These are components of long-term care, not optional decoration after medication. | NICE CG178 §§1.3.7, 1.5.8; APA 2020 statements 15–17 |
| Q17 | Persistent negative/cognitive symptoms | UPDATE | Do not label persistent withdrawal/inactivity as antipsychotic non-response alone. First identify secondary contributors and functional needs; rehabilitation, psychosocial intervention and treatment of specific secondary causes are central. Cognitive impairment is clinically important but no routine medication is established as a specific cognitive treatment. Avoid the categorical Oxford phrase that antipsychotics treat “positive symptoms only”; effects on other domains are modest/heterogeneous rather than literally absent. | EPA negative-symptom guidance 2021 and cognitive guidance 2022, already verified in Q12 packet |
| Q17a | Secondary causes of negative-looking symptoms | KEEP | Before calling symptoms primary/persistent negative symptoms, actively assess residual positive symptoms, depression, antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism/EPS, sedation or other medication effects, substance use, cognitive impairment and environmental deprivation/understimulation. | EPA Guidance on Assessment of Negative Symptoms 2021; Q12 current packet |

## C. Q18 + Q19

| Question | Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q18 | Operational TRS definition | UPDATE | For a practical board definition: persistent clinically significant schizophrenia symptoms despite **at least two adequate antipsychotic trials**, after excluding pseudoresistance. TRRIP operationalises this more tightly for research/clinical translation with at least moderate symptoms and functional impairment persisting ≥12 weeks across the adequate trials. | TRRIP Working Group, *Am J Psychiatry* 2017; NICE CG178 §1.5.7 |
| Q18 | Adequate antipsychotic trial | ADD | TRRIP’s minimum benchmark is **≥6 weeks at a therapeutic dose for each trial**. Dose should ordinarily be the target/midpoint of the licensed acute schizophrenia range; TRRIP uses ≥600 mg chlorpromazine equivalents/day only when an appropriate target dose cannot otherwise be established. This is an operational consensus benchmark, not a mandate to force every patient to 600-mg-equivalent dosing despite tolerability. | TRRIP 2017 §5 and Table 2 |
| Q18 | Number of failed trials before clozapine | KEEP | Two sequential adequate failed trials remain the mainstream threshold. NICE specifies at least **2 different antipsychotics**, at least one a non-clozapine second-generation antipsychotic; APA recommends clozapine for TRS. Delay through repeated non-clozapine trials after genuine TRS is established is not supported. | NICE CG178 §1.5.7.2; APA 2020 statement 7 |
| Q18 | Pseudoresistance | UPDATE | Before TRS, reassess diagnosis, target symptoms, dose/duration, adherence/exposure, substance use, concurrent medicines/pharmacokinetic factors, medical illness and whether appropriate psychosocial treatment has been delivered. Non-adherence is a major source of false TRS classification. | NICE CG178 §1.5.7.1; TRRIP 2017 |
| Q18 | Exposure/adherence verification | ADD | TRRIP requires systematic adherence assessment (minimum benchmark ≥80% of prescribed doses over the trial period) and recommends antipsychotic blood levels at least once for oral therapy when feasible; an LAI/directly observed exposure is an optimal way to reduce covert non-adherence uncertainty. **Plasma-level confirmation is therefore strongly useful when available, but is not a universal regulatory prerequisite for diagnosing TRS.** | TRRIP 2017 Table 2 and §5.5 |
| Q19 | Current indications | UPDATE | **European product indication:** treatment-resistant schizophrenia and schizophrenia with severe, untreatable neurological adverse reactions to other antipsychotics; also psychosis in Parkinson disease after standard treatment failure. **Guideline indications extend clinical use:** APA recommends clozapine for TRS and persistent substantial suicide risk despite other treatment, and suggests it for persistent substantial aggression. Preserve the regulatory-versus-guideline distinction. | Current Clozaril SmPC §4.1; APA 2020 statements 7–9 |
| Q19 | EU/Greece haematological initiation threshold | UPDATE | The **2025 EU-wide revision** replaced WBC+ANC monitoring with **ANC-only** risk minimisation and lowered thresholds. Start clozapine at ANC ≥1.5 ×10⁹/L; in confirmed benign ethnic/Duffy-null neutropenia, ≥1.0 ×10⁹/L. The Greek EOF issued the EU safety communication on 8 Sep 2025. | EMA 2025 clozapine safety revision; EOF DHPC 8 Sep 2025; EU regulator implementation details |
| Q19 | EU continuation/stop framework | UPDATE | Under the revised EU framework, mild neutropenia ANC 1.0–1.5 ×10⁹/L can be managed with enhanced monitoring; once stabilised/resolved, monthly ANC continues. **ANC <1.0 ×10⁹/L requires immediate discontinuation and no re-exposure** under the general threshold; for confirmed BEN/Duffy-null neutropenia, the corresponding stop threshold is <0.5 ×10⁹/L. | EMA/PRAC 2025 recommendations; Danish Medicines Agency EU implementation notice 8 Sep 2025 |
| Q19 | Current routine ANC schedule | UPDATE | Revised EU schedule: **weekly for first 18 weeks; monthly for the next 34 weeks (to 1 year); every 12 weeks after year 1 if no neutropenia; annually after 2 years if no neutropenia in the first 2 years.** Infection symptoms require prompt clinical assessment and immediate ANC testing. This materially replaces the older lifelong-monthly European framing. | EMA annual report 2025; PRAC/European regulator 2025 revision; EOF 2025 notification |
| Q19 | Haematological monitoring after interruption | UPDATE | Keep this separate from dose re-titration. EU/PRAC clarification (2026): after ≥2 years of treatment with no prior neutropenia, resume the pre-interruption ANC schedule regardless of interruption length. If treatment was >18 weeks to 2 years, or there was prior neutropenia, interruptions 3 days to <4 weeks require weekly ANC for 6 weeks then ≤4-weekly; interruption ≥4 weeks requires weekly ANC for 18 weeks then monthly and re-titration. | PRAC signal outcome as clarified by Danish Medicines Agency, 9 Feb 2026 |
| Q19 | Pharmacological restart / re-titration | KEEP | Independently of blood-monitoring frequency, **if more than 2 days have elapsed since the last dose**, the current Clozaril SmPC requires restarting at **12.5 mg once or twice on day 1**, then re-titrating according to tolerability; faster re-titration than a completely new start may be possible in selected previously tolerant patients. This prevents severe hypotension/collapse from resuming the prior dose abruptly. | Current Clozaril SmPC §4.2 |
| Q19 | Myocarditis | ADD | Highest regulatory risk is during the **first 2 months**, especially early titration. Suspect myocarditis with otherwise unexplained persistent resting tachycardia, palpitations/arrhythmia, chest pain, dyspnoea/tachypnoea, fatigue/heart-failure features or flu-like illness; if suspected, stop clozapine promptly and obtain urgent cardiology assessment. International expert guidance supports baseline and weekly CRP/troponin during roughly the first 4 weeks as a useful screening strategy, but this is consensus guidance rather than one universally mandated EU laboratory schedule. | Current Clozaril SmPC §§4.4/4.8; TRRIP Clozapine Optimization Delphi 2023 |
| Q19 | Constipation / gastrointestinal hypomotility / ileus | ADD | Clozapine-related GI hypomotility is a major potentially fatal toxicity, ranging from constipation to faecal impaction, obstruction, paralytic ileus, megacolon and intestinal ischaemia/infarction. **Constipation must be actively asked about, recognised and treated**, with extra caution when anticholinergic drugs/opioids or prior bowel disease/surgery add risk. It deserves the same “serious adverse effects” prominence as haematological/cardiac toxicity in a viva answer. | Current Clozaril SmPC §4.4; CNS Drugs clozapine management guideline 2022 |
| Q19 | Seizures | KEEP | Clozapine lowers seizure threshold in a **dose-related** manner; risk rises with higher exposure/rapid dose escalation and pre-existing seizure vulnerability. If seizures occur, reduce dose and consider anticonvulsant treatment when indicated. Exact dose-band percentages are unnecessary for this oral answer. | Current Clozaril SmPC §§4.4/4.8 |
| Q19 | Metabolic, sedation and hypersalivation | KEEP | Weight gain/metabolic disturbance, sedation/drowsiness and hypersalivation remain common/high-yield adverse effects and should be monitored/managed, but they do not need a separate numerical board-fact burden here. | Current Clozaril SmPC §§4.4/4.8 |
| Q19 | Smoking / CYP1A2 | ADD | Cigarette smoke induces CYP1A2 and increases clozapine clearance; **abrupt smoking cessation can raise clozapine concentrations and toxicity**, whereas resumption can lower exposure and risk relapse. Current SmPC explicitly advises clozapine-level monitoring when smoking stops. Expert consensus suggests an initial dose reduction of roughly 25–33% after abrupt cessation in a stable heavy smoker, followed by clinical/TDM-guided individualisation; do not apply this percentage mechanically to every patient. | Current Clozaril SmPC §4.5 and blood-level advice; TRRIP Clozapine Optimization Delphi 2023 |
| Q19 | Infection/inflammation and pneumonia | ADD | Serious infection—especially pneumonia—can reduce CYP1A2 activity through inflammation and markedly raise clozapine exposure; clozapine itself also carries pneumonia/aspiration risks. During pneumonia/serious infection, urgently assess for toxicity, obtain ANC and a clozapine level when possible, review interacting drugs and reduce/hold clozapine according to severity. A widely used expert guideline recommends **approximately halving the dose during severe infection** until recovery, but the exact action remains clinical/TDM-guided rather than a universal regulatory formula. | Current Clozaril SmPC blood-level advice; Correll et al. CNS Drugs guideline 2022; TRRIP Delphi 2023 |
| Q19 | Plasma levels | UPDATE | Oxford’s 200/500 µg/L framing is not a good current board anchor. TDM is particularly useful for apparent non-response, adherence uncertainty, suspected toxicity, major smoking change, interacting medication, poor metabolism and pneumonia/serious infection. Before declaring clozapine non-response, TRRIP uses a **trough concentration ≥350 ng/mL** as an optimum exposure threshold. Dose and concentration must be interpreted with clinical response and tolerability; there is no single concentration that defines toxicity in every patient. | TRRIP 2017 §5.4; current Clozaril SmPC blood-level advice |
| Q19a | Pneumonia + abrupt smoking cessation | ADD | **Both changes push clozapine exposure upward at the same time**: inflammatory CYP1A2 suppression plus removal of smoke-induced CYP1A2 activity. Anticipate rapid toxicity—sedation, confusion/delirium, hypersalivation/aspiration, hypotension and seizures—and possible further pneumonia risk. Priorities: urgent physical assessment and infection treatment; check ANC; obtain clozapine level; review interacting antibiotics/medicines; promptly reduce or temporarily hold clozapine according to severity rather than continuing the pre-illness smoking dose; monitor closely and re-titrate/re-adjust after inflammation resolves and smoking status stabilises. | Current Clozaril SmPC §§4.4–4.5; TRRIP Delphi 2023; CNS Drugs clozapine guideline 2022 |

## Board-fact anchors resolved

- `bf.psychosis.duration_boundaries` → **Current:** DSM schizophrenia uses a ≥6-month overall disturbance framework; ICD-11 schizophrenia uses ≥1 month and has no direct schizophreniform equivalent. This anchor still requires its separate designated **exam-source** answer for publication. → Q12 current packet; First et al. 2021.
- `bf.psychosis.schizoaffective_mood_independence` → **Current split:** DSM requires ≥2 weeks of delusions/hallucinations without a major mood episode at some point; ICD-11 does **not**. ICD-11 requires concurrent schizophrenia + moderate/severe mood-episode requirements for ≥1 month, with near-simultaneous onset. → First et al. 2021.
- `bf.psychosis.fep_response_maintenance` → **Early response:** useful prognostically but not a hard 2-week switch command; NICE uses an optimum-dose antipsychotic trial of 4–6 weeks. **Maintenance after first episode:** WHO 2023 minimum 7–12 months after remission; NICE highlights high relapse risk if stopping in the first 1–2 years. → NICE CG178; WHO mhGAP 2023.
- `bf.psychosis.sex_onset_patterns` → Sex/age patterns are epidemiological, but younger age at onset should not be overpromoted as a robust independent prognosis predictor; recent prognosis meta-analysis found limited/no evidence for age at onset. → Schnack et al. 2023.
- `bf.psychosis.trs_adequate_trials` → ≥2 different adequate trials; TRRIP minimum operational benchmark: each ≥6 weeks at therapeutic dose, with systematic adherence/exposure assessment and pseudoresistance excluded. → TRRIP 2017; NICE CG178.
- `bf.clozapine.monitoring_thresholds` → 2025 EU revision: ANC-only; initiation ≥1.5×10⁹/L (confirmed BEN/Duffy-null ≥1.0); general stop/no-re-exposure threshold <1.0×10⁹/L (BEN/Duffy-null <0.5). → EMA/PRAC 2025; EOF DHPC 2025.
- `bf.clozapine.monitoring_schedule` → EU: weekly first 18 weeks → monthly next 34 weeks → q12 weeks after year 1 if no neutropenia → annually after 2 years if no neutropenia. → EMA/PRAC 2025 EU revision.
- `bf.clozapine.restart_interruption` → Dose safety: interruption **>2 days** requires restart at 12.5 mg once/twice on day 1 and re-titration. Haematological monitoring restart follows the separate 2025/26 EU ANC rules above. → current Clozaril SmPC; PRAC clarification 2026.
- `bf.clozapine.plasma_level_response` → Use TDM for apparent non-response/adherence uncertainty, toxicity, smoking change, interacting drugs and serious infection/pneumonia; TRRIP uses trough ≥350 ng/mL as optimum exposure before declaring clozapine non-response. → TRRIP 2017; current Clozaril SmPC.

## Exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q13 schizoaffective:** the DSM “2 weeks psychosis without mood episode” rule must not be presented as ICD-11. ICD-11 is an episode-based concurrent syndrome and can diagnose schizoaffective disorder even when psychosis occurs wholly within the mood episode if the full schizophrenia syndrome is concurrently met.
- **Q16 response timing:** Oxford’s 2-week/20% rule is useful as an early-response predictor, but current guideline practice should not turn it into a mandatory switch point; NICE evaluates an optimum-dose trial over 4–6 weeks when clinically tolerable.
- **Q17 maintenance duration:** Oxford’s 1–2 years is not the only current standard. WHO 2023 gives a minimum 7–12 months after first-episode remission; NICE emphasises high relapse risk with stopping within 1–2 years. State uncertainty and individualisation rather than inventing one universal duration.
- **Q17 LAIs:** older adherence-only framing is incomplete. Current NICE/APA explicitly include **patient preference** as an indication.
- **Q19 haematology:** any Oxford/legacy lifelong monthly European clozapine monitoring framework is materially outdated after the **2025 EMA/PRAC revision** and Greek EOF safety communication.

## Unresolved items

- None that block creation of the Q11–Q19 answer briefs.
- For eventual prescribing implementation in Greece, the clinician should still use the current **Greek-authorised product SmPC/EOF communication for the specific clozapine product**, because national product-information roll-out and monitoring-service procedures can lag or operationalise EU regulatory changes differently. This is an implementation check, not a block on the current board-fact framework.
