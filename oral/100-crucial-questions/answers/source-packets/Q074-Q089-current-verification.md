## Q74. Pharmacokinetic / pharmacodynamic principles

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| CYP1A2 / smoking | KEEP | The clinically important psychiatry example remains **tobacco-smoke polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons inducing CYP1A2**, lowering exposure to clozapine and, less dramatically, olanzapine. Nicotine itself does not reproduce this induction. Smoking reduction/cessation can therefore raise drug levels within days. | MHRA/NHS SPS smoking-interaction guidance; existing Q37a verification |
| CYP2D6 | KEEP | CYP2D6 remains high-yield because several antidepressants—notably **fluoxetine and paroxetine**—are clinically important inhibitors and may raise concentrations of susceptible substrates such as some TCAs, antipsychotics, atomoxetine and beta-blockers. Avoid presenting every substrate as equally affected; check the relevant SmPC for consequential combinations. | Current SmPCs / interaction guidance |
| CYP3A4 | KEEP | CYP3A4 inhibition can raise exposure to susceptible psychotropics and induction can lower it. Strong inhibitors/inducers are the board-level principle; the Writer does not need an encyclopaedic list. Carbamazepine remains a classic inducer. | SmPC/BNF-style interaction guidance |
| Induction vs inhibition | KEEP | **Inhibition** usually acts relatively quickly and can raise substrate exposure/toxicity; **enzyme induction** develops and resolves more gradually because enzyme expression changes, and can reduce efficacy of susceptible substrates. | Clinical pharmacology principles |
| Steady state | UPDATE | Teach steady state as occurring after **several elimination half-lives**, commonly around 4–5 half-lives, rather than as an immutable exact number. Long-lived active metabolites and non-linear kinetics can complicate this. | Clinical pharmacology principles |
| Renal/hepatic impairment | KEEP | Dose/exposure consequences depend on route of elimination, active metabolites, protein binding and therapeutic index. Lithium is the key renal-clearance psychiatric example; hepatic impairment especially matters for drugs extensively hepatically metabolised. “Normal dose in all liver disease” or “halve every renally cleared drug” are both unsafe simplifications. | SmPCs / prescribing guidance |
| Pharmacogenetics | UPDATE | Pharmacogenetic information can sometimes help explain unusual exposure or guide selected drugs, but **routine panel testing is not established as a universal prerequisite for psychotropic selection** in major UK/European practice. Do not promise reliably improved outcomes from commercial combinatorial tests as a board fact. | Major guideline/regulatory position; CPIC-style gene–drug guidance is drug-specific |

## Q75. Important psychotropic interactions

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smoking–clozapine/olanzapine | KEEP | Retain the smoke–CYP1A2 interaction. Abrupt smoking cessation can substantially raise clozapine exposure; use clinical monitoring and TDM rather than a universal fixed dose reduction. NRT does not maintain CYP1A2 induction. | MHRA; NHS SPS; Q37a current-verification packet |
| Serotonergic combinations / MAOIs | KEEP | Combining an **irreversible non-selective MAOI** with serotonergic antidepressants is contraindicated because of potentially severe serotonin toxicity. Linezolid/methylene blue and some serotonergic opioids are also clinically important interaction contexts. | Current fluoxetine/phenelzine/tranylcypromine SmPCs |
| Additive QT prolongation | KEEP | Combining QT-prolonging drugs plus electrolyte abnormalities, structural heart disease or high exposure increases torsades risk. Treat this as **cumulative risk**, not as a property of psychotropics alone. | SmPCs / cardiac safety guidance |
| Benzodiazepines + opioids/alcohol | KEEP | Additive CNS and **respiratory depression** is a major potentially fatal interaction. Avoid casual co-prescribing; if combination is unavoidable, use the minimum necessary exposure and monitor according to clinical context. | Regulatory opioid/benzodiazepine warnings |
| Lithium interactions | KEEP | **NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and diuretics—especially thiazides—can raise lithium exposure** through renal mechanisms. Dehydration, reduced sodium intake and acute illness further increase toxicity risk. | NICE CG185; SPS lithium monitoring |
| Valproate–lamotrigine | KEEP | Valproate **inhibits lamotrigine glucuronidation**, increasing lamotrigine exposure and rash/SJS/TEN risk; lamotrigine therefore requires a slower/lower titration when combined with valproate. | Lamotrigine SmPC; NICE CG185 |
| Enzyme-inducing ASMs | KEEP | Carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital/primidone can reduce exposure to many psychotropics and other medicines. The interaction principle matters more than a large table. | Current SmPCs / interaction guidance |
| SSRI/SNRI + NSAID/anticoagulant bleeding | KEEP | SSRIs/SNRIs can increase bleeding risk through platelet effects, which is amplified by NSAIDs, antiplatelets and anticoagulants. Do **not** teach “always prescribe a PPI”; gastroprotection depends on the patient’s GI-risk profile and the interacting regimen. | NICE/SmPC interaction guidance |

## Q76. Antipsychotic selection

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selection principle | KEEP | Antipsychotic choice should be individualised according to **previous response, adverse-effect vulnerability, physical comorbidity, interactions, adherence history, formulation preference and patient priorities**. Do not convert small mean efficacy differences into a rigid hierarchy for ordinary first-line treatment. | NICE CG178 / current schizophrenia guidance |
| Metabolic burden | KEEP | **Clozapine and olanzapine** remain high metabolic-risk agents. Aripiprazole, lurasidone and ziprasidone generally have lower metabolic liability, but real-world effects vary and monitoring remains required. | NICE/SmPC comparative adverse-effect evidence |
| EPS/prolactin | KEEP | Potent D2 blockade is associated with greater EPS risk. **Risperidone/paliperidone and amisulpride** are important prolactin-elevating examples; aripiprazole is relatively prolactin-sparing and may lower prolactin. Avoid claiming all FGAs or SGAs behave identically. | SmPCs / NICE adverse-effect profiles |
| QT | KEEP | QT liability varies by drug and patient risk. Selection should consider baseline cardiac disease, other QT-prolonging drugs, electrolyte disturbance and dose/exposure. | SmPCs / cardiac safety guidance |
| LAIs | KEEP | Long-acting injectable antipsychotics are appropriate when the patient prefers them or when avoiding covert/non-adherence is a clinical priority. They are **not merely a coercive “non-compliance treatment.”** | NICE CG178 |
| Clozapine threshold | KEEP | Clozapine remains indicated after **failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials** for treatment-resistant schizophrenia. Do not impose Oxford’s extra historical requirement that one failed drug must be an SGA unless the applicable guideline specifically says so. | NICE CG178; Q18 current verification |

## Q77 / Q77a. EPS and akathisia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute dystonia | KEEP | Acute dystonia is managed urgently by stopping/reducing the offending exposure where possible and giving a **parenteral anticholinergic/antimuscarinic** such as procyclidine or benztropine according to local formulary. Laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency. | Acute EPS prescribing guidance |
| Drug-induced parkinsonism | UPDATE | First reassess antipsychotic dose/need and consider switching to a lower-EPS agent. Short-term anticholinergics can be used when necessary but should not become automatic long-term prophylaxis, especially in older adults because of cognitive/anticholinergic burden. Amantadine may be an alternative in selected patients. | Current EPS guidance / SmPCs |
| Akathisia definition | KEEP | **Akathisia = subjective inner restlessness/urge to move plus observable motor restlessness**, usually temporally linked to starting or increasing a causative drug. Distinguish it from anxiety, agitation, mania and psychotic excitement. | Clinical EPS consensus |
| Akathisia treatment | KEEP | Reduce dose or switch the culprit when feasible. **Propranolol** remains a commonly recommended first pharmacological option when not contraindicated. Benzodiazepines and low-dose mirtazapine are secondary/selected options. Anticholinergics are not a reliable general treatment unless concomitant parkinsonism is present. | Current akathisia reviews/consensus |
| Tardive dyskinesia | UPDATE | Reassess dopamine-blocker exposure, discontinue unnecessary anticholinergics, consider switching to a lower-TD-risk strategy and use a VMAT2 inhibitor when appropriate. **Deutetrabenazine (Austedo) received EU-wide marketing authorisation on 8 Jan 2026 for moderate–severe tardive dyskinesia in adults.** This is a major update from older “US-only VMAT2 inhibitor” teaching. | EMA Austedo EPAR 2026 |
| Greek availability | UNRESOLVED | EU authorisation of deutetrabenazine is confirmed, but **routine Greek market availability/reimbursement was not verified** in this pass. Do not equate EMA authorisation with guaranteed Greek access. | EMA EPAR; no authoritative Greek access source confirmed |

## Q78. Metabolic / endocrine / cardiovascular adverse effects

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline monitoring | UPDATE | Before antipsychotics, current NICE schizophrenia guidance includes **weight, waist circumference, pulse/BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement-disorder assessment, nutrition/diet/activity**. Not every service uses exactly the same panel for every indication, so label this as a major-guideline monitoring framework rather than universal law. | NICE CG178 |
| Follow-up | UPDATE | NICE CG178: weight weekly for 6 weeks, then 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; waist annually; pulse/BP, glucose/HbA1c and lipids at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually. Other indications/guidelines vary, but repeated metabolic monitoring is mandatory in principle. | NICE CG178 |
| ECG | UPDATE | Do **not** teach “ECG for every outpatient before every antipsychotic.” NICE recommends baseline ECG when required by the SmPC, with cardiovascular risk/history, sudden-collapse/arrhythmia risk, relevant QT co-medication, or inpatient admission. | NICE CG178/CG185/NG222 |
| QTc >500 ms | KEEP | **QTc >500 ms is a clinically important high-risk threshold** that should prompt urgent review of causative drugs, electrolytes and cardiac risk and generally discontinuation/switch of the offending QT-prolonging antipsychotic where feasible, with cardiology input depending on context. | British/European cardiac safety guidance |
| Prolactin | KEEP | Baseline prolactin is recommended in NICE schizophrenia monitoring; thereafter repeat according to symptoms/drug and clinical context. Symptoms include sexual dysfunction, menstrual disturbance, galactorrhoea, infertility and longer-term bone effects. | NICE CG178; SmPCs |
| Drug tendencies | KEEP | High-yield tendencies: clozapine/olanzapine—metabolic; risperidone/paliperidone/amisulpride—prolactin; high-potency D2 antagonism—EPS. Avoid oversimplified rank tables. | SmPC/current guideline synthesis |
| Clozapine myocarditis | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Early clozapine myocarditis is a genuine high-stakes risk. **Troponin/CRP surveillance protocols are widely used and supported by specialist guidance**, particularly during the first weeks, but they are **not a universally legally mandated global monitoring schedule**. Present any exact protocol as a specialist/local protocol rather than universal law. | Existing Q19 verification; clozapine specialist guidance |

## Q79. Antidepressant selection

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| SSRIs | KEEP | SSRIs remain a major first-line pharmacological class for depressive disorders because of overall efficacy, tolerability and overdose safety relative to TCAs/MAOIs. | NICE NG222 |
| Individualised choice | KEEP | Choose according to **previous response, symptom profile, comorbidity, sleep/appetite, pain, sexual adverse effects, cardiac risk, overdose toxicity, interactions, pregnancy considerations and patient preference**. | NICE NG222 / SmPCs |
| SNRIs / mirtazapine | KEEP | SNRIs and mirtazapine remain standard alternatives/first-line options in appropriate patients rather than intrinsically “second-class” treatments. ## Q80. Antidepressant adverse effects / switching / discontinuation

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Serotonin syndrome | KEEP | Serotonin toxicity is most concerning after overdose, dose escalation, metabolic interaction or combination of serotonergic drugs. **Clonus and hyperreflexia** are especially useful diagnostic signs; stop serotonergic agents and provide supportive care. | Current serotonin-toxicity reviews; SmPCs |
| Hyponatraemia | KEEP | SSRIs/SNRIs can cause SIADH/hyponatraemia, particularly in older adults, patients on diuretics or those with low body weight/other risk factors. Investigate new confusion, seizures or unexplained deterioration. | SmPCs / medicines safety guidance |
| Sexual dysfunction | KEEP | SSRIs/SNRIs commonly cause reduced libido, delayed orgasm/anorgasmia and erectile/ejaculatory dysfunction; persistent symptoms after discontinuation have been reported in SmPCs. | Current SSRI SmPCs |
| Bleeding | KEEP | Platelet-mediated bleeding risk rises with NSAIDs, antiplatelets or anticoagulants. Risk mitigation should be individualised. | SmPCs / prescribing guidance |
| BP/QT | KEEP | Venlafaxine and some other SNRIs can raise BP; citalopram/escitalopram have dose-related QT cautions. Do not generalise these risks equally to all antidepressants. | SmPCs / MHRA |
| Withdrawal | UPDATE | Withdrawal/discontinuation symptoms can be clinically significant and prolonged in some patients. **Paroxetine and venlafaxine have higher withdrawal liability; fluoxetine generally lower because of its long half-life.** Taper according to drug, duration, dose and prior withdrawal—not a rigid universal 4-week schedule. | NICE NG222 |
| Fluoxetine → irreversible MAOI | KEEP | **At least 5 weeks** should elapse after stopping fluoxetine before starting an irreversible non-selective MAOI. This remains an exact board-worthy safety interval. | Fluoxetine SmPC |
| Irreversible MAOI → fluoxetine | KEEP | Current fluoxetine SmPC requires **at least 2 weeks** after stopping an irreversible non-selective MAOI before starting fluoxetine. Individual MAOI product information may specify exact intervals for other antidepressants. | Fluoxetine SmPC |
| Switching strategy | UPDATE | Direct switch, cautious cross-taper or washout depends on the specific pair and interaction risk. Do not teach cross-taper as universally safe; MAOIs are the classic situation where washout rules override routine switching habits. | Specialist Pharmacy Service / SmPCs |

## Q81 / Q81a. Lithium

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indications | KEEP | Lithium remains a major treatment for acute mania, bipolar maintenance and antidepressant augmentation in resistant depression. It has evidence for reducing suicide/self-harm risk in mood disorders, but do not promise absolute protection or freeze a single percentage. | NICE CG185/NG222; systematic evidence |
| Maintenance target | KEEP | NICE’s standard maintenance target when initiating lithium for bipolar disorder is **0.6–0.8 mmol/L**. Consider **0.8–1.0 mmol/L** for at least 6 months in people who relapse on lithium or have persistent subthreshold symptoms with impairment. | NICE CG185 |
| Acute mania range | UPDATE | Product/formulary targets for acute mania vary somewhat. Avoid presenting 0.8–1.0 mmol/L as a universal “acute mania law”; titrate to the current product/guideline target, clinical response and toxicity risk. | SmPC / specialist guidance |
| Older/frail patients | UPDATE | Older adults often require **lower doses and closer monitoring** because renal clearance and toxicity vulnerability are greater. Some specialist guidance accepts lower effective concentrations in selected older adults, but do not hard-code one universal geriatric target into the main answer. | NICE/SPS/specialist lithium guidance |
| Level timing | KEEP | Standard lithium TDM is a **12-hour post-dose trough**. Check after initiation/dose changes once the new level can be interpreted at steady state; NICE operationally recommends a level 1 week after starting and after dose changes, weekly until stable. | NICE CG185/NG222 |
| Baseline | UPDATE | Baseline: **weight/BMI, U&Es/eGFR including calcium, thyroid function and FBC**; ECG when cardiovascular disease/risk factors are present. Pregnancy status/planning must be addressed where relevant. | NICE CG185 |
| Ongoing levels | KEEP | NICE: lithium level **every 3 months for the first year**, then every **6 months**, or every **3 months** in higher-risk groups (older age, interacting drugs, renal/thyroid/calcium risk, poor control/adherence, or level ≥0.8). | NICE CG185/NG222 |
| Ongoing renal/thyroid/calcium | KEEP | Weight/BMI, eGFR/U&Es including **calcium**, and thyroid function at least **every 6 months**, more often when abnormalities/risk factors emerge. | NICE CG185/NG222 |
| NDI / hypothyroidism / hyperparathyroidism | KEEP | Retain nephrogenic diabetes insipidus/polyuria-polydipsia, hypothyroidism, chronic renal impairment and **hyperparathyroidism/hypercalcaemia** as major long-term adverse effects. | SmPCs / NICE monitoring |
| Toxicity phenotype | KEEP | Toxicity may produce GI symptoms, coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion/drowsiness, neuromuscular abnormalities, seizures and coma. **Clinical symptoms matter even if the level is not dramatically elevated.** | SPS lithium monitoring; SmPCs |
| Interactions / illness | KEEP | NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs and diuretics can raise lithium levels. Dehydration from **vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, sweating or reduced intake** can precipitate toxicity. Maintain stable fluid/sodium intake and seek assessment during acute illness. | NICE CG185; SPS |
| Q81a gastroenteritis + NSAID | UPDATE | If a lithium-treated patient develops significant vomiting/diarrhoea/dehydration—especially after NSAID use—the correct immediate safety action is to **withhold lithium when toxicity is suspected and urgently assess hydration, U&Es/renal function and serum lithium**, stop/avoid the interacting NSAID, and obtain specialist advice. | SPS lithium monitoring; NICE CG185 |
| Emergency referral | ADD | Emergency/acute medical assessment is required for significant neurological toxicity, marked drowsiness/confusion, ataxia, seizures, arrhythmia, severe dehydration/AKI, inability to maintain intake, or other severe systemic illness. Do not wait for a “toxic” laboratory number before escalating a symptomatic patient. | SPS/NHS lithium safety guidance |
| Dialysis | UPDATE | Do **not** teach dialysis by serum lithium concentration alone. EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment for severe poisoning and specifically integrates **renal function, lithium concentration, level of consciousness, seizures, dysrhythmia and expected time to lithium <1.0 mmol/L**. Intermittent haemodialysis is preferred when ECTR is indicated. | EXTRIP lithium recommendations |

## Q83. Lamotrigine

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bipolar role | KEEP | Lamotrigine is useful particularly for **bipolar depression prevention/maintenance** and selected bipolar depressive episodes; it is **not an effective acute antimanic treatment** and NICE explicitly says not to offer it for mania. | NICE CG185 |
| Rash/SJS/TEN | KEEP | Serious rash including SJS/TEN is a defining safety issue; risk is associated with **rapid titration, high starting dose and concomitant valproate**. Stop and urgently assess a clinically significant rash unless clearly unrelated. | Lamotrigine SmPC |
| Titration | UPDATE | Exact titration is drug-interaction dependent and should follow the SmPC. The board principle is **slow titration, slower with valproate and faster/higher requirements with enzyme inducers**. Avoid one universal schedule divorced from co-medication. | Lamotrigine SmPC; NICE CG185 |
| Valproate | KEEP | Valproate reduces lamotrigine clearance and raises exposure; use the specific valproate-combination titration. | SmPC |
| Enzyme inducers | KEEP | Carbamazepine/phenytoin/phenobarbital/primidone increase lamotrigine clearance, changing titration and maintenance requirements. | SmPC |
| Combined hormonal contraceptives | ADD | Ethinylestradiol-containing combined hormonal contraceptives can increase lamotrigine clearance **about two-fold**, lowering levels; stopping the contraceptive can sharply raise lamotrigine exposure. Pill-free weeks can cause transient increases. Clinical/TDM review is needed when starting or stopping combined hormonal contraception. | Lamotrigine SmPC 2026 |
| Lamotrigine → contraception | UPDATE | Lamotrigine is not a strong enzyme inducer like carbamazepine, but interaction with contraceptive hormones can be clinically relevant. Do not simply tell patients “the pill has no interaction”; consult the specific contraceptive/lamotrigine product information. | SmPC |
| Pregnancy | ADD | Lamotrigine concentrations often **fall during pregnancy and rise rapidly postpartum** because clearance changes. Monitor clinically and, where used for a condition in which levels guide management, consider pre-pregnancy/during/postpartum concentrations to avoid relapse or postpartum toxicity. | NICE CG192; lamotrigine SmPC |

## Q84. Benzodiazepines / hypnotics

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dependence / tolerance | KEEP | Dependence and tolerance can develop with regular benzodiazepine use; risk increases with higher dose, longer exposure and certain patient vulnerabilities. | Current prescribing/deprescribing guidance |
| Withdrawal | KEEP | Abrupt cessation after physiological dependence can cause anxiety, insomnia, perceptual symptoms and, in severe cases, **seizures and delirium**. | 2025 joint benzodiazepine taper guideline; Q43 verification |
| Falls/cognition | KEEP | Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs can cause sedation, psychomotor impairment, falls and cognitive impairment, especially in older adults. | SmPCs / geriatric prescribing guidance |
| Opioids/alcohol | KEEP | Co-use with opioids/alcohol raises respiratory-depression and overdose risk. | Regulatory safety warnings |
| “2–4 weeks” | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | **2–4 weeks remains a useful short-term prescribing anchor** for many benzodiazepine indications, but it is not a universal legal maximum and some specialist indications require different durations. Current consensus often starts with small percentage reductions and slows further if withdrawal emerges. Switching to diazepam is optional/situation-dependent. | 2025 Joint Benzodiazepine Tapering Guideline; Q43 packet |

## Q86. Pregnancy / reproductive psychopharmacology

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| General principle | UPDATE | Use **individualised maternal relapse/untreated-illness risk versus fetal/neonatal medication risk**. Pregnancy is not a reason to abruptly stop an effective psychotropic without assessment; relapse can itself create major maternal and fetal risk. | NICE CG192 / specialist perinatal guidance |
| Antidepressants | KEEP | SSRIs are commonly used when benefits justify treatment. Consider previous response, reproductive safety data and neonatal issues. Do not present one SSRI as universally safest in every patient. | NICE CG192; SmPCs |
| SSRI PPHN | UPDATE | Late-pregnancy SSRI exposure is associated with a **possible small absolute increase** in persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn; this is a counselling point, not a deterministic outcome and not usually a reason by itself to withhold necessary treatment. | Regulatory/perinatal evidence |
| Neonatal adaptation | KEEP | Late-pregnancy antidepressant exposure can be associated with transient neonatal adaptation symptoms; inform patients without implying permanent toxicity. | NICE/perinatal guidance |
| Antipsychotics | KEEP | If a pregnant patient is stable on an antipsychotic and likely to relapse without it, NICE advises continuing treatment after individual risk-benefit discussion. Monitor weight/metabolic and gestational-diabetes risk. | NICE CG192 |
| Lithium / “Ebstein anomaly” | UPDATE | Replace the old deterministic “lithium causes Ebstein anomaly” teaching with: **first-trimester lithium is associated with an increased risk of cardiac malformations, but the absolute risk is much smaller than historic teaching implied and the exact risk is uncertain**. Lithium can be continued in selected high-relapse-risk patients with specialist monitoring. | NICE CG192; current lithium SmPCs / modern cohort evidence |
| Lithium in pregnancy | KEEP | If used, lithium requires **more frequent serum monitoring** because renal clearance changes during pregnancy and around delivery. Decisions about continuation, dose around delivery and postpartum restart belong in specialist perinatal care. | NICE CG192; lithium SmPC |
| Valproate | KEEP | For bipolar disorder, **do not use valproate in pregnancy**; in women able to become pregnant, current PPP/regulatory restrictions apply. | EMA/MHRA/EOF |
| Lamotrigine | KEEP | Lamotrigine is an important alternative for bipolar depressive-polarity illness; levels can fall substantially in pregnancy and rebound postpartum, requiring clinical/level review. | NICE CG192; SmPC |
| Carbamazepine | KEEP | NICE does not recommend carbamazepine for mental-health indications in women planning pregnancy, pregnant or considering breastfeeding because of reproductive risk and interactions. | NICE CG192 |
| Benzodiazepines | KEEP | Avoid routine benzodiazepines in pregnancy/postpartum; NICE reserves them for **short-term severe anxiety/agitation** when clinically necessary. Avoid chronic high-dose exposure where possible. | NICE CG192 |
| Breastfeeding | UPDATE | Breastfeeding compatibility is **drug-specific**. NICE generally encourages breastfeeding unless taking **lithium, clozapine or carbamazepine**; valproate is not recommended as a mental-health treatment in women/girls under current reproductive restrictions. Do not generalise “all psychotropics contraindicated” or “all SSRIs safe.” | NICE CG192 |

## Q87. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome versus serotonin syndrome

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time course | KEEP | Serotonin toxicity usually develops **rapidly—often within hours** of serotonergic change/overdose. NMS more often evolves over **1–3 days** after dopamine blockade or abrupt dopaminergic withdrawal, though timing is not absolute. | Current toxidrome/NMS guidance |
| Neuromuscular distinction | KEEP | NMS classically has severe **generalised rigidity with normal/reduced reflexes**; serotonin syndrome is characterised by **clonus, hyperreflexia and tremor**, often greater in the lower limbs. This is a high-value viva discriminator. | Hunter-based serotonin guidance; NMS consensus |
| CK | UPDATE | CK elevation supports NMS/rhabdomyolysis and severity assessment but is **not specific and not required alone for diagnosis**. | NMS consensus |
| Immediate treatment | KEEP | Stop causative drugs. Provide aggressive supportive care: airway/oxygenation, fluids, cooling, electrolyte/renal monitoring, management of rhabdomyolysis/complications and early HDU/ICU escalation for severe disease. | BAP catatonia/NMS guideline; serotonin-toxicity guidance |
| Benzodiazepines | KEEP | Benzodiazepines are useful for agitation and neuromuscular overactivity in serotonin toxicity and may be considered in early/mild NMS/catatonic presentations. | BAP / toxicology guidance |
| Bromocriptine / amantadine | UPDATE | These are **considered options**, particularly in moderate/severe NMS, not universal mandatory antidotes. Evidence is largely observational/case-based. | BAP 2023 NMS recommendations |
| Dantrolene | UPDATE | Dantrolene is most defensible in **severe NMS with extreme hyperthermia/rigidity**; it should not be presented as obligatory for every case. | BAP 2023 |
| Cyproheptadine | UPDATE | Cyproheptadine remains a commonly recommended option for **moderate/severe serotonin toxicity** when supportive care/benzodiazepines are insufficient, but high-quality efficacy evidence is limited. It is adjunctive, not a replacement for cooling/supportive care. | Current serotonin-toxicity reviews |

## Q88. TCA overdose

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanism / ECG | KEEP | TCA cardiotoxicity is driven importantly by **fast sodium-channel blockade**. Obtain an early 12-lead ECG and continuous monitoring in significant overdose. | Current toxicology guidance |
| QRS | KEEP | **QRS >100 ms remains a useful high-risk/intervention anchor** associated with increased seizure risk; greater widening—classically >160 ms—is associated with ventricular dysrhythmia risk. Treat the patient/ECG, not the serum TCA level. | Current toxicology references |
| Major toxicity | KEEP | Seizures, hypotension, reduced consciousness and ventricular dysrhythmias are the major life-threatening manifestations. Acidosis worsens sodium-channel blockade. | Toxicology guidance |
| Sodium bicarbonate | KEEP | IV **sodium bicarbonate is first-line for clinically significant sodium-channel cardiotoxicity**, including QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia and hypotension attributable to TCA toxicity. | Toxicology guidance |
| Endpoints | UPDATE | Titrate bicarbonate to **clinical/ECG improvement and controlled alkalinisation**, monitoring sodium, potassium and pH; avoid unlimited bicarbonate dosing. Exact bolus/infusion protocols should follow toxicology/local poison-centre guidance rather than be memorised as one universal regimen. | Current toxicology guidance |
| Antiarrhythmics | KEEP | Avoid **class Ia/Ic sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmics** such as procainamide/flecainide in TCA toxicity. Lidocaine may be used by toxicology/critical-care specialists in refractory ventricular dysrhythmia after adequate alkalinisation. | Toxicology guidance |
| Physostigmine | KEEP | **Do not use physostigmine in acute TCA poisoning with conduction toxicity** because of seizure/bradyarrhythmia risk. | Toxicology guidance |
| Critical care | KEEP | Early airway protection, seizure control with benzodiazepines, fluids/vasopressors as needed, correction of acidosis and intensive monitoring are central. Severe cases require ICU/toxicology input. | Current toxicology guidance |

## Q89. Electroconvulsive therapy

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Severe depression | KEEP | ECT remains a major treatment for **severe depression when rapid response is required**, when illness is life-threatening, or when other treatments have failed; psychotic depression is a classic high-response context. | NICE NG222 / 2025–26 ECT surveillance |
| Catatonia | KEEP | ECT is established for catatonia when benzodiazepines fail or when severity/medical urgency warrants rapid definitive treatment. | BAP catatonia guideline; NICE surveillance |
| Mania | KEEP | ECT remains an option for **prolonged or severe mania** when other treatments have failed or the condition is life-threatening. | NICE CG185/TA59 |
| Anaesthesia / muscle relaxation | KEEP | Modern ECT is performed under **general anaesthesia with neuromuscular blockade** and physiological monitoring. | NICE/RCPsych ECT standards |
| Unilateral vs bilateral | KEEP | Bilateral ECT generally has greater/faster efficacy at comparable treatment courses but more cognitive adverse effects; high-dose right unilateral ECT can reduce cognitive burden while retaining efficacy in many patients. Choice is individualised by urgency, prior response and cognitive risk. | Modern ECT guideline evidence |
| Cognitive adverse effects | UPDATE | Discuss acute confusion, transient **anterograde memory impairment** and **retrograde/autobiographical memory loss**, which may persist for some patients. Do not reassure that all memory effects invariably resolve quickly. | NICE ECT surveillance / RCPsych guidance |
| Continuation / maintenance ECT | KEEP | Continuation/maintenance ECT may be considered for selected patients who respond but repeatedly relapse, usually alongside pharmacotherapy; evidence is strongest in recurrent severe mood illness/catatonia rather than as routine continuation for everyone. | NICE 2025–26 surveillance / ECT guidelines |
| Contraindications / medical risk | UPDATE | Avoid teaching a simplistic list of absolute psychiatric/medical contraindications. ECT requires anaesthetic/medical risk assessment; raised intracranial pressure with mass effect, unstable cardiovascular disease and other major risks may require optimisation/specialist input. Most contraindications are **relative rather than absolute** in life-threatening psychiatric illness. | Modern ECT/anaesthesia guidance |

## Q89a. ECT consent, incapacity and Greek law

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacitous adult | KEEP | Under Greek general medical-consent law, a physician may not perform a medical act without the patient’s **prior valid informed consent**. ECT is a medical act; no current Greek source found creates a routine exception allowing ECT in a capacitous refusing adult merely because the treatment is recommended. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12; Oviedo Convention Law 2619/1998 art. 5 |
| Involuntary admission | KEEP | **Involuntary psychiatric admission does not itself establish global incapacity or automatically authorise every treatment.** This principle remains from the verified Q08–Q09 Greek-law packet. | Law 3418/2005 arts. 12, 28; Law 2071/1992; Q008–Q009 packet |
| Adult lacking capacity | UPDATE | General Greek law provides substitute-consent routes for adults lacking capacity (judicial supporter if appointed; otherwise the Code refers to close persons/`οικείοι`), with patient participation as far as possible. **However, no ECT-specific modern Greek statute or ministerial procedure was identified that clearly states whether these general substitute-consent rules alone are sufficient for ECT.** | Law 3418/2005 art. 12; Oviedo art. 6 |
| Emergency treatment | UNRESOLVED | Greek medical law permits emergency intervention without consent when appropriate consent cannot be obtained and there is an **immediate, absolute and urgent need** for care. It is legally plausible that life-saving ECT in exceptional malignant catatonia/severe life-threatening illness could be argued within this framework, but **no current Greek ECT-specific authoritative source was found confirming the procedural threshold or authorisation pathway**. Do not convert this inference into a board-law fact. | Law 3418/2005 art. 12(3); no ECT-specific official procedure located |
| Psychiatric treatment against will | UPDATE | Art. 28(8) of the Greek Code of Medical Ethics permits psychiatric treatment against the patient’s will in defined circumstances where refusal endangers life of the patient/others or seriously worsens the mental disorder, while compulsory admission must follow statutory procedure. **This provision is broad and does not specify ECT-specific safeguards.** | Law 3418/2005 art. 28(8) |
| Court / prosecutor / guardian approval | UNRESOLVED | No current official Greek source was found establishing a specific rule that ECT for an incapacitous or involuntarily admitted adult requires—or does not require—prior **court, prosecutor, judicial supporter or second-opinion authorisation** beyond general consent law. Do not import UK MHA section/second-opinion rules. | Greek MoH rights legislation index; Law 3418/2005; Law 2071/1992 |
| Procedural protections | UNRESOLVED | General rights protections—information, dignity, least restriction, participation, record/documentation, rights during involuntary admission—apply, but an **ECT-specific national procedural protection framework was not cleanly verifiable** from current official sources searched. | MoH mental-health rights sources |

### Q89a adjudication note
The legally safe learner-facing formulation is: **a capacitous adult must ordinarily give informed consent; involuntary admission alone does not authorise ECT; for incapacity or emergency ECT, Greek general consent/emergency law applies, but a current ECT-specific authorisation procedure was not verified and should be labelled unresolved rather than filled with UK rules.**

## Genuine exam/current splits for later adjudication

1. **Smoking interaction:** nicotine is not the CYP1A2 inducer; tobacco-smoke PAHs are.
2. **Antipsychotic ECG:** baseline ECG is risk/SmPC/setting-dependent, not automatically required for every outpatient.
3. **TD treatment:** older Europe-facing texts that list VMAT2 inhibitors as US-only are now outdated—**deutetrabenazine is EU-authorised as of Jan 2026**.
4. **Clozapine myocarditis:** troponin/CRP protocols are specialist monitoring standards, not a universally legislated schedule.
5. **Antidepressant withdrawal:** modern guidance recognises potentially substantial/prolonged withdrawal and individualised tapering; “one month for everyone” is obsolete.
6. **Lithium dialysis:** serum concentration alone does not determine dialysis; EXTRIP integrates symptoms, renal function and expected clearance.
7. **Valproate women:** not “absolutely banned in every woman of childbearing potential”; current wording is contraindicated **unless PPP conditions are met**, with use only when alternatives are unsuitable/ineffective/not tolerated under specialist care.
8. **Valproate men:** 2024 EU/Greek precautionary reproductive measures now belong in current practice.
9. **Lamotrigine contraception:** combined estrogen-containing contraception can roughly double lamotrigine clearance; starting/stopping it can materially change lamotrigine exposure.
10. **Benzodiazepines:** 2–4 weeks is a useful short-term anchor, not a universal statutory maximum; tapering is individualised.
11. **Pregnancy/lithium:** “lithium causes Ebstein anomaly” should be replaced by a smaller, uncertain increased cardiac-malformation risk plus maternal-relapse risk balancing.
12. **NMS treatment:** bromocriptine/dantrolene are options by severity, not mandatory antidotes for every case.
13. **ECT contraindications:** most medical contraindications are relative and require anaesthetic/medical risk assessment rather than a memorised absolute list.
14. **Greek ECT law:** UK ECT consent/second-opinion rules must not be imported; the Greek ECT-specific incapacity/emergency authorisation route remains incompletely verified.

## Unresolved issues

1. **Greek ECT-specific incapacity/emergency procedure:** general Greek consent, substitute-consent, emergency and psychiatry-specific provisions are verified, but no current official source was found that clearly specifies ECT-specific court/prosecutor/guardian/second-opinion requirements. This remains `UNRESOLVED` and should survive into adjudication.
2. **Routine Greek access to deutetrabenazine (Austedo):** EU authorisation is confirmed; Greek reimbursement/market availability was not verified.
3. **Greek/local clozapine myocarditis protocol:** internationally used CRP/troponin protocols are established, but the packet intentionally does not label one exact schedule as universally mandatory in Greece.
4. **Exact acute-mania lithium target:** product/guideline ranges vary; maintenance targets and monitoring are stable enough for the board answer, but do not freeze one universal acute-mania concentration without a later source-locked prescribing question.
5. No unresolved issue blocks creation of Q74–Q89 answer briefs, provided the Q89a legal uncertainty is explicitly preserved.
