## Q37. Substance-use-disorder assessment and formulation

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 retains substance-specific disorder groupings but expands the clinical architecture beyond the old ICD-10 “harmful use / dependence syndrome” shorthand. For each relevant substance class, ICD-11 can code **single episode of harmful use**, **harmful pattern of use** (continuous or episodic/recurrent), **dependence**, intoxication, withdrawal and substance-induced mental/behavioural disorders. There is also a non-disorder category for **hazardous use**. | WHO ICD-11 substance-use terminology |
| Dependence construct | UPDATE | “Dependence syndrome” remains recognisable conceptually, but ICD-11 simplifies the diagnostic requirements for **substance dependence** rather than preserving the ICD-10 six-feature/3-of-6 teaching as the only current formulation. The learner-facing answer should therefore avoid presenting the ICD-10 checklist as current ICD law. | WHO ICD-11 substance-use terminology/CDDR |
| Assessment domains | KEEP | Core clinical assessment remains pattern/quantity/frequency/route, intoxication and withdrawal, loss of control/craving, harms and functional consequences, prior treatment, comorbid psychiatric/medical illness, prescribed/non-prescribed substances, overdose and self-harm risk, social supports, safeguarding, readiness/goals, and physical-health/harm-reduction needs. Classification does not materially change this practical structure. | NICE CG51 assessment framework; ICD-11 architecture |

## Q37a. Tobacco cessation in schizophrenia

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| What induces CYP1A2 | UPDATE | The clinically important inducer is **polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke**, not nicotine itself. Smoke induces CYP1A2 and increases clearance of CYP1A2 substrates, especially clozapine and, to a lesser degree, olanzapine. | MHRA Drug Safety Update; NHS SPS smoking-interaction guidance |
| Clozapine and olanzapine exposure | KEEP | Smokers generally have lower clozapine exposure at a given dose; smoking cessation/reduction can substantially increase clozapine levels and toxicity risk. Olanzapine exposure also rises after smoking cessation, but the interaction is usually less clinically dramatic. | NHS SPS; MHRA |
| Timing after smoking cessation | ADD | A clinically meaningful rise in clozapine can occur **within several days** of smoking cessation; NHS GGC notes substantial rises within about **3–5 days**, while the broader CYP1A2 de-induction effect evolves over roughly the first week and may continue thereafter. Do not assume hospital smoking abstinence is pharmacokinetically irrelevant. | NHS GGC clozapine-and-smoking guidance; MHRA |
| Fixed percentage dose reduction | UPDATE | Do **not** teach one automatic percentage reduction for clozapine. Safer current practice is urgent specialist review, clinical monitoring for toxicity, and **clozapine TDM before/after smoking-status change where possible**, with dose adjustment according to exposure and symptoms. Some protocols use an empirical reduction as a starting point, but it should not be frozen as a universal board number. | NHS SPS clozapine guidance; MHRA |
| Nicotine replacement / vaping | ADD | **NRT does not reproduce smoke-induced CYP1A2 induction.** Switching from cigarettes to NRT or nicotine vaping therefore behaves pharmacokinetically like stopping smoke exposure for clozapine. The key distinction is tobacco smoke versus nicotine delivery. | NHS GGC; NHS SPS |
| Smoking restart | ADD | Restarting cigarette smoking can lower clozapine exposure and risk loss of efficacy; the prescriber should reassess levels/clinical response and titrate if needed. | NHS SPS |
| Smoking-cessation pharmacotherapy | UPDATE | Varenicline, combination NRT and bupropion are evidence-based cessation treatments generally; current WHO/NICE tobacco guidance no longer supports the old broad psychiatric-safety alarm around varenicline. In severe mental illness, cessation should be actively offered with tailored behavioural support. For schizophrenia specifically, varenicline and NRT are appropriate options; bupropion requires individual contraindication/risk assessment. | WHO tobacco-cessation guideline 2024; NICE NG209; GOV.UK mental-health smoking guidance |

### Q37a adjudication note

The must-not-miss board point is not “smoking lowers clozapine” in isolation; it is that **abrupt smoking reduction/cessation can convert a previously tolerated clozapine dose into a toxic exposure within days**, including when the patient is admitted to a smoke-free ward and placed on NRT.

## Q38. Alcohol withdrawal

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Severe withdrawal / complications | KEEP | Alcohol withdrawal can progress to **seizures and delirium tremens**, both of which require urgent medical management. History of withdrawal seizures/DT, severe dependence, significant physical/psychiatric comorbidity, frailty/cognitive impairment, poor support and concurrent sedative withdrawal lower the threshold for inpatient/residential withdrawal. | NICE CG100/CG115; UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26 |
| Benzodiazepines | KEEP | Benzodiazepine reducing regimens remain the standard pharmacological treatment for medically assisted alcohol withdrawal. Chlordiazepoxide or diazepam are common first choices; treatment is titrated to dependence/withdrawal severity and monitoring. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26; NICE CG115 |
| Fixed-dose versus symptom-triggered | UPDATE | **Fixed-dose regimens are suitable for community withdrawal.** In inpatient/residential settings, either fixed-dose or symptom-triggered regimens can be used. Symptom-triggered treatment is a specialist approach requiring frequent, reliable assessment by trained staff; it should not be treated as automatically superior in every setting. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26; NICE CG115 |
| CIWA-Ar | KEEP | CIWA-Ar can support structured monitoring but is an **adjunct to clinical judgement**, not a substitute for it. It may be unreliable or inappropriate when the patient cannot communicate reliably, has delirium, severe medical illness, language/cognitive barriers or confounding symptoms. | NICE CG100; NICE CG115 |
| Liver disease | UPDATE | In significantly compromised liver function, consider shorter-acting agents such as **oxazepam or lorazepam** to reduce accumulation; longer-acting benzodiazepines can still be used with careful dose reduction and monitoring. Severe/decompensated liver disease warrants specialist/inpatient management. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26 |
| Thiamine | KEEP | Thiamine prophylaxis is part of medically assisted withdrawal and should also be considered in harmful/dependent drinkers at risk of deficiency. High-risk or acutely ill/malnourished patients may need parenteral rather than oral thiamine. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment; NICE CG100 |
| Monitoring / escalation | KEEP | Monitor vitals, hydration, mental state, sedation and withdrawal progression; escalate urgently for seizures, delirium, worsening autonomic instability, suspected Wernicke encephalopathy or inability to manage safely in the current setting. | NICE CG100; UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |

## Q38a. Wernicke encephalopathy

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classic triad | DROP | Do **not** require the complete classic triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia. Wernicke encephalopathy is commonly incomplete; any compatible mental-status change, ocular abnormality, ataxia/gait disturbance or high-risk nutritional context should lower the threshold for treatment. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26 |
| Emergency treatment | KEEP | Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy is a **medical emergency** and should be treated immediately with **parenteral thiamine**, preferably IV in hospital. Treatment should not await imaging or confirmation. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment; NICE CG100 |
| Exact treatment regimen | ADD | Current UK authoritative guidance gives **IV thiamine 300–500 mg three times daily for 3–5 days**, with daily review; if still symptomatic, continue **300–500 mg IV once daily for a further 3–5 days or while improvement continues**, then follow with oral thiamine. This is sufficiently high-stakes and stable to retain as a board-level regimen, while recognising local product formulations may differ. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment 2025/26 |
| Glucose/carbohydrate | UPDATE | In a thiamine-deficient/high-risk patient, give thiamine **before or at the same time as carbohydrate/glucose whenever feasible**; do not delay urgently needed treatment of hypoglycaemia, but ensure prompt parenteral thiamine. Avoid using dextrose as routine rehydration in malnourished alcohol-dependent patients at risk of WE/refeeding complications. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment, acute hospital section |
| Magnesium | ADD | Check and correct **hypomagnesaemia**, particularly when response to thiamine is poor, because magnesium is required for thiamine-dependent enzymatic function and deficiency can contribute to apparent thiamine refractoriness. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |
| Korsakoff syndrome | KEEP | Untreated or undertreated Wernicke encephalopathy can progress to persistent amnestic/cognitive impairment within the Wernicke–Korsakoff spectrum. Prevention and prompt treatment are therefore neurologically consequential, not simply symptomatic. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |

## Q39. Alcohol-use disorder after withdrawal

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychosocial relapse prevention | KEEP | Structured psychosocial treatment remains central after withdrawal: motivational approaches, CBT/behavioural approaches, social-network interventions, mutual aid and couples/family work according to need. Medication should generally be paired with psychosocial care rather than presented as stand-alone relapse prevention. | NICE CG115; WHO mhGAP 2023 |
| Acamprosate / oral naltrexone | KEEP | **Acamprosate and oral naltrexone** remain the two principal guideline-supported relapse-prevention medicines after withdrawal for moderate/severe alcohol dependence. Selection depends on treatment goal, liver/renal status, opioid use/need, adherence and prior response. | NICE CG115; UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |
| Disulfiram | KEEP | Disulfiram is a later/selected option, mainly when the goal is **abstinence**, acamprosate/naltrexone are unsuitable or the patient specifically prefers it and understands risks. Supervision and hepatotoxicity/cardiovascular/psychiatric cautions matter. | NICE CG115 |
| Nalmefene | KEEP | Nalmefene remains a **European/UK reduction-oriented option** for a defined subgroup with alcohol dependence and persistently high drinking risk who do not need immediate detoxification, alongside psychosocial support. It should not displace acamprosate/naltrexone in the standard post-withdrawal abstinence pathway. | NICE TA325; UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |
| Abstinence versus reduction | UPDATE | Treatment goals should be explicit. Abstinence is preferred when clinically necessary (for example advanced liver disease, pregnancy, prior severe withdrawal/medical harm), while reduction may be an appropriate negotiated goal in selected patients. | UK Clinical Guidelines for Alcohol Treatment |
| Duration anchor | KEEP | NICE commonly uses **about 6 months** for acamprosate or oral naltrexone, with continuation beyond that when the patient is benefiting and wishes to continue. This is a useful practical anchor, not a mandatory stop date. | NICE CG115 |

## Q40. Opioid use disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maintenance treatment | KEEP | **Opioid agonist maintenance treatment with methadone or buprenorphine** is evidence-based standard care for opioid dependence/OUD. WHO’s 2026 update reaffirmed strong recommendations for methadone and oral buprenorphine maintenance. | WHO opioid-dependence guideline update 2026; NICE TA114 |
| Detoxification alone | UPDATE | Detoxification is an option for a patient choosing abstinence, but it should not be presented as the default or superior strategy. Loss of tolerance after detoxification raises overdose risk, and maintenance treatment improves retention and reduces mortality/harm. | NICE CG52; WHO 2026 opioid guideline update; DHSC OST guidance 2024 |
| Buprenorphine precipitated withdrawal | KEEP | Buprenorphine’s high receptor affinity can precipitate withdrawal if started while sufficient full-agonist opioid effect remains. The board-level principle is to begin when **clinically significant withdrawal is present** and tailor induction to the opioid exposure; a single universal hour count or COWS threshold is unsafe in the fentanyl era and need not be memorised here. | NICE/DHSC OST guidance; contemporary OUD practice |
| Methadone induction safety | KEEP | Methadone is a full agonist with no respiratory-depression ceiling; **induction is a high-risk period** and dose increases must be cautious, especially with sedatives/alcohol, uncertain tolerance, respiratory disease or other overdose risks. | DHSC OST guidance 2024 |
| QT risk | KEEP | Methadone can prolong QT and warrants attention to cardiac history, interacting QT-prolonging drugs, electrolyte disturbance and higher-risk clinical contexts. Routine ECG practice varies by guideline/risk profile; do not turn this into a universal ECG-before-every-dose rule. | Methadone SmPC/BNF-type prescribing guidance; specialist OUD guidance |
| Overdose / naloxone | KEEP | Opioid overdose should be suspected with depressed consciousness and respiratory depression, often with miosis but not invariably. **Naloxone plus airway/ventilatory support** is the emergency treatment; repeat dosing/ongoing observation may be required because naloxone can wear off before the opioid. | WHO overdose guidance; standard emergency prescribing guidance |
| Take-home naloxone | UPDATE | Take-home/community naloxone is current harm-reduction standard. In **Greece, since July 2025**, intranasal and prefilled injectable naloxone can be prescribed by physicians of any specialty, dispensed by private pharmacies and administered by anyone in an overdose emergency. This is a genuine Greek current-practice update worth retaining. | Greek Ministry of Health, 17 Jul 2025 |
| Greek treatment framework | ADD | Current official Greek sources confirm that **OKANA integrated opioid-dependence programmes use methadone and buprenorphine** as substitution/agonist treatment alongside psychosocial and health/social rehabilitation. Do not invent UK-style supervision/dispensing rules for Greece. | OKANA MOΘΕ / substitution-treatment pages |
| Harm reduction / physical health | KEEP | Assess injecting and other route-related harms, HIV/HBV/HCV risk, vaccination/testing, skin/soft-tissue and endocarditis risk, overdose history, polysubstance use, sexual health and social vulnerability. Link to needle/syringe and infectious-disease care where indicated. | NICE drug-use standards; WHO harm-reduction principles |

## Q41. Cannabis-related disorders

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 architecture | UPDATE | ICD-11 has distinct **cannabis intoxication, withdrawal, harmful use/pattern, dependence and cannabis-induced mental/behavioural disorders**, and separately classifies disorders due to **synthetic cannabinoids**. | WHO ICD-11 substance-use terminology |
| Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder | KEEP | Diagnose a cannabis-induced psychotic disorder when psychosis has a plausible temporal relationship to cannabis intoxication or withdrawal and exceeds what would ordinarily be expected from simple intoxication/withdrawal. A history of independent psychosis, psychosis predating cannabis exposure, or persistence well beyond the expected substance-related period argues for a primary psychotic disorder. ICD-11 does not give a single universal abstinence-duration cutoff that proves causation. | ICD-11 CDDR; prior Q11–Q19 current verification |
| Cannabis and primary psychosis | UPDATE | Cannabis exposure—especially frequent/high-potency use—is associated with increased risk of psychotic disorder and with poorer relapse/course outcomes in established psychosis. Frame this as **risk association and potentially modifiable relapse factor**, not deterministic one-to-one causation. | Current psychosis guideline/review consensus; NICE CG120 |
| Cannabis-use-disorder treatment | KEEP | Psychosocial treatment remains the mainstay. WHO supports brief intervention for cannabis use and CBT/MET/family approaches for dependence; no routinely licensed pharmacotherapy has sufficient evidence to become a standard board answer. | WHO mhGAP cannabis recommendations 2023; NICE CG51 |
| Synthetic cannabinoids | ADD | Synthetic cannabinoids deserve a concise warning because they can produce more severe/unpredictable toxicity, agitation, psychosis and medical complications than traditional cannabis. They are separately classified in ICD-11, but do not need a separate treatment chapter here. | WHO ICD-11 architecture |

## Q42. Stimulant-related disorders

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intoxication / psychosis | KEEP | Cocaine and amphetamine-type stimulants can cause a sympathomimetic toxidrome, severe agitation, paranoia/psychosis, hyperthermia, seizures and cardiovascular/cerebrovascular complications. Stimulant-induced psychosis should be assessed against temporal course and persistence outside intoxication/withdrawal. | ASAM/AAAP Stimulant Use Disorder Guideline 2024; ICD-11 substance-induced framework |
| Acute agitation / hyperadrenergic toxicity | KEEP | **Benzodiazepines are first-line/core treatment** for stimulant-induced agitation and hyperadrenergic states, together with cooling, fluids/supportive care and treatment of complications. Severe toxicity needs acute-care monitoring. | ASAM/AAAP 2024 recommendations 63–66 |
| Red flags | KEEP | Chest pain/ischemia, severe hypertension, arrhythmia, hyperthermia, seizure, focal neurological signs, altered consciousness, rhabdomyolysis/renal injury and severe psychosis/suicidality require escalation and targeted investigation. | ASAM/AAAP 2024 |
| Cocaine and beta-blockers | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | The historical blanket statement **“beta-blockers are contraindicated in cocaine-associated chest pain” is too absolute**. Current ASAM/AAAP guidance acknowledges the controversy and lower-than-theorised risk. Benzodiazepines and vasodilator-based strategies remain preferred acutely; if a beta-blocker is needed during stimulant intoxication, a drug with **combined alpha-1 antagonism (for example labetalol/carvedilol)** is preferred. Do not teach unopposed beta-blockade as universally forbidden in all cocaine-exposed patients. | ASAM/AAAP 2024 recommendations 65, 68 |
| Withdrawal / crash | KEEP | Stimulant withdrawal commonly produces fatigue, hypersomnia or insomnia, dysphoria/depression, anhedonia and craving. **Suicide risk can increase during the crash/early abstinence period**, so mood and self-harm assessment is part of withdrawal care. | ASAM/AAAP 2024 |
| Longer-term treatment | UPDATE | **Contingency management is the best-supported/standard-of-care psychosocial intervention** for stimulant-use disorder; CBT and other behavioural approaches can be added. There is no single universally approved pharmacotherapy; selected medications may be used off-label by appropriately experienced clinicians. | ASAM/AAAP 2024; WHO mhGAP 2023 |

## Q43. Benzodiazepine dependence and withdrawal

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gradual taper | KEEP | Do not abruptly stop benzodiazepines in a physically dependent patient. Standard management is a **gradual, collaborative, individualised taper**, adjusted to withdrawal symptoms, indication, duration/dose, comorbidity and patient goals. | Joint ASAM-led Benzodiazepine Tapering Guideline 2025 |
| Severe withdrawal | KEEP | Severe benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause **seizures, delirium, severe autonomic symptoms and psychiatric destabilisation**. Patients with anticipated/actual complicated withdrawal or major safety/medical/psychiatric risks may require inpatient or medically managed residential care. | ASAM Joint Guideline 2025 |
| Diazepam/long-acting substitution | UPDATE | Conversion to a longer-acting benzodiazepine such as diazepam is **optional/situation-dependent**, not mandatory. The ASAM guideline states clinicians *can consider* transition in suitable patients; hepatic impairment and other factors may favour keeping or using a shorter-acting agent such as lorazepam/oxazepam. | ASAM Joint Guideline 2025; BAP substance-dependence consensus 2026 |
| Taper-rate anchor | ADD | A modern useful starting principle is **5–10% dose reduction every 2–4 weeks**, with the taper typically not exceeding **25% every 2 weeks**. Long-term/high-dose users or patients with substantial withdrawal may need reductions as slow as 5–10% every 6–8 weeks or slower. These are starting ranges, not rigid rules. | ASAM Joint Guideline 2025 |
| Fixed Ashton / “1/8 every 2 weeks” schedule | DROP | Do not freeze classic Ashton-style or “reduce by one-eighth every 2 weeks” schedules as universal practice. Modern guidance emphasises symptom-guided individualisation, pauses and slower reductions near the end when necessary. | ASAM Joint Guideline 2025 |
| Psychological support | ADD | Treat the underlying anxiety/insomnia and offer CBT/CBT-I or other indicated behavioural support during tapering; first response to troublesome withdrawal is usually to **pause or slow the taper**, not automatically add multiple medications. | ASAM Joint Guideline 2025 |

## Q44. Gambling disorder

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-11 classification | UPDATE | Gambling disorder is classified under **Disorders due to addictive behaviours**, alongside gaming disorder, rather than as an impulse-control disorder. | WHO ICD-11 addictive-behaviour framework |
| Core diagnosis | KEEP | ICD-11’s core pattern is **impaired control over gambling**, **increasing priority** given to gambling over other activities, and **continuation/escalation despite negative consequences**, with clinically significant impairment. | WHO gambling fact sheet 2024 |
| Mania differential | KEEP | Gambling that occurs only as part of a manic/hypomanic episode should prompt bipolar assessment rather than automatic gambling-disorder diagnosis. Persistent gambling pathology outside mood episodes can coexist with bipolar disorder. | ICD/DSM differential logic; NICE NG248 comorbidity framework |
| CBT | UPDATE | **Gambling-specific CBT is core first-line treatment.** NICE 2025 recommends group CBT, with individual CBT when group treatment is unsuitable/unwanted, and relapse-prevention content. Motivational interviewing can help strengthen commitment/readiness. | NICE NG248 §§1.5.12–1.5.15 |
| Financial-harm management | ADD | A competent answer should include practical harm reduction: assess debt/financial crisis, safeguarding/dependants, access to funds, self-exclusion/limit-setting tools, and links to financial/social/legal support where needed. | NICE NG248; WHO gambling fact sheet |
| Pharmacotherapy | UPDATE | Pharmacotherapy is **not first-line**, but it is no longer accurate to say “there is no medication option.” NICE 2025 recommends considering **naltrexone** under specialist supervision after an adequate psychological treatment has failed or repeated relapses occur; this is an **off-label** use in the UK. | NICE NG248 §§1.5.16–1.5.19 |
| Suicide risk | ADD | Gambling-related harm has a clinically important association with **suicidal ideation/behaviour**. Risk assessment should be explicit when there is severe debt, shame, relationship/legal crisis, depression or escalating gambling. Avoid freezing epidemiological percentages in the board answer. | NICE NG248; WHO gambling fact sheet 2024 |

## Genuine exam/current splits worth preserving

- **Q37 classification:** ICD-10 “harmful use + dependence syndrome” remains recognisable exam-source language, but current ICD-11 adds single harmful episodes, harmful patterns, hazardous use and simplifies dependence requirements.
- **Q37a smoking/clozapine:** older teaching sometimes attributes the interaction loosely to “smoking/nicotine”; current practice must state **tobacco-smoke PAHs induce CYP1A2, not nicotine**, so NRT/vaping do not maintain the induction.
- **Q38 alcohol withdrawal:** symptom-triggered regimens are not universally preferred; current guidance ties them to inpatient settings with trained staff and frequent monitoring, while fixed-dose remains appropriate in community withdrawal.
- **Q38a Wernicke:** the complete triad is neither required nor common enough to wait for. Current practice is immediate parenteral thiamine on suspicion.
- **Q40 OUD:** old detoxification-centred framing should yield to maintenance/OAT as the evidence-based standard for many patients; detox alone carries loss-of-tolerance overdose risk.
- **Q40 Greek naloxone:** since July 2025, Greek access expanded materially—physicians of any specialty may prescribe intranasal or prefilled injectable naloxone, private pharmacies may dispense it, and lay administration is permitted in overdose emergencies.
- **Q42 cocaine/beta-blockers:** the historic blanket contraindication is outdated. Acute management still prioritises benzodiazepines/vasodilators, but combined alpha/beta blockers can be considered when clinically necessary.
- **Q43 benzodiazepines:** fixed Ashton-style schedules are historical templates, not modern universal standards; the contemporary approach is patient-specific, slow and adjustable.
- **Q44 gambling:** ICD-11 places gambling under addictive behaviours, and NICE 2025 now gives a defined second-line specialist role to off-label naltrexone after psychological-treatment failure/relapse.
