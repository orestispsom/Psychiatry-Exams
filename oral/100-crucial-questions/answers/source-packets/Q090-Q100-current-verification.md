## Q90. Cognitive behavioural therapy

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core CBT architecture | KEEP | CBT remains **structured, collaborative, formulation-based and goal-oriented**. Collaborative empiricism, explicit links among situations/appraisals/emotions/physiology/behaviour, agreed goals, between-session practice and relapse prevention remain defensible core teaching. | NICE disorder-specific CBT guidance; Oxford base map |
| Core techniques | KEEP | **Behavioural activation, graded/in-vivo exposure, cognitive restructuring and behavioural experiments** remain high-yield techniques. Technique choice is formulation/disorder-specific: exposure should not be reduced to simple habituation alone, because inhibitory/new-learning models are also current. | NICE depression/anxiety/OCD/PTSD guidance |
| “Third wave” therapies | UPDATE | Do not bolt a generic “third-wave” section onto the answer. **Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)** has a clear current guideline role, particularly for relapse prevention in recurrent/higher-relapse-risk depression. ACT has evidence across several conditions but is not necessary to a concise general CBT viva answer unless the examiner asks about contextual/third-wave approaches. | NICE NG222 |
| Digital CBT | ADD | Supported digital CBT/guided self-help is now a legitimate delivery mode in current guidance for selected disorders and severity levels. It should be described as **structured evidence-based treatment with appropriate support/selection**, not as an app automatically equivalent to full therapist-delivered CBT for every patient. | NICE NG222; NICE digital-health guidance |

### Q90 adjudication note
Oxford’s CBT spine remains current. The only learner-facing update worth adding is that MBCT and supported digital CBT are established contemporary extensions; neither requires a broad “third-wave therapies” detour.

## Q91 / Q91a / Q91b. Transference, countertransference, splitting; supportive versus exploratory work

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transference | KEEP | Transference remains a useful clinical/formulation concept: expectations, affects and relational templates from prior relationships may be re-enacted in the therapeutic relationship. It is a **hypothesis-generating formulation concept**, not a directly measurable fact that overrides the patient’s account. | Contemporary psychodynamic/clinical practice; Oxford map |
| Countertransference | UPDATE | Clinician reactions can provide useful information about the therapeutic relationship, but they should be used **reflectively and tentatively**, checked against the wider formulation and the clinician’s own vulnerabilities. Drop any implication that “objective countertransference” gives privileged truth about the patient. | Contemporary psychodynamic practice / supervision principles |
| Team splitting | KEEP | When teams become polarised into rescuing versus rejecting/punitive positions, management is **shared formulation, regular communication, consistent boundaries/care plans, supervision/reflective practice and avoidance of retaliatory responses or special exceptions**. Do not simply blame the patient for “splitting the team.” | Personality-disorder/team practice principles |
| Supportive psychotherapy | KEEP | Supportive work emphasises **stabilisation, containment, coping, reality testing, problem-solving and preservation/improvement of functioning**, often with a more active therapist stance. | Stable psychotherapy practice |
| Exploratory work | KEEP | Interpretive/exploratory work requires sufficient **psychological stability, reflective capacity, impulse control, alliance and ability to tolerate affect/ambiguity**. Degree of support versus exploration can shift over time; they are not mutually exclusive schools in every clinical encounter. | Stable psychotherapy practice |

## Q95. Dopamine pathways

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mesolimbic | KEEP | VTA → limbic/ventral-striatal projections: reward/incentive salience; increased presynaptic striatal dopamine signalling is strongly implicated in psychosis/positive symptoms. | Stable psychopharmacology / modern schizophrenia neurobiology |
| Mesocortical | KEEP | VTA → prefrontal projections: cognition/motivation. The classic “relative cortical hypodopaminergia contributes to negative/cognitive symptoms” model remains useful pedagogically but should not be presented as a complete causal theory. | Stable psychopharmacology |
| Nigrostriatal | KEEP | Substantia nigra → dorsal striatum: motor function; D2 blockade explains major **EPS** risk. | Stable psychopharmacology |
| Tuberoinfundibular | KEEP | Hypothalamus → pituitary: dopamine tonically suppresses prolactin; D2 blockade can produce **hyperprolactinaemia**. | Stable psychopharmacology |
| Dopamine hypothesis caveat | ADD | Schizophrenia is **not “too much dopamine everywhere.”** Current evidence is circuit- and stage-specific and interacts with glutamatergic, developmental and broader network mechanisms. Preserve the four pathways as a clinical pharmacology model, not total disease biology. | Modern schizophrenia evidence |

## Q96. Heritability, familial and polygenic risk

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heritability | KEEP | Heritability estimates describe **variation in a population under particular environmental conditions**; they do not mean “X% of this individual’s illness is genetic” and do not determine an individual outcome. | Quantitative-genetics principles |
| Familial risk | KEEP | Familial aggregation reflects **shared genetic liability plus environmental/developmental factors**; family history is clinically informative but is not a Mendelian prediction for most adult psychiatric disorders. | Psychiatric genetics consensus |
| Polygenicity | KEEP | Major psychiatric disorders are usually **highly polygenic**: large numbers of common variants each confer very small effects. | ISPG genetic-testing statement |
| Rare variants/CNVs | KEEP | Rare pathogenic variants/CNVs can have substantially larger effects and may be clinically important in selected neurodevelopmental/psychiatric presentations or families. They explain only a minority of ordinary adult psychiatric illness. | ISPG genetic-testing statement |
| Pleiotropy/genetic overlap | ADD | Shared genetic liability across diagnostic boundaries is established; pleiotropy helps explain genetic correlations among schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression and neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Do not imply that current categories map to isolated genetic architectures. | Psychiatric genomic studies/consensus |
| Polygenic risk scores | UPDATE | **PRS are not routine stand-alone diagnostic or prognostic tests in ordinary psychiatric practice.** The International Society of Psychiatric Genetics continues to describe psychiatric PRS as research tools rather than recommended clinical tests for identifying/diagnosing high-risk individuals. | ISPG statements |

## Q97. Critical appraisal

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxford framework | KEEP | The Oxford viva framework remains current: define **PICO/question**, identify study design, comparator and population; assess randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding, attrition/missing data and intention-to-treat; examine effect size and precision; then applicability and harms. | Standard evidence-based medicine / CONSORT principles |
| Relative vs absolute effect | KEEP | Separate relative measures from **absolute risk difference**, and use NNT/NNH when clinically interpretable. A statistically significant relative effect can still have small absolute benefit. | Evidence-based medicine principles |
| Confidence intervals | KEEP | Confidence intervals convey **precision and clinically plausible effect range**; do not reduce appraisal to whether p<0.05. | Evidence-based medicine principles |
| Statistical vs clinical significance | KEEP | Statistical significance does not establish clinical importance. Consider magnitude, baseline risk, patient-valued outcomes, adverse effects and treatment burden. | Evidence-based medicine principles |
| Bias/confounding/generalisation | KEEP | Examine selection/performance/detection/attrition bias, confounding where relevant, multiplicity, outcome switching, external validity, harms and funding/conflicts. | CONSORT/STROBE-style principles |
| One modern addition | ADD | Ask whether the trial/protocol was **prospectively registered and outcomes/analyses were prespecified**. Discrepancy between registration/protocol and publication is a practical viva clue for selective reporting. | Current trial-reporting standards |

## Q98. Confidentiality in Greece

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core Greek duty | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Replace generic/UK confidentiality law with **Greek Law 3418/2005 (Code of Medical Ethics), Article 13**. The physician owes strict confidentiality for information learned from the patient or third parties in the course of professional duties concerning the patient or close persons. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13 |
| Continuing duty | KEEP | Confidentiality continues after the doctor stops practising and **does not end with the patient’s death**. The physician must also supervise collaborators/assistants and take measures to protect confidentiality. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13(2), (6) |
| Patient-authorised disclosure | KEEP | Confidentiality may be lifted with **valid consent of the person concerned**, subject to the Code’s validity/human-dignity qualifications. Consent should be specific enough for the disclosure contemplated. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13(4) |
| Disclosure required by law | UPDATE | Article 13 expressly permits disclosure when fulfilling a **legal duty**, whether imposed by special law (the Code gives examples such as births/deaths/notifiable disease) or general law. The learner should not invent a broad “public interest” power where a specific reporting statute governs. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13(3)(a) |
| Serious protected interests / necessity | ADD | Article 13 also permits lifting confidentiality to protect a **legal or other justified substantial public interest, or a substantial interest of the doctor/another person that cannot otherwise be protected**, and where a state of necessity or defence exists. This is the central Greek basis for proportionate disclosure to prevent sufficiently serious harm when no narrower route can protect the interest. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13(3)(b)–(c); Penal Code art. 25 |
| Penal consequences | ADD | Penal Code **Article 371** separately criminalises unauthorised disclosure of professional secrets by doctors and other specified professionals. Its justification framework also recognises fulfilment of duty/protection of a justified substantial interest that cannot otherwise be protected. This reinforces that disclosure must have a defensible legal basis rather than mere clinical convenience. | Penal Code art. 371; current codification |
| Relatives | UPDATE | **Relatives do not have an automatic entitlement to a competent living adult’s confidential psychiatric information merely because they are family.** Share with consent or another lawful basis; clinicians may still receive collateral information without promising reciprocal disclosure. After death, confidentiality remains, although separate record-access rules may give certain heirs/relatives rights in defined circumstances. | Law 3418/2005 arts. 13–14; MoH GDPR guidance |
| Treating team | UPDATE | Necessary information may be processed/shared within the **legitimate treating-care system** for diagnosis/treatment under health-care legal bases and professional confidentiality safeguards. Keep access need-to-know and purpose-limited; “the team” is not a licence for unrestricted circulation. | GDPR art. 9(2)(h); Law 4624/2019 art. 22; Law 3418/2005 art. 13 |
| GDPR / Law 4624/2019 | ADD | Health data are a **special category of personal data**. Greek Law 4624/2019 Article 22 permits necessary health-data processing for diagnosis/care/system management under specified safeguards and professional secrecy. Do not confuse GDPR consent with clinical consent: routine care generally does **not** require GDPR consent as the processing basis. | Hellenic DPA; MoH GDPR guidance |
| Minimum necessary disclosure | UPDATE | “Minimum necessary” is best taught as a **necessity/proportionality/data-minimisation principle**, not as verbatim wording of Article 13. If disclosure is justified, disclose only information needed for the protective/legal purpose and to the appropriate recipient. | GDPR data-minimisation; Article 13 necessity structure |
| Documentation | ADD | Document the threat/issue, legal/ethical basis considered, alternatives attempted, advice sought when feasible, what was disclosed, to whom and why. This is strong risk-management/professional practice; do **not** misquote it as a specific sentence of Article 13. | Professional risk-management principle |

### Q98 adjudication note
The board answer should lead with **Article 13 of Law 3418/2005**, not a foreign confidentiality framework. GDPR governs processing/security/lawful basis but does not replace the physician’s substantive duty of medical confidentiality.

## Q98a. Credible imminent threat of violence

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical assessment | KEEP | Assess **identified/identifiable target, specificity, intent, planning, means/access, imminence, past violence, intoxication/psychosis, escalating circumstances and protective factors**. Act to reduce access/opportunity and intensify treatment/support where possible; document the formulation and response. | Violence-risk clinical practice |
| Greek “Tarasoff duty” | DROP | Do **not** teach a US-style categorical “Tarasoff duty to warn” as Greek law. No standalone Greek rule equivalent to Tarasoff was identified in the current statutory/professional sources reviewed. | Greek Medical Code/Penal Code review |
| Planned felony reporting duty | ADD | There is, however, a **specific Greek reporting duty**: Penal Code Article 232 penalises failure to report promptly to the authorities when a person reliably learns that a **felony is being planned or has begun** at a time when its commission/result can still be prevented, if it is later committed or attempted. Article 13(3)(a) of the Medical Code expressly cites this type of legal duty as a basis for lifting confidentiality. | Penal Code art. 232; Law 3418/2005 art. 13(3)(a) |
| Other serious imminent harm | UPDATE | A serious threat that does not cleanly engage Article 232 may still justify disclosure under Article 13(3)(b)–(c) where a substantial protected interest **cannot otherwise be safeguarded**, or under necessity principles. This is context-dependent and proportionate, not a blanket obligation to disclose every threat. | Law 3418/2005 art. 13; Penal Code art. 25; art. 371 justification framework |
| Recipient / warning | UNRESOLVED | Article 232 specifies reporting to **the authorities**; it does not create a general statutory rule that the psychiatrist must personally warn the threatened individual. Whether direct victim notification is required in a particular non-Article-232 scenario was **not cleanly established** by an authoritative Greek Tarasoff-equivalent rule in this pass. | Current Greek statutory review |
| Practical response | ADD | If disclosure is justified/required, choose the route most likely to reduce the danger (for example emergency services/police/other relevant service, and direct warning only where legally/clinically justified), disclose **only what is necessary**, and seek senior/legal advice when feasible without delaying urgent protective action. | Article 13 proportionality + clinical risk management |

## Q99. Greek criminal responsibility

| Point | Decision | Current concise finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing test | EXAM_CURRENT_SPLIT | Use **Greek Penal Code Article 34**, not M’Naghten or another imported insanity test. An act is not imputable when, because of mental/intellectual disorder or disturbance of consciousness **at the time of the act**, the person lacked capacity either **to understand the wrongfulness (`άδικο`) of the act or to act according to that understanding**. | Penal Code art. 34, Law 4619/2019, current codification |
| Cognitive and volitional limbs | KEEP | Greek law therefore contains both a **cognitive limb** (appreciating wrongfulness) and a **volitional/control limb** (capacity to act in accordance with that appreciation). Either may ground Article 34 if absent because of the qualifying condition. | Penal Code art. 34 |
| Diagnosis is insufficient | KEEP | **Mental disorder alone does not equal criminal irresponsibility.** The disorder/disturbance must have produced the legally relevant incapacity at the material time. Supreme Court jurisprudence describes this as a mixed biological–psychological/valuative test rather than diagnosis alone. | Penal Code art. 34; Areios Pagos 507/2022 |
| Retrospective/offence-specific | KEEP | The assessment is **retrospective and offence-specific**: reconstruct mental state, symptoms, cognition/volition and behaviour at the time of the alleged act using records, collateral, contemporaneous behaviour and examination. Current mental state may inform but cannot substitute for the historical question. | Article 34 structure / forensic practice |
| Diminished responsibility | ADD | Penal Code **Article 36** covers substantially reduced capacity: if an Article 34 state did not abolish but **significantly reduced** capacity for responsibility, a reduced sentence under Article 83 applies. This is not the UK partial defence of diminished responsibility; it is the Greek general reduced-imputability provision. | Penal Code art. 36 |
| Self-induced incapacity / intoxication | ADD | Article 35 limits reliance on culpably self-induced incapacity. Ordinary intoxication therefore does not automatically produce Article 34 irresponsibility; causation, degree of consciousness disturbance and the Article 35 rules matter. Keep this as an examiner follow-up, not a full intoxication-law section. | Penal Code art. 35 |
| Separate legal questions | ADD | Distinguish **criminal responsibility** from psychiatric diagnosis, present treatment capacity, dangerousness/risk, and fitness/ability to participate in proceedings. They ask different questions and use different legal/clinical evidence. | Forensic psychiatry principles / Greek legal structure |
| Role of psychiatrist | KEEP | The forensic psychiatrist provides expert evidence on psychopathology and functional capacities; **the legal conclusion ultimately belongs to the court**. Avoid wording that the psychiatrist “decides” guilt or criminal responsibility. | Forensic practice / judicial role |

## Genuine exam-answer versus current-practice splits to preserve

1. **Q90 CBT:** classic CBT remains the core answer; MBCT and supported digital CBT are contemporary guideline-supported extensions. Do not replace CBT with a generic “third wave” survey.
2. **Q91 countertransference:** older “objective countertransference” language should be softened. Clinician reactions are reflective/formulation data, not objective proof about the patient.
3. **Q93 family intervention:** evidence-based and recommended to be **offered** in schizophrenia/bipolar contexts; do not translate guideline recommendations into compulsory family participation.
4. **Q95 dopamine:** retain the four-pathway model but explicitly reject “schizophrenia = too much dopamine everywhere.”
5. **Q96 PRS:** strong genetic/polygenic evidence does **not** mean routine PRS diagnosis/prognosis is clinically established.
6. **Q98 confidentiality:** replace UK/common-law framing with Greek **Law 3418/2005 Article 13**, plus relevant Penal Code/data-protection provisions.
7. **Q98a threat:** do not import **Tarasoff**. Greek law has a narrower Article 232 duty to report a preventable planned/ongoing felony to authorities, alongside Article 13 substantial-interest/necessity exceptions.
8. **Q99 criminal responsibility:** replace M’Naghten-style foreign language with Greek Penal Code Article 34: incapacity to understand wrongfulness **or act according to that understanding**; Article 36 covers substantially reduced capacity.
9. **Q100 system:** replace legacy sector-only service organisation with the post-2024 **Ε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ. / seven Πε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ.** architecture while retaining community Mental Health Centres and Mobile Units as service components.

## Unresolved issues

1. **Direct warning of a threatened individual in Greece:** no authoritative current source reviewed established a standalone Tarasoff-equivalent statutory duty requiring a psychiatrist personally to warn a named victim. Article 232 clearly concerns timely reporting of a preventable planned/ongoing felony to **the authorities**; other disclosures depend on Article 13/necessity/proportionality and any specific applicable duty. The learner-facing answer should therefore avoid a categorical “duty to warn” formulation.
2. **Exact boundary of family/heir access after death:** Article 13 clearly states that confidentiality survives death and Article 14 contains record-access rules, but detailed succession/legitimate-interest scenarios are not necessary for Q98. Do not improvise a hierarchy beyond the statute/current data-protection rules.
3. **Social-media-specific Greek professional code:** no dedicated current Greek physician social-media rule was identified that materially adds to Article 13/GDPR. The packet uses WMA/AMA principles only for the online-boundary details and labels them as international professional guidance.
4. **Legacy sector (`ΤοΨΥ`) terminology after Law 5129/2024:** the current top-level administrative architecture is clearly Ε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ./Πε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ.; local historical/catchment terminology may persist in documents or practice. Do not make a categorical claim that every legacy term has disappeared from every regulation/service.
5. No unresolved issue blocks answer-brief/adjudication production.

## 11 current-practice facts the Writer must not get wrong

1. CBT remains structured, collaborative and formulation-based; behavioural activation, exposure, cognitive restructuring and behavioural experiments remain core. MBCT has a guideline role in depression relapse prevention.
2. Countertransference is useful **reflective data**, not objective truth about the patient; team polarisation is managed through communication, shared formulation, supervision and consistent boundaries.
3. Family intervention in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder is evidence-based and should be **offered** where relevant; it is not compulsory participation.
4. Psychiatric heritability is population-level, major disorders are highly polygenic, and current psychiatric PRS are **not routine stand-alone diagnostic/prognostic tests**.
5. Greek medical confidentiality is governed centrally by **Law 3418/2005 Article 13** and continues after the patient’s death.
6. Greek confidentiality may be lifted with valid consent, for a legal duty, to protect a justified substantial interest that cannot otherwise be protected, or in necessity/defence; disclosure should be proportionate to the purpose.
7. There is **no verified Greek Tarasoff-style general duty to warn**. Penal Code Article 232 instead creates a specific duty to report to authorities a reliably known planned/ongoing felony while it can still be prevented, if it is later committed/attempted.
8. Penal Code Article 371 makes unjustified professional-secret disclosure potentially criminal; confidentiality is therefore not merely etiquette.
9. Greek Penal Code Article 34 requires offence-time incapacity to understand the **wrongfulness** of the act **or to act according to that understanding**; diagnosis alone is insufficient.
10. Article 36 addresses **significantly reduced** capacity for criminal responsibility; responsibility, current capacity, dangerousness and fitness for proceedings are separate questions.
11. Since Law 5129/2024 the current Greek top-level mental-health system is **Ε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ. organised into seven Πε.Δ.Υ.Ψ.Υ. under the Υ.Πε.**, while ΚΨΥ, Mobile Mental Health Units and early-intervention/community structures continue within the network.
