## Q20. What are the clinical features, diagnosis and important differentials of major depressive disorder?

Examiner is asking: recognise the depressive syndrome comprehensively, diagnose it longitudinally, identify severe/psychotic patterns, and exclude bipolar, substance/medication and medical/neurological causes rather than equating depression with low mood.

Must cover:
- Begin with syndrome and impairment: persistent depressed mood and/or loss of interest/pleasure with clinically significant change in functioning.
- Organise phenomenology across affective, cognitive, biological/somatic and psychomotor domains.
- Affective: low mood, anhedonia, anxiety/irritability where present, reduced emotional reactivity.
- Cognitive: hopelessness/pessimism, guilt/worthlessness, impaired concentration/decision-making, recurrent thoughts of death/suicide; assess suicidal intent separately rather than merely naming suicidality.
- Biological/somatic: sleep disturbance, appetite/weight change, reduced energy/fatigue, reduced libido and diurnal variation where present.
- Psychomotor: retardation or agitation; severe retardation may progress to stupor.
- Psychotic depression: mood-congruent or mood-incongruent delusions/hallucinations may occur in severe depression; nihilistic/Cotard-type ideas are a classic severe-depression phenomenon. Psychotic depression materially changes urgency and treatment.
- Clinically useful patterns: melancholic features (marked anhedonia/lack of reactivity, biological/psychomotor pattern) and atypical phenomenology may be mentioned, but neither replaces the core depressive-episode diagnosis.
- Current classification anchor: ICD-11 depressive episode requires at least 5 of 10 symptoms for at least 2 weeks, with depressed mood or markedly diminished interest/pleasure among them, clinically significant impairment and appropriate exclusions.
- Terminology: “major depressive disorder” is DSM terminology; ICD-11 uses single episode depressive disorder or recurrent depressive disorder according to longitudinal course. Do not mix labels as if they are identical systems.
- Before concluding unipolar depression, actively seek lifetime mania/hypomania, mixed manic symptoms, collateral evidence and treatment-emergent episodes; severe/recurrent/psychotic depression alone does not prove bipolarity.
- Differential: bipolar depression; adjustment/bereavement-related presentations; substance/medication-induced depression; endocrine/metabolic/neurological and other medical causes; depressive symptoms secondary to other psychiatric disorders where clinically relevant.
- Diagnosis should integrate history, MSE, physical/neurological assessment and targeted investigations according to presentation rather than rely on symptom count alone.

Recall spine: syndrome + impairment → affective/cognitive/biological/psychomotor → suicide/psychosis → current diagnostic anchor → bipolar screen → secondary causes/differential → longitudinal diagnosis.

Must-know board facts:
- Current ICD-11 depressive episode: ≥5/10 symptoms, ≥2 weeks, with depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure required.
- “MDD” is DSM terminology; ICD-11 uses single episode/recurrent depressive disorder.
- Cotard-type nihilistic delusions are a classic severe psychotic-depression phenomenon.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- If older exam teaching uses the ICD-10 “2 of 3 core symptoms” hierarchy, that is historical/exam-source framing. Do not present it as current ICD-11 diagnostic truth.

Common traps:
- Diagnosing depression from sadness alone.
- Forgetting bipolar history before calling recurrent depression unipolar.
- Treating melancholic/atypical features as separate disorders.
- Missing psychosis, suicidality or medical/substance causes.

Deliberate exclusions: historical suicide percentages, recurrence percentages, full DSM criteria recital, treatment details beyond noting that psychotic depression changes management.

### Follow-up Q20a. How do you distinguish PMDD from premenstrual worsening of another disorder?

Answer points:
- The key distinction is an **on/off cyclical pattern**.
- PMDD/core premenstrual disorder: symptoms emerge in the luteal/premenstrual phase, improve within a few days after menstruation begins, and are minimal or absent during the post-menstrual follicular interval; functional impairment is required.
- Premenstrual exacerbation (PME): an underlying psychiatric or medical disorder is present across the cycle but worsens premenstrually.
- Persistent significant symptoms during the follicular phase therefore favour PME/comorbidity rather than pure PMDD.
- Retrospective recall is insufficient when diagnostic certainty matters. Prospective daily symptom ratings across **at least 2 consecutive symptomatic cycles** are the key confirmation method; DRSP may be mentioned as a common tool if useful.
- Do not turn the follow-up into a detailed PMDD treatment algorithm.

Follow-up recall spine: cyclical on/off → follicular symptom-free interval → persistent baseline disorder = PME → prospective charting ×2 cycles.

## Q21. How do you treat a major depressive episode?

Examiner is asking: choose treatment according to severity, risk, psychosis, previous response and patient preference; know when psychotherapy, medication, combination treatment and ECT become appropriate; and reassess intelligently before declaring treatment failure.

Must cover:
- Start by reassessing diagnosis and immediate clinical context: suicide risk, psychosis, catatonia/severe psychomotor retardation, self-neglect, nutrition/hydration, substance use, relevant medical causes, previous episodes/treatments, adherence and social support.
- Decide treatment setting according to risk and ability to remain safe, not diagnosis alone.
- Shared decision-making matters: severity, previous response, comorbidity, adverse-effect priorities, patient preference and treatment availability should determine modality.
- Current practice is **not** a rigid “mild = psychotherapy, moderate = antidepressant, severe = combination” ladder.
- Less-severe depression: evidence-based psychological/behavioural interventions are often preferred initially; antidepressants are not automatically first-line unless preferred or otherwise clinically indicated.
- More-severe depression: structured psychotherapy and antidepressant medication are both legitimate first-line choices; combined treatment is especially attractive with greater severity, chronicity, high safety risk or incomplete response.
- Psychological treatments should be evidence-based and structured; CBT is a high-yield example. Do not imply psychotherapy is merely an adjunct to drugs.
- Antidepressant selection should be individualised according to previous benefit, tolerability, overdose toxicity, interactions, comorbidity and patient preference. Do not write a class-by-class pharmacology chapter here.
- Psychotic depression: specialist care; generally consider antidepressant + antipsychotic. If an antipsychotic is declined, antidepressant monotherapy may still be used, but combination treatment is the high-yield answer.
- ECT: consider for severe depression when rapid response is required because the illness is life-threatening (e.g. not eating/drinking), when psychotic/catatonic severity makes rapid treatment important, when other treatments have failed, or when there was strong previous response/patient preference. Do not frame ECT only as a remote last resort.
- Early response review: if there is no/limited response, review at about **4 weeks at a recognised therapeutic dose**, first checking adherence, tolerability, diagnosis, psychosocial/medical contributors and whether the treatment was actually adequate. Four weeks is a reassessment checkpoint, not the definition of TRD.
- After non-response: switch when there is little/no benefit or poor tolerability; augment/add when there is meaningful partial response to a tolerated antidepressant. Psychological treatment can also be added or changed.
- Continuation: once remission is achieved, continue effective antidepressant treatment rather than stopping abruptly. A useful current anchor is **at least 6 months after remission** for moderate-to-severe depression, with longer relapse-prevention treatment individualised for recurrent/high-risk illness.
- Include relapse prevention, gradual discontinuation and follow-up without duplicating the later antidepressant adverse-effect/switching question.

Recall spine: reassess/risk/setting → shared modality choice → psychotherapy/antidepressant/combination → psychotic depression → ECT → 4-week review → switch vs augment → continuation/relapse prevention.

Must-know board facts:
- About 4 weeks at a therapeutic dose is a useful checkpoint for inadequate antidepressant response; it is not automatically TRD.
- Continue successful antidepressant treatment for at least 6 months after remission in moderate-to-severe depression, then individualise longer-term prevention.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- Older rigid stepped-care severity ladders are too categorical. Current guidance uses severity, risk, prior response, preference and access; psychotherapy and medication can both be first-line in appropriate cases.

Common traps:
- Starting treatment without checking bipolarity, psychosis or suicide risk.
- Waiting passively for many weeks despite zero response/tolerability problems.
- Calling 4-week nonresponse “treatment resistance.”
- Treating ECT as an absolute last resort in life-threatening/psychotic/catatonic depression.
- Stopping antidepressants immediately after remission.

Deliberate exclusions: exact antidepressant doses, exhaustive class comparison, detailed augmentation hierarchy (Q22).

## Q22. What do you do when depression is treatment-resistant?

Examiner is asking: define genuine TRD pragmatically, exclude pseudoresistance first, then move logically through switching/augmentation and major somatic options without turning the answer into a catalogue of every new intervention.

Must cover:
- Practical board definition: persistent clinically significant depression despite **at least two adequate antidepressant treatments in the current episode**.
- Introduce contemporary nuance: difficult-to-treat depression (DTD) is increasingly used to describe persistent burden across multiple reasonable treatments because binary “resistant/not resistant” language can obscure partial benefit, chronicity and the need for long-term management.
- Before escalating, exclude pseudoresistance:
  - wrong diagnosis, especially bipolar disorder;
  - inadequate dose/duration;
  - poor adherence or intolerable adverse effects;
  - substance use;
  - psychiatric comorbidity;
  - relevant medical illness/medications;
  - psychosocial adversity;
  - treatment not delivered competently or not actually completed.
- Reassess target symptoms and severity, including suicide risk, psychosis and catatonia.
- Strategic choice:
  - switch after essentially no response or problematic tolerability;
  - augment/add after meaningful partial response to a well-tolerated antidepressant.
- Pharmacological augmentation: atypical-antipsychotic augmentation is strongly evidence-based; aripiprazole may be used as a representative example. Lithium augmentation remains an established high-yield strategy. Do not give a universal ranking that erases guideline differences.
- ECT remains the most important somatic option for severe, urgent or highly refractory depression, particularly with psychosis, catatonia, severe suicidality or life-threatening refusal of food/fluids.
- rTMS is an established non-invasive treatment option for depression that has not responded adequately to standard treatment; it is not experimental fringe therapy.
- Esketamine: intranasal esketamine is **EU-authorised** with an SSRI/SNRI for adults with TRD after at least two antidepressant failures, under supervised administration/monitoring. However, NICE still does not recommend it for NHS use because of clinical/economic uncertainty; Greek access/reimbursement was not verified. Mention briefly as a specialist EU-licensed option, not as routine universal next-line treatment.
- IV racemic ketamine may have evidence internationally but is not an EU-authorised antidepressant indication; do not develop this unless needed for one sentence of distinction.
- Long-term DTD care should include functional recovery, relapse prevention, comorbidity treatment and patient goals rather than serial medication changes alone.

Recall spine: confirm 2 adequate failures → exclude pseudoresistance → reassess severity/diagnosis → switch vs augment → antipsychotic/lithium augmentation → ECT → rTMS → esketamine specialist option → long-term DTD formulation.

Must-know board facts:
- Conventional TRD anchor: failure of ≥2 adequate antidepressant treatments in the current episode.
- rTMS is established treatment, not experimental.
- Esketamine: EU authorised for TRD, but licensing does not equal universal guideline recommendation/access.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- Older exam answers may stop at switching/lithium/antipsychotic augmentation/ECT. Current practice adds rTMS as established and esketamine as an EU-licensed specialist option, while retaining uncertainty about routine access and guideline positioning.

Common traps:
- Calling inadequate treatment/nonadherence TRD.
- Missing bipolar disorder.
- Reflexive polypharmacy after zero response.
- Listing every emerging intervention.
- Saying “EU licensed” as though it means routinely available/reimbursed in Greece.

Deliberate exclusions: exact augmentation doses, lithium levels, NNTs, detailed ketamine protocols.

## Q23. How do you diagnose bipolar disorder and distinguish it from important alternatives?

Examiner is asking: diagnose bipolar disorder longitudinally from episodes and change from baseline, distinguish mania from hypomania, and separate bipolarity from ADHD, borderline affective instability, substances/medications and unipolar depression.

Must cover:
- Bipolar diagnosis is **longitudinal and episodic**. Do not diagnose from vague “mood swings.”
- Establish episode phenomenology: elevated/expansive or irritable mood plus increased energy/activity, reduced need for sleep, increased speech/pressure, racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, disinhibition/risk-taking, grandiosity; psychosis may occur in mania.
- Mania versus hypomania: the decisive distinction is severity and functional consequence, not symptom count alone. Mania causes marked impairment, may require hospitalisation and may include psychosis; hypomania is a clear episodic departure from baseline without the same marked impairment or psychosis.
- Current classification architecture:
  - Bipolar I: at least one manic episode.
  - Bipolar II: at least one hypomanic episode plus at least one depressive episode, with no history of mania.
- DSM duration facts may be mentioned as board facts if useful: mania ≥1 week or any duration if hospitalisation is required; hypomania ≥4 consecutive days. Do **not** present these as universal ICD-11 wording; ICD-11 uses “several days” for hypomania.
- Mixed features: current systems allow clinically important manic symptoms during depression and depressive symptoms during mania/hypomania without requiring the old full simultaneous “mixed episode.” Mixed features complicate diagnosis and increase caution with antidepressants.
- Bipolar versus unipolar depression: actively elicit lifetime hypomania/mania, episodicity, collateral history, family history, mixed symptoms and treatment-emergent activation. No single depressive phenotype establishes bipolarity.
- ADHD versus hypomania: ADHD is typically trait-like/persistent from development; hypomania/mania is a distinct episodic departure from baseline. Decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, clear mood/energy shift and psychosis favour bipolar disorder. Comorbidity can occur.
- Borderline personality affective instability: usually rapid/reactive to interpersonal/environmental events and embedded in a pervasive pattern of identity/interpersonal instability and impulsivity; bipolar disorder requires sustained mood episodes representing a clear baseline change. They can coexist.
- Substance/medication/medical differential: stimulants, corticosteroids, antidepressants and other drugs; intoxication/withdrawal; thyroid/neurological and other medical causes according to presentation.
- Psychotic differential: schizophrenia-spectrum illness is favoured when persistent psychotic syndrome is not organised around mood episodes; longitudinal chronology is critical.
- Antidepressant-emergent mania/hypomania: current ICD-11/DSM logic allows a primary manic/hypomanic episode to count when the full syndrome **persists beyond discontinuation and the direct physiological effects of the antidepressant have ceased/receded**. Brief activation confined to the direct drug effect does not automatically establish bipolar disorder.

Recall spine: episodic change from baseline → manic syndrome → mania vs hypomania → bipolar I vs II → mixed features → unipolar/ADHD/BPD/psychosis → substances/medical → antidepressant-emergent rule.

Must-know board facts:
- Bipolar I = ≥1 manic episode.
- Bipolar II = hypomania + depressive episode, no mania.
- DSM anchor: mania ≥1 week (or any duration if hospitalised); hypomania ≥4 days. ICD-11 wording is not identical.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- ICD-11 now explicitly distinguishes bipolar I and II; older ICD-10-era teaching is outdated.
- A full antidepressant-emergent syndrome that persists beyond direct drug effects can count toward primary bipolar diagnosis; “any antidepressant-associated mania = substance-induced” is too simplistic.

Common traps:
- Diagnosing bipolar disorder from ordinary mood variability.
- Calling chronic ADHD activation hypomania.
- Calling reactive BPD affective shifts bipolar episodes.
- Treating DSM duration wording as universal ICD wording.
- Assuming any antidepressant activation proves bipolar disorder.

## Q24. How do you treat acute mania?

Examiner is asking: demonstrate safe acute management—risk/setting, cessation of destabilising drugs, evidence-based antimanic treatment, appropriate use of combination therapy, and current valproate reproductive restrictions.

Must cover:
- Immediate assessment: risk to self/others, behavioural control, psychosis, agitation, sleep deprivation, substance use, capacity, medical/neurological causes, medication history and ability to adhere.
- Severe mania frequently requires urgent specialist/inpatient management; containment and physical assessment should proceed in parallel where safely possible.
- If mania/hypomania emerges while taking an antidepressant, stop/consider stopping the antidepressant as part of acute management.
- Core antimanic treatments are **antipsychotics, lithium and valproate/divalproex**, but guideline sequencing differs.
- Do not invent one universal hierarchy:
  - NICE pragmatically starts with an antipsychotic (examples include haloperidol, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone), then changes antipsychotic/steps onward before adding lithium.
  - CANMAT/ISBD places lithium, several atypical antipsychotics and divalproex among first-line monotherapies and also supports antipsychotic + lithium/divalproex combination as first-line.
- For the viva, give a principle-based answer: monotherapy can be reasonable for less severe mania; combination therapy (usually antipsychotic + lithium or valproate/divalproex) is appropriate early in more severe mania or when a faster/more robust response is required.
- Short-term benzodiazepines may be used adjunctively for severe agitation/insomnia while definitive antimanic treatment takes effect; they are not the core treatment.
- ECT remains an option for prolonged, severe, refractory or life-threatening mania.
- Treat contributing intoxication/withdrawal and medical causes; restore sleep, hydration/nutrition, reduce stimulation and involve carers where appropriate.
- **Valproate reproductive safety must be prominent whenever valproate is mentioned:**
  - it must not be used for bipolar disorder during pregnancy;
  - in girls/women able to become pregnant, it must not be used unless Pregnancy Prevention Programme conditions are met and alternatives are ineffective/not tolerated;
  - for men, current EU/Greek precautions require specialist review/counselling regarding a potential paternal-exposure neurodevelopmental signal, effective contraception for the man and female partner during treatment and for **at least 3 months after stopping**, and no sperm donation during that period.
- Do not give exact doses or detailed lithium/valproate monitoring here; those belong elsewhere.

Recall spine: risk/setting → stop antidepressant → antipsychotic/lithium/valproate → mono vs combination by severity → brief benzodiazepine adjunct → ECT if severe/refractory → valproate reproductive safety.

Must-know board facts:
- Combination antipsychotic + lithium/divalproex may be appropriate early in severe mania.
- Valproate is prohibited for bipolar disorder during pregnancy and requires current reproductive-safety precautions in both women able to conceive and men.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- Do not present an old fixed antimanic ranking as current universal truth; major guidelines differ in sequencing.
- Older routine valproate prescribing is no longer acceptable without the current EU reproductive-safety qualification.

Common traps:
- Treating agitation alone and forgetting the manic syndrome/medical differential.
- Continuing antidepressants uncritically during mania.
- Saying “valproate is first line” without reproductive restrictions.
- Using benzodiazepines as the definitive antimanic treatment.

Deliberate exclusions: exact doses, serum levels, rapid-tranquillisation protocol, detailed pregnancy pharmacotherapy beyond valproate restriction.

## Q25. How do you treat bipolar depression?

Examiner is asking: show that bipolar depression is not ordinary unipolar depression, know the core evidence-based agents, understand the limited role of antidepressants, and distinguish international evidence from EU licensing.

Must cover:
- Reassess diagnosis, bipolar subtype, current mixed/manic symptoms, suicide risk, psychosis/catatonia, medication adherence, substance use, previous treatment response and maintenance needs.
- Bipolar depression should **not** be managed as ordinary unipolar depression.
- For a European-oriented board answer, high-value core options are **quetiapine, lithium and lamotrigine**, selected according to bipolar subtype, previous response, polarity, severity and maintenance needs.
- NICE also supports olanzapine–fluoxetine as a treatment option for moderate/severe bipolar depression; mention only if useful, not as mandatory hierarchy.
- Lamotrigine is especially relevant to depressive polarity/maintenance but is not an acute antimanic drug.
- International guideline nuance: CANMAT/ISBD includes cariprazine and lurasidone-based strategies among first-line bipolar-I depression options. However, current EU product information for **Reagila (cariprazine)** and **Latuda (lurasidone)** remains schizophrenia-only. They may be mentioned as international evidence but must not be presented as routine EU/Greek licensed bipolar-depression treatments.
- Antidepressants are not the backbone of bipolar-I depression treatment.
- **Do not recommend antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I.** If an antidepressant is used, it should be adjunctive to an established mood-stabilising treatment and reserved for selected patients.
- Avoid/caution antidepressants with previous antidepressant-induced mania, mixed features or recent rapid cycling; educate about switch warning signs and stop the antidepressant if mania emerges.
- ECT is appropriate for refractory bipolar depression and should be considered earlier when rapid response is required because of severe suicidality, psychosis, catatonia or medical compromise from poor intake.
- Psychological intervention, sleep/circadian regularity, substance management and relapse planning are useful adjuncts but should not dominate the acute-treatment answer.

Recall spine: confirm bipolar depression/mixed risk → quetiapine/lithium/lamotrigine core → selected alternatives → antidepressants only cautiously/adjunctively → no bipolar-I antidepressant monotherapy → ECT for urgent/refractory illness → licensing caveat for newer agents.

Must-know board facts:
- Antidepressant monotherapy should not be recommended for bipolar-I depression.
- Cariprazine/lurasidone have international bipolar-depression evidence but are not currently EU-licensed for bipolar depression.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- International CANMAT/ISBD recommendations and EU product licensing are not identical. Do not convert international evidence into a claim of routine European licensing.

Common traps:
- Treating bipolar depression exactly like MDD.
- Antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I.
- Presenting cariprazine/lurasidone as EU-approved bipolar-depression drugs.
- Forgetting mixed features/switch risk.

Deliberate exclusions: exact doses, full drug-by-drug hierarchy, detailed maintenance treatment (Q26).

## Q26. How do you manage bipolar disorder long term?

Examiner is asking: integrate maintenance pharmacotherapy with relapse prevention, adherence, psychoeducation, physical health, functional recovery and reproductive safety; choose maintenance according to previous response and polarity rather than reciting one fixed drug list.

Must cover:
- Establish a shared longitudinal plan: relapse signatures, episode polarity, triggers, sleep/circadian regularity, adherence, substance use, suicide risk, occupational/social recovery and carer/family needs.
- Bipolar disorder is generally recurrent and maintenance is commonly long term, often indefinite after recurrent/severe episodes. Do not give a single universal stopping date.
- **Lithium remains a central first-line maintenance treatment** and NICE describes it as the most effective long-term pharmacological treatment for bipolar disorder. Selection still depends on previous response, tolerability, polarity and patient preference.
- Other established maintenance options include quetiapine, lamotrigine, valproate/divalproex and selected antipsychotics, depending on previous response and relapse polarity.
- Phase specificity matters: **lamotrigine is useful for prevention of depressive relapse but is not an acute antimanic drug.**
- Consider the polarity of previous episodes/predominant polarity and previous treatment response rather than assuming every maintenance agent is equally useful for mania and depression prevention.
- Psychological/behavioural care is core: psychoeducation, adherence work, relapse-signature recognition, family/CBT-informed interventions where appropriate, regular sleep/routine and substance-use management.
- Physical health: monitor weight/BMI, metabolic/cardiovascular risk and drug-specific adverse effects; smoking/substance-use intervention matters. Do not duplicate the later dedicated lithium-monitoring answer.
- Lithium and suicide prevention: lithium has evidence suggesting reduction in suicide attempts/deaths and this is a useful additional reason to favour it in suitable patients; do not quote an exact percentage or claim complete protection.
- If discontinuation is considered, individualise according to episode number/severity, psychosis, consequences, residual symptoms, relapse pattern, adverse effects and preference; withdrawal should be gradual.
- **Valproate reproductive restrictions:** apply the same current EU pregnancy prohibition/PPP requirements and male paternal-exposure precautions described in Q24. A long-term answer that simply lists valproate without this qualification is unacceptable current practice.

Recall spine: shared relapse plan → lithium central → alternatives by polarity/previous response → psychoeducation/adherence/sleep/substances → physical health → suicide prevention → duration/gradual withdrawal → valproate reproductive safety.

Must-know board facts:
- Lithium is a central first-line maintenance treatment.
- Lamotrigine is useful for depressive-relapse prevention but not acute mania.
- Long-term bipolar care is not medication-only.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- Older maintenance answers that list valproate without reproductive qualification are outdated; current EU restrictions must be attached to its use.

Common traps:
- Treating maintenance as “continue the acute drug forever” without polarity/previous-response reasoning.
- Forgetting psychoeducation/adherence/physical health.
- Overloading this answer with detailed lithium laboratory intervals.
- Listing valproate without current reproductive warnings.

## Q27. How do you recognise and manage postpartum psychosis?

Examiner is asking: recognise a rapidly evolving postpartum psychiatric emergency, assess maternal and infant safety, understand its bipolar-spectrum relationship, and initiate urgent specialist/inpatient treatment with perinatal planning.

Must cover:
- Postpartum psychosis is a **psychiatric emergency**.
- Timing: usually sudden onset in the immediate postpartum period, commonly within the **first 2 weeks after childbirth**.
- Phenomenology is often rapidly fluctuating and may combine mania/mixed symptoms, severe depression, insomnia, agitation, delusions/hallucinations, confusion/disorganisation and behavioural disturbance.
- It is strongly associated with the **bipolar spectrum**; do not conceptualise it simply as schizophrenia occurring after childbirth.
- Immediate assessment must include suicide risk, risk to the infant, ability to care safely, severe agitation, psychosis, catatonia, confusion/delirium and relevant obstetric/medical/substance causes.
- Obtain collateral information urgently and involve obstetric/perinatal services as appropriate.
- Urgent specialist assessment and **inpatient treatment** are generally required. Where available, admission to a specialist **mother-and-baby unit (MBU)** is preferred so maternal treatment and infant safety/bonding can be managed together.
- Acute pharmacological treatment: antipsychotic treatment is usually required; lithium should be added/used where clinically appropriate, especially given the bipolar-spectrum relationship and relapse-prevention evidence. Short-term benzodiazepines may be used for severe agitation/insomnia when necessary.
- ECT should be considered **early** when rapid response is required, particularly with severe/refractory illness, catatonia, severe agitation or suicidal/infanticidal risk. Do not frame it only as a last resort.
- Breastfeeding decisions require drug-specific risk–benefit assessment incorporating maternal relapse risk, infant exposure and infant medical vulnerability. Do not make blanket “safe in breastfeeding” claims. NICE does not recommend breastfeeding while taking lithium, clozapine or carbamazepine.
- Relapse prevention: lithium has the strongest evidence for prevention after postpartum psychosis and is an important prophylactic option in women with a previous episode.
- Future pregnancies require preconception specialist perinatal planning and an explicit antenatal/postpartum relapse-prevention plan; prophylaxis immediately after delivery may be appropriate in high-risk women.
- Do not quote old recurrence percentages.

Recall spine: emergency + first 2 weeks → fluctuating affective/psychotic syndrome → bipolar link → maternal/infant safety + medical differential → urgent inpatient/MBU → antipsychotic ± lithium → early ECT if severe → breastfeeding-specific assessment → future prophylaxis planning.

Must-know board facts:
- Postpartum psychosis commonly starts abruptly within the first 2 weeks postpartum.
- It is strongly linked to bipolar-spectrum illness.
- MBU admission is preferred where available.
- ECT may be an early treatment when rapid response is needed.

Common traps:
- Calling it “postpartum schizophrenia.”
- Missing infant-safety assessment.
- Treating it as routine outpatient depression.
- Ignoring medical/obstetric causes of confusion/psychosis.
- Making blanket breastfeeding-safety statements.

### Follow-up Q27a. How does postpartum depression differ and how is it managed?

Answer points:
- Postpartum depression is a depressive syndrome in the postpartum period, without the defining psychotic/manic/disorganised emergency picture of postpartum psychosis.
- Distinguish from maternity blues: blues are short-lived, mild and self-limiting; persistent clinically significant depressive symptoms, impairment, suicidality or inability to function/care require assessment for postpartum depression.
- Assess severity, suicide risk, infant safety, functioning, bonding/care context, supports, bipolar history and psychotic symptoms.
- Treatment broadly follows evidence-based depression care but is modified by breastfeeding, previous response and maternal–infant context.
- Mild/moderate presentations: facilitated self-help or structured psychotherapy may be appropriate.
- Moderate/severe depression: high-intensity psychotherapy, antidepressant treatment, or combination treatment according to preference/history/risk; specialist perinatal input where needed.
- Severe/psychotic/catatonic or life-threatening depression may require inpatient care and ECT according to clinical need.
- Breastfeeding: make drug-specific risk–benefit decisions rather than broad class claims.
- Current European update: **zuranolone (Zurzuvae) received EU marketing authorisation in September 2025 for postpartum depression in adults following childbirth, as a 14-day oral course, alone or added to another antidepressant.** It is worth a concise board/current-practice note, but actual Greek commercial availability/reimbursement has not been verified and it should not displace established psychotherapy/SSRI-based pathways in the general answer.
- Do not import brexanolone as an equivalent routine European option.

Follow-up recall spine: depression vs blues vs psychosis → severity/safety/bipolar screen → psychotherapy/antidepressant/combination → perinatal/breastfeeding adaptation → zuranolone EU update.

Exam answer vs current practice:
- Zuranolone is no longer US-only; it has EU authorisation since September 2025. However, EU authorisation does not establish Greek availability/reimbursement.
