## Q01. How do you approach a new psychiatric patient and organise the initial assessment?

Examiner is asking: show that psychiatric assessment is structured, hypothesis-driven, safe, clinically broad and ends in formulation/plan rather than being a checklist recital.

Must cover:
- Three core purposes: establish diagnosis/syndrome; understand context and why this problem has arisen in this person now; establish a therapeutic relationship.
- Begin with immediate safety and setting: risk to self/others, medical instability, intoxication/withdrawal, delirium, agitation, need for urgent containment.
- History: presenting complaint and history of present condition; onset/course/severity/fluctuation; functional effect; psychiatric, medical and neurological history; medication including OTC/other agents; substance use; family history; developmental/personal/social/occupational/relationship history; forensic history when relevant.
- Collateral/informants are often essential, especially when insight, cognition, reliability or risk is uncertain.
- Mental-state examination: appearance/behaviour → speech → mood/affect → thought form/content → perception → cognition → insight.
- Physical and neurological examination are integral, especially in first presentations or suspected secondary causes; targeted investigations should follow the clinical picture rather than a universal battery.
- Explicit risk assessment must be included even in brief assessments.
- End with synthesis/formulation: problem list, differential, predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating factors across biological/psychological/social domains, treatment plan and prognosis.
- Assessment is iterative and hypothesis-testing; absence of expected symptoms is clinically informative.

Recall spine: safety/setting → history → collateral → MSE → physical/neuro → investigations → risk → formulation/plan.

Follow-up Q01a: A patient reports hearing a voice while fully awake. How do you distinguish a true hallucination from an illusion, an organic/substance-related phenomenon or a sensory-deprivation phenomenon?
Answer points:
- Hallucination = perception-like experience without an external stimulus; illusion = misperception/misinterpretation of a real external stimulus.
- Characterise modality, clarity, location, relation to external space, timing, triggers, level of consciousness, insight and associated symptoms.
- Delirium/organic clues: impaired attention or consciousness, disorientation, cognitive fluctuation, visual illusions/misinterpretations, acute onset, 24-hour fluctuation, sleep–wake disturbance and medical precipitants.
- Substance/medication clues: temporal relation to intoxication/withdrawal or exposure; do not infer causation from exposure alone.
- Sensory impairment/deprivation can produce hallucinations; ask about vision/hearing impairment and context.
- A single hallucination does not diagnose schizophrenia; interpret form, context and whole syndrome.

Follow-up Q01b: What do you mean by formal thought disorder, and how do the main abnormalities differ?
Answer points:
- Formal thought disorder concerns the organisation/flow/form of thinking as expressed in speech, not merely unusual thought content.
- Distinguish accelerated/pressured or flight-like associations, loosening of associations/derailment, tangentiality, circumstantiality, thought blocking, perseveration, incoherence/word salad and neologistic or idiosyncratic use where clinically present.
- Keep form separate from content: delusions/obsessions are thought-content phenomena.
- Do not overdiagnose from culturally unfamiliar or simply verbose speech; judge against context and coherence.

Common traps:
- Treating the assessment as a fixed checklist with no synthesis.
- Omitting physical examination or collateral history.
- Diagnosing from a single symptom.
- Confusing thought form with thought content.

## Q02. How do you distinguish a delusion, an overvalued idea and an obsession?

Examiner is asking: make a clean psychopathological distinction by form, degree of conviction, relationship to the self and resistance, while recognising that insight exists on a spectrum.

Must cover:
- Delusion: false belief held with delusional conviction, not ordinarily amenable to counterargument and out of keeping with the person’s cultural/subcultural context; the key is abnormal form, not bizarre content alone.
- Overvalued idea: comprehensible, emotionally charged belief that comes to dominate thinking/behaviour but is not held with the fixed, qualitatively delusional certainty of a delusion; often understandable from personality, values, affect or context.
- Obsession: recurrent intrusive thought, image or impulse experienced as arising in one’s own mind, unwanted/distressing, usually resisted or recognised as excessive/unreasonable to some degree; compulsions may be performed to neutralise anxiety or feared outcomes.
- Insight can vary in OCD; poor insight does not automatically convert an obsession into a delusion.
- Distinguish by phenomenology: conviction/fixity, intrusiveness, ego-dystonicity, resistance, comprehensibility and relation to behaviour.

Recall spine: form → conviction/fixity → intrusive vs endorsed → resistance/ego-dystonicity → comprehensibility/context → insight spectrum.

Common traps:
- Defining delusion simply as “a false belief”.
- Calling any strongly held belief an overvalued idea.
- Assuming obsessions always have perfect insight.
- Using bizarre content alone to classify the phenomenon.

## Q03. When psychiatric symptoms appear for the first time, when should you suspect a medical, neurological, medication-related or substance-related cause?

Examiner is asking: recognise red flags for secondary psychiatric syndromes and show a targeted organic work-up without implying that every patient needs exhaustive testing.

Must cover:
- Keep secondary causes high on the differential in first or atypical presentations, especially acute/subacute onset, fluctuating course, altered attention/consciousness, cognitive change, neurological signs, seizures, autonomic instability, abnormal vital signs, endocrine/systemic symptoms, unusual age of onset, rapid deterioration or atypical phenomenology.
- Delirium is a medical emergency: impaired attention/consciousness with acute onset and fluctuation, often with disorientation, sleep–wake reversal, illusions or visual hallucinations.
- Explicitly review prescribed medication, OTC/alternative agents, recent medication changes, intoxication, withdrawal and substance exposure.
- Physical and neurological examination are essential when secondary causes are plausible.
- Secondary syndromes are phenomenologically indistinguishable from primary psychiatric disorders; clues come from associated features and chronology.
- Useful causal reasoning: known disease capable of producing syndrome + plausible temporal relation + improvement when cause resolves + lack of a better primary psychiatric explanation.
- Examples may include neurological disease, epilepsy, neurodegeneration, endocrine/metabolic disorders, infection/encephalitis, autoimmune/limbic processes, head injury, hypoxia, medication toxicity and substances.
- Investigations should be targeted by history/examination; do not imply one universal test panel.

Recall spine: atypical/acute onset → consciousness/cognition → neuro/systemic signs → medication/substances → physical/neuro exam → targeted tests → temporal/causal reasoning.

Common traps:
- Assuming “psychiatric-looking” means primary psychiatric.
- Missing delirium because the patient is agitated or psychotic.
- Ignoring medication/withdrawal causes.
- Ordering indiscriminate testing without clinical direction.

## Q04. How do you assess suicidal intent and immediate suicide risk?

Examiner is asking: perform a direct suicide assessment and convert it into a dynamic risk formulation and immediate management plan rather than a prediction score.

Must cover:
- Ask directly about current suicidal thoughts, intent, planning, preparation, access to means, recent suicidal behaviour, precautions against discovery, perceived lethality, hopelessness, acute stressors and reasons for living/protective factors.
- Clarify mental state, agitation, psychosis, depression, intoxication/withdrawal, substance use, impulsivity, medical needs, supports and ability to collaborate with safety planning.
- Previous attempts/self-harm are a major historical vulnerability and must be explicitly elicited.
- Assess access to lethal means and foreseeable near-term stressors.
- Distinguish prediction from formulation: do not claim to predict whether suicide will occur; explain why risk is elevated now, which factors are static/background, which are dynamic/changeable, what may happen next, and what interventions reduce danger.
- Do not use global low/medium/high categories or a risk score to predict suicide or determine discharge/treatment.
- Disposition depends on immediate safety needs, engagement, mental state, intoxication/medical issues, supports, means access and whether a safe plan can actually be implemented.
- Document the formulation, dynamic drivers, protective factors, means, safety actions and follow-up plan; avoid a bare “low risk” label.

Recall spine: ideation → intent → plan/preparation/means → past behaviour → mental state/substances → stressors/protective factors → formulation → disposition/safety plan.

Board/current-practice fact:
- Risk scales/categories do not provide reliable individual prediction and should not determine discharge.

Common traps:
- Asking only “Are you suicidal?”
- Treating denial of ideation as sufficient reassurance.
- Using low/medium/high risk labels as if predictive.
- Listing risk factors without linking them to management.

Exam/current split:
- Older teaching often emphasised risk-factor accumulation and categorical risk. Current practice emphasises individualised dynamic formulation and management rather than prediction.

## Q05. A patient has survived a suicide attempt. What can you do to reduce the risk of recurrence?

Examiner is asking: show evidence-informed post-attempt care from immediate psychosocial assessment through safety planning, treatment, follow-up and continuity.

Must cover:
- Complete psychosocial assessment at the earliest appropriate opportunity, including function/context of the act, current mental state, ongoing intent, supports, strengths, vulnerabilities and immediate safety needs.
- Treat underlying psychiatric and substance-use disorders; do not present medication as a generic anti-self-harm intervention in itself.
- Collaborative safety plan: warning signs/triggers, internal coping strategies, supportive people/places, family/friend supports, crisis/out-of-hours contacts, and concrete actions to make the environment safer.
- Restrict access to likely lethal means collaboratively and specifically to the person’s formulation.
- Ensure clear discharge/management plan, named responsibility, continuity of care and communication between services.
- If ongoing safety concerns remain, initial aftercare within 48 hours is a useful current anchor.
- A structured, person-centred CBT-informed intervention tailored to self-harm is supported; a typical current recommendation is 4–10 sessions, adjusted to need.
- Avoid using a score or risk category as the discharge gate.

Recall spine: psychosocial assessment → underlying disorders → safety plan → means safety → continuity/follow-up → targeted psychotherapy → reassess dynamically.

Follow-up Q05a: What are the major suicide risk factors, and how does risk differ between psychiatric disorders?
Answer points:
- High-yield factors: previous attempt/self-harm; current intent/plan; hopelessness; severe/worsening psychiatric symptoms; substance misuse/intoxication; social isolation; acute interpersonal/financial/legal crises; chronic/painful physical illness; recent discharge/care transition; access to lethal means.
- Separate background/static vulnerabilities from dynamic/future factors and protective factors.
- Mood disorders, psychotic disorders, substance-use disorders and personality pathology are all associated with increased suicide risk, but do not give a rigid diagnostic ranking.
- Do not quote old lifetime diagnosis-specific percentages as board facts; diagnosis informs formulation but does not predict an individual outcome.

Common traps:
- “No-suicide contracts” or generic reassurance instead of a concrete safety plan.
- Discharge without defined follow-up ownership.
- Treating means restriction as generic rather than method-specific.
- Quoting old diagnosis-specific suicide percentages as if they predict the individual.

## Q06. How do you assess the risk of violence or aggression?

Examiner is asking: give a structured, scenario-based and management-focused assessment, acknowledging prediction limits.

Must cover:
- Previous violence/aggression is an important historical factor, but never sufficient alone.
- Assess current mental state and symptoms, substance use/intoxication, threats/intent, access to weapons, likely targets/victims, triggers, situational context, personality/impulsivity, cognitive/communication problems, adherence and social/environmental stressors.
- Separate relatively static/background factors from dynamic, situational and environmental factors that can be modified.
- Ask what has reduced escalation previously: engagement style, space, staff approach, medication, removal of triggers, supportive people.
- Use calibrated scenario language: plausible scenarios, immediacy, severity, likely targets and modifiable drivers; do not state that someone “will” or “will not” become violent.
- Structured tools may support defined short-term inpatient assessment; BVC/DASA-IV are examples, but tools are adjuncts and do not replace formulation.
- Translate assessment into an operational management plan: triggers, warning signs, de-escalation strategies, observation/engagement, substance management, victim safety and contingencies.
- Document reasoning and share relevant risk information with receiving teams; do not import UK-specific confidentiality law into the Greek answer.

Recall spine: past violence → current symptoms/substances → threats/means/targets → triggers/context → static vs dynamic → structured tools adjunctively → scenario formulation → management plan.

Common traps:
- Equating mental illness with dangerousness.
- Treating actuarial scores as deterministic.
- Listing risk factors without a management plan.
- Ignoring environmental/interactional triggers.

## Q07. How do you manage severe agitation in the emergency setting?

Examiner is asking: show safe emergency containment while simultaneously identifying the cause, using the least restrictive intervention and monitoring any rapid tranquillisation carefully.

Must cover:
- Severe agitation is a symptom, not a diagnosis. First assess immediate danger, staffing/environment, medical instability and whether urgent containment is required.
- Simultaneously consider delirium, intoxication/withdrawal, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, head injury, seizure/post-ictal state, infection, metabolic disturbance, medication toxicity, mania, psychosis and other behavioural causes.
- Use calm verbal/non-verbal de-escalation, reduced stimulation, appropriate personal space, a quieter setting and avoidance of provocation whenever safely possible.
- Restrictive interventions are last-resort/least-restrictive measures for the shortest necessary time; preserve dignity and physical safety. Do not state unverified Greek legal details for restraint or seclusion.
- Rapid tranquillisation = urgent parenteral medication when oral medication is not possible/appropriate and urgent sedation is required; it is emergency containment, not routine treatment of schizophrenia/mania.
- Current NICE adult options: IM lorazepam alone OR IM haloperidol + IM promethazine. If information is insufficient, the patient is antipsychotic-naïve, cardiovascular/QT concern exists or no ECG is available, lorazepam is favoured.
- Do not give exact rapid-tranquillisation doses in this product yet; follow current local protocol/product information.
- Initial medication should be a single dose and not repeated until its effect has been reviewed.
- After rapid tranquillisation monitor adverse effects, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, temperature, hydration and level of consciousness at least hourly; use 15-minute observations in higher-risk situations such as sleep/sedation, alcohol/illicit drugs, physical-health problems, injury during restriction or maximum-dose concerns.
- After the event, review precipitating factors, de-escalation attempted, injuries/adverse effects and update the future risk-management plan.

Recall spine: safety → medical/psychiatric differential → de-escalation → least restriction → rapid tranquillisation if required → monitoring → post-incident review.

Must-know board facts:
- Rapid tranquillisation is a separate emergency intervention, not routine antipsychotic treatment.
- Current NICE options: IM lorazepam OR IM haloperidol + IM promethazine; exact local dosing not frozen here.

Common traps:
- Sedating before considering delirium/medical causes.
- Repeating medication before assessing the first dose.
- Giving unverified exact doses as universal.
- Treating restraint as punishment or behavioural control.

## Q08. How do you assess whether a patient can give valid informed consent?

Examiner is asking: distinguish the clinical assessment of decision-making ability from the Greek legal validity of consent, and show what happens when the patient cannot consent.

Must cover:
- Greek law requires prior consent for medical acts as the default.
- Valid consent follows full, clear and comprehensible information about the health condition, proposed intervention, expected result, foreseeable consequences/risks, alternatives and expected recovery, sufficient for an informed decision.
- Consent must be voluntary, specific to the proposed act and not produced by mistake, fraud or threat; it may be withdrawn.
- Greek law uses `ικανότητα για συναίνεση` but does not codify the UK Mental Capacity Act four-part test. Do not present understand–retain–use/weigh–communicate as Greek statute.
- Clinically assess functionally whether this particular patient can understand sufficiently the problem, intervention, relevant risks/consequences and alternatives to make meaningful consent. Psychiatric diagnosis, involuntary status or disagreement with the doctor does not itself prove incapacity.
- If an adult lacks capacity, consent is given by the judicial supporter if appointed; if none exists, the Code refers broadly to `οικείοι`. Do not invent a hierarchy.
- The patient should still be informed as far as feasible and involved/assented to the greatest possible extent.
- For minors, consent comes from those exercising parental responsibility/custody; the minor’s views should be considered according to maturity and understanding.
- Emergency exception: necessary treatment may proceed when appropriate consent cannot be obtained and there is an immediate, absolute and urgent need; attempted suicide is specifically covered in the Code.
- Psychiatry-specific autonomy remains: mental disorder does not erase rights, dignity, preferences or the need for least-restrictive care.
- Involuntary admission and consent to a particular treatment are distinct legal questions; involuntary status does not automatically authorise every intervention.

Recall spine: information → ability to consent → voluntariness → specificity → who consents if incapable → emergency exception → psychiatric autonomy → admission ≠ treatment consent.

Must-know board facts:
- No UK statutory four-part capacity test in Greek law.
- Diagnosis/involuntary admission does not equal global incapacity.
- Substitute consent: judicial supporter if appointed; otherwise `οικείοι`, without an invented priority hierarchy.

Common traps:
- Importing UK capacity law as Greek law.
- Equating psychosis with incapacity.
- Treating involuntary admission as blanket treatment consent.

## Q09. When can a patient be admitted involuntarily in Greece, and what is the procedure?

Examiner is asking: state the current statutory criteria and procedural safeguards accurately, including the modernised transport framework.

Must cover — criteria:
- Governing substantive framework remains Law 2071/1992 arts. 95–100, with later amendments/reforms.
- Route I requires all three: mental disorder + inability to judge the interest of one’s own health + without hospitalisation treatment would be excluded or health would deteriorate.
- Route II is alternative and remains operative: mental disorder + hospitalisation necessary to prevent acts of violence against self or others.
- Social/moral/political nonconformity alone is explicitly not a mental disorder.

Must cover — initiation and medical opinions:
- Procedure may be initiated by the statutorily specified spouse/relatives/caregiver; if none is available, in an emergency the competent Public Prosecutor may initiate ex officio.
- Ordinarily two reasoned written medical opinions are required, usually from two psychiatrists or, if two cannot be found, one psychiatrist plus a physician of related specialty; they must address the statutory criteria and the doctors must not be related to applicant/patient.
- If prior examination was impossible because of refusal or ex officio initiation, prosecutor may order transfer to a public psychiatric clinic for examination; detention for this examination may not exceed 48 hours.

Must cover — prosecutor/court procedure:
- With concordant supporting opinions the prosecutor orders transfer/admission; conflicting opinions can still lead to transfer with judicial determination.
- Current first-instance jurisdiction is the Μονομελές Πρωτοδικείο, despite legacy wording in Law 2071/1992 referring to the Πολυμελές Πρωτοδικείο.
- Prosecutor submits the matter to court within 3 days of ordering transfer.
- Court sits within 10 days.
- Patient is summoned at least 48 hours before the hearing and may appear with lawyer and psychiatrist as technical adviser.
- Proceedings are in camera; decision must be specifically reasoned. Rejection means immediate discharge.

Must cover — rights/duration/transport:
- On transfer the patient must be informed immediately of rights, especially the right to challenge the decision; record this information.
- Involuntary hospitalisation ends when criteria cease; ordinary statutory maximum remains 6 months, with review/reporting safeguards including a report after the first 3 months and exceptional conditions for extension.
- 2022 art. 96A modernised transport: healthcare-led, dignity-preserving transfer; police involvement during onward transfer is exceptional and tied to statutory conditions and a psychiatrist’s written assessment when imminent harm cannot be prevented by de-escalation.
- 2024 service reform reorganised services and aims to reduce involuntary hospitalisation but did not replace the core criteria.
- Do not claim that involuntary admission automatically authorises every treatment.

Recall spine: two statutory routes → who initiates → two medical opinions → 48h examination route if needed → prosecutor → court deadlines → rights → duration/review → modern transport.

Must-know board facts:
- Route I: disorder + impaired health-interest judgement + treatment excluded/health deterioration without admission.
- Route II: disorder + hospitalisation necessary to prevent violence to self/others.
- 48h examination maximum when prior examination impossible; prosecutor 3 days; court 10 days; summons ≥48h beforehand.
- Current first-instance court: Μονομελές Πρωτοδικείο.

Exam/current split:
- Do not use the unenacted 2019 reform proposal as law.
- Legacy statutory wording still names the Πολυμελές Πρωτοδικείο, but current procedural jurisdiction is the Μονομελές Πρωτοδικείο.
- Real-world availability of the 2022 healthcare-led transport model is service-dependent; state the statutory model, not local implementation variability, unless asked.

Common traps:
- Treating dangerousness as the only criterion.
- Mixing the 48-hour examination window with the duration of involuntary hospitalisation.
- Saying police transport is the default current model.
- Conflating involuntary admission with unrestricted involuntary treatment.

## Q10. How do you recognise catatonia and how do you treat it?

Examiner is asking: recognise catatonia as a transdiagnostic psychomotor syndrome, identify medical causes/complications and institute benzodiazepine/ECT treatment promptly.

Must cover:
- Catatonia is a transdiagnostic syndrome across mood disorders, psychotic disorders, autism, medical/neurological disease and substance/medication states; “catatonic schizophrenia” is historical rather than the current organising concept.
- Practical current diagnostic threshold: 3 or more catatonic signs.
- Clinical signs span decreased activity (stupor, mutism), abnormal psychomotor phenomena (posturing, catalepsy/waxy flexibility, negativism, mannerisms, stereotypy, grimacing, echophenomena) and excited/agitated forms.
- Use a structured examination/rating instrument when helpful; BFCRS is a common clinical tool, but it does not replace diagnosis/aetiological assessment.
- Search actively for underlying psychiatric, neurological, medical, toxic and withdrawal causes; physical/neurological examination, collateral and targeted investigations are essential.
- Treat complications/supportive needs in parallel: hydration/nutrition, immobility complications, pressure injury, aspiration, thromboembolism and other medical risks as clinically relevant.
- Benzodiazepines and/or ECT are first-line treatments.
- Lorazepam challenge may support diagnosis/treatment response: 1–2 mg IV/IM or 2 mg oral is the verified challenge range.
- Do not turn the challenge into a requirement before treatment or into a claim that non-response excludes catatonia.
- ECT is indicated when benzodiazepines are ineffective/inadequate, when severity/medical risk is high, and particularly in malignant catatonia.
- Be cautious with dopamine antagonists/antipsychotics in catatonia, especially when malignant catatonia or NMS is possible.

Recall spine: recognise ≥3 signs → transdiagnostic causes → medical work-up/complications → lorazepam → ECT → antipsychotic caution.

Follow-up Q10a: What changes if the patient also has fever, autonomic instability and rigidity?
Answer points:
- Treat as malignant catatonia/NMS-level emergency; urgent medical assessment and supportive care.
- Stop dopamine antagonists when malignant catatonia is suspected.
- Lorazepam should be started urgently; if response is inadequate, escalate rapidly to ECT, with 48–72 hours as the verified current urgency anchor rather than prolonged waiting.
- NMS is a critical overlapping differential; consider recent dopamine antagonist exposure or withdrawal of dopaminergic agents, rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability and CK/medical complications, while recognising substantial clinical overlap.
- Do not delay definitive treatment while waiting for perfect nosological certainty when the patient is medically deteriorating.

Must-know board facts:
- Current practical threshold: ≥3 catatonic signs.
- Lorazepam challenge: 1–2 mg IV/IM or 2 mg oral.
- Malignant catatonia: stop dopamine antagonists, urgent lorazepam, rapid ECT escalation if inadequate response within 48–72h.

Common traps:
- Restricting catatonia to schizophrenia.
- Missing excited catatonia because the patient is not stuporous.
- Giving antipsychotics reflexively before considering malignant catatonia/NMS.
- Delaying ECT in a medically unstable malignant presentation.
