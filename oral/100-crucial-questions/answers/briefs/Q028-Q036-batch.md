## Q28. How do you recognise and manage panic disorder and agoraphobia?

Examiner is asking: recognise recurrent panic clinically, exclude important secondary causes, distinguish panic disorder from agoraphobia, and give a modern treatment plan without carrying forward obsolete panic-with-agoraphobia architecture.

Must cover:
- Panic attack: abrupt surge/crescendo of intense fear or discomfort with autonomic, respiratory, neurological and cognitive symptoms; examples can include palpitations, chest discomfort, dyspnoea, dizziness, tremor, sweating, paraesthesiae, fear of losing control/dying.
- Panic attacks can occur in several disorders and do not by themselves establish panic disorder.
- Panic disorder requires recurrent unexpected attacks plus persistent concern about recurrence/consequences and/or maladaptive behavioural change.
- Assess anticipatory anxiety, avoidance, functional impact, substance/medication exposure and medical mimics such as cardiac, respiratory, endocrine and neurological causes according to presentation.
- Agoraphobia is a separate current diagnosis: marked persistent fear/avoidance of situations in which escape may be difficult or help unavailable if incapacitating symptoms occur. Panic symptoms are common but not required.
- Panic disorder and agoraphobia can coexist when both are independently met; panic confined to agoraphobic situations does not automatically establish panic disorder.
- Treatment: psychoeducation; CBT with cognitive work, interoceptive exposure for feared bodily sensations and situational/graduated exposure for avoidance/agoraphobia; SSRIs are core long-term pharmacotherapy, with SNRIs also evidence-based/used where appropriate.
- Benzodiazepines should not be presented as routine long-term treatment because of dependence and poorer long-term management considerations.

Recall spine: panic phenomenology → unexpected/recurrent + concern/change → exclude secondary causes → agoraphobia separately → CBT/exposure → SSRI/SNRI.

Must-know board facts:
- Current ICD-11 treats panic disorder and agoraphobia as independent diagnoses.
- Panic attacks are transdiagnostic phenomena; they are not synonymous with panic disorder.

Exam/current split:
- Older “panic disorder with/without agoraphobia” architecture is historical; current ICD-11 separates the diagnoses.

Common traps:
- Calling every panic attack panic disorder.
- Missing hyperthyroid/cardiac/substance/withdrawal presentations.
- Treating agoraphobia as merely a complication/specifier of panic disorder.
- Using benzodiazepines as the centre of long-term treatment.

## Q29. How do you diagnose and manage generalized anxiety disorder?

Examiner is asking: identify the chronic pervasive-worry syndrome, distinguish it from neighbouring anxiety/mood/medical conditions, and know current first-line psychological/pharmacological management.

Must cover:
- Core phenotype: persistent excessive worry across multiple life domains and/or generalized apprehensiveness, present on more days than not, difficult to control, with clinically significant distress/impairment.
- Associated symptoms may include restlessness/tension, autonomic anxiety, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability and sleep disturbance.
- The syndrome is broader and more persistent than discrete panic attacks or situation-bound phobic anxiety.
- Differential: depressive rumination, panic disorder, OCD, health anxiety, substance/medication effects and relevant medical causes.
- Current ICD-11 requires symptoms for at least several months; DSM retains a 6-month duration requirement. Do not present 6 months as a universal current rule.
- CBT is a core treatment; applied relaxation and lower-intensity CBT-based interventions can also be useful depending on severity and service model.
- If medication is used, SSRIs are first-line; another SSRI or an SNRI is a standard next option when ineffective/not tolerated.
- Pregabalin is principally a guideline-supported alternative when SSRIs/SNRIs cannot be tolerated, not a co-equal routine first-line drug.
- Benzodiazepines should be restricted to short-term crisis use rather than routine treatment.

Recall spine: pervasive worry → associated tension/autonomic symptoms → several-month course → differential → CBT → SSRI/SNRI → pregabalin if intolerance.

Board fact/exam-current split:
- DSM: 6 months. ICD-11: at least several months, more days than not.

Common traps:
- Equating GAD with “anxious personality.”
- Missing depression/OCD/medical or substance causes.
- Presenting pregabalin or benzodiazepines as routine first-line equivalents to SSRIs/CBT.

## Q30. How do you diagnose and manage social anxiety disorder?

Examiner is asking: recognise fear of negative evaluation, distinguish social anxiety from personality/neurodevelopmental conditions, and give disorder-specific CBT plus appropriate pharmacotherapy.

Must cover:
- Marked fear/anxiety in one or more social situations because of anticipated scrutiny, embarrassment or negative evaluation.
- Avoidance or endurance with intense anxiety plus functional impairment; distinguish from normal shyness.
- Explore safety behaviours and self-focused attention where clinically useful.
- Avoidant personality disorder is a broader enduring personality pattern of social inhibition, inadequacy and hypersensitivity across contexts; social anxiety is organised around feared social evaluation. They may coexist.
- Autism is neurodevelopmental and involves persistent social-communication differences plus restricted/repetitive/sensory features; social avoidance may arise from communication/sensory demands rather than primarily fear of negative evaluation. Autism and social anxiety can coexist.
- Individual disorder-specific CBT is preferred first-line: exposure/behavioural experiments, reduction of safety behaviours/self-focused attention and cognitive restructuring.
- If medication is preferred/needed, SSRIs are the principal pharmacological option; alternative SSRI/SNRI strategies follow nonresponse/intolerance.
- Beta-blockers are not established treatment for social anxiety disorder. At most, mention occasional off-label use for circumscribed performance-related autonomic symptoms; do not make this a treatment pillar.

Recall spine: scrutiny/negative evaluation → avoidance/impairment → AvPD/autism differential → individual disorder-specific CBT → SSRI if medication.

Must-know board facts:
- DSM-5-TR uses a performance-only specifier; the old formal “generalized” subtype is obsolete.

Exam/current split:
- ICD-11 does not use the DSM performance-only specifier as its organising architecture.

Common traps:
- Calling severe shyness a disorder without impairment.
- Confusing avoidant personality disorder or autism with social anxiety from one symptom.
- Recommending propranolol/beta-blockers as standard treatment for the disorder.

## Q31. How do you diagnose and treat obsessive-compulsive disorder?

Examiner is asking: define obsessions/compulsions precisely, distinguish OCD from psychosis/rumination/personality/repetitive behaviour, and know ERP/SSRI treatment including the logic of treatment resistance.

Must cover:
- Obsessions: recurrent intrusive unwanted thoughts, images or impulses/urges causing distress and typically resisted/neutralised to some degree.
- Compulsions: repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to obsessions or rigid rules to reduce distress/prevent feared outcomes; they are excessive or not realistically connected to the feared event.
- Common domains can include contamination/cleaning, doubt/checking, symmetry/ordering, taboo/repugnant thoughts and mental rituals, but do not turn this into a symptom catalogue.
- Insight varies. Poor-to-absent insight can occur within OCD; extreme conviction does not automatically mean delusional disorder if the belief is congruent with OCD phenomenology and other psychotic features are absent.
- Differential: psychosis, depressive rumination, GAD, OCPD, autism-related repetitive behaviour and tic-related phenomena.
- Current classification places OCD within obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, not under anxiety disorders.
- ERP-based CBT is the core psychological treatment.
- SSRIs are first-line pharmacotherapy and can require a longer trial than depression: a proper full trial may require up to 12 weeks.
- If a standard dose is ineffective but tolerated after about 4–6 weeks, gradual dose optimisation toward the upper licensed/SmPC range may be considered. Do not teach automatic supra-licensed “high-dose SSRI” prescribing.
- Clomipramine remains an evidence-based later-line option after SSRI failure/intolerance or previous benefit, with greater anticholinergic/cardiovascular/overdose burden.
- Antipsychotic augmentation is a specialist resistant-OCD strategy after substantial failure of adequate SSRI and ERP-based pathways; never present antipsychotic monotherapy as standard OCD treatment.

Recall spine: obsession → compulsion → insight → differential → ERP → SSRI up to 12 weeks → clomipramine/augmentation if resistant.

Must-know board facts:
- OCD is an OCRD in current classification.
- ERP is the core psychotherapy.
- SSRI response may require up to 12 weeks.

Common traps:
- “OCD = liking order/cleanliness.”
- Confusing obsession with delusion or OCPD personality traits.
- Declaring SSRI failure after a short depression-style trial.
- Presenting supra-label high-dose SSRIs as automatic.

### Q31a. How do you distinguish hoarding disorder from OCD?

Answer points:
- Hoarding disorder is a separate ICD-11 OCRD diagnosis, not merely an OCD subtype.
- Persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of objective value leads to accumulation/clutter that compromises living spaces and causes distress/impairment.
- Excessive acquisition is common but not obligatory.
- Saving beliefs/urges may be more ego-syntonic and insight may be limited/absent.
- Distinguish from collecting, material deprivation/realistic need and clutter secondary to another condition.
- If treatment is mentioned: specialised CBT targeting discarding, acquisition, decision-making and avoidance; do not assume standard OCD pharmacotherapy has equivalent evidence.

## Q32. How do you diagnose and treat post-traumatic stress disorder?

Examiner is asking: recognise the trauma-specific syndrome, know the compact ICD-11 architecture and Complex PTSD, and prioritise trauma-focused psychotherapy correctly.

Must cover:
- Follows exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event/event series.
- ICD-11 PTSD has three core domains: re-experiencing in the present, avoidance, and persistent sense of current threat (for example hypervigilance/exaggerated startle), with impairment.
- Re-experiencing is not simply remembering the event; it has a here-and-now quality such as intrusive reliving/flashbacks/nightmares.
- Differential: acute stress responses, adjustment disorder, depression, other anxiety disorders, dissociation, substance use and medical/neurological conditions as relevant.
- ICD-11 Complex PTSD = full PTSD plus disturbances in self-organisation across affect dysregulation, persistent negative self-concept and relationship difficulties. Do not equate CPTSD with “very severe PTSD” or BPD.
- Individual trauma-focused CBT is first-line. EMDR is also an established first-line trauma-focused treatment in adults.
- Medication should not routinely be preferred over trauma-focused psychotherapy when therapy is acceptable/available. If medication is preferred, an SSRI or venlafaxine is a reasonable current option.
- Routine single-session psychological debriefing should not be offered to prevent or treat PTSD.

Recall spine: trauma → re-experiencing-now → avoidance → current threat → impairment/differential → CPTSD DSO → TF-CBT/EMDR → medication secondary.

Must-know board facts:
- ICD-11 PTSD: 3 domains — re-experiencing in present, avoidance, current threat.
- ICD-11 CPTSD adds affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulties.

Exam/current split:
- ICD-11 has a formal CPTSD diagnosis; DSM-5-TR does not have a directly equivalent CPTSD category.

Common traps:
- Treating any distress after trauma as PTSD.
- Listing a large DSM symptom checklist when asked for a concise current viva answer.
- Calling debriefing preventive treatment.
- Treating medication as first choice over trauma-focused therapy by default.

## Q33. How do you distinguish acute stress disorder from adjustment disorder?

Examiner is asking: separate DSM and ICD architectures explicitly, then distinguish trauma-specific acute syndromes from maladaptive reactions to broader stressors.

Must cover:
- DSM-5-TR retains Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) as a mental disorder after qualifying trauma.
- DSM ASD uses 9 or more symptoms drawn from intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance and arousal, lasting 3 days to 1 month after trauma, with distress/impairment and exclusions.
- ICD-11 does not have an equivalent “acute stress disorder.” Acute Stress Reaction is explicitly not classified as a mental disorder; it is a transient normal-range response after exceptional stress, typically beginning quickly and subsiding over days/about a week as threat resolves.
- ICD-11 adjustment disorder follows an identifiable psychosocial stressor and is characterised particularly by preoccupation with the stressor/consequences and failure to adapt, with impairment.
- Adjustment disorder usually emerges within about 1 month of the stressor and tends to resolve within 6 months after the stressor/consequences end, unless the stressor persists.
- The practical viva distinction: DSM ASD is trauma-specific and occupies the 3-day–1-month post-trauma window; adjustment disorder can follow a much broader stressor and lacks the defining trauma-specific ASD syndrome.

Recall spine: classification system first → DSM ASD trauma/3d–1mo → ICD ASR not disorder → adjustment = stressor + preoccupation/failure to adapt.

Must-know board facts:
- DSM ASD: 3 days–1 month.
- ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction: not a mental disorder.

Exam/current split:
- Do not use “acute stress disorder” as if it were an ICD-11 diagnosis.

Common traps:
- Blending DSM ASD with ICD Acute Stress Reaction.
- Diagnosing adjustment disorder after any distressing event without disproportionate maladaptation/impairment.

## Q34. What are the main dissociative disorders and how do you assess them?

Examiner is asking: organise dissociation phenomenologically, identify major current ICD-11 categories, and exclude neurological/substance/cultural explanations rather than diagnosing by normal tests alone.

Must cover:
- Depersonalisation: detachment/unreality regarding the self; derealisation: unreality/detachment regarding surroundings; reality testing is generally preserved.
- Dissociative amnesia: inability to recall important autobiographical information inconsistent with ordinary forgetting, after exclusions.
- Dissociative fugue is not a separate ICD-11 disorder; it is a qualifier of dissociative amnesia.
- DID belongs in the adult differential: clinically significant disruption/discontinuity of identity with associated memory/agency phenomena; avoid extensive criteria recital.
- Current ICD-11 also places dissociative neurological symptom disorder within the dissociative grouping.
- Assessment: detailed phenomenology and course, trauma/context where relevant, collateral information, mental state, neurological assessment, substance/medication history and differential including epilepsy/syncope, delirium, sleep disorders, PTSD and other psychiatric illness.
- Dissociation should not be diagnosed merely because investigations are normal.

Recall spine: depersonalisation/derealisation → amnesia/fugue → identity disturbance → FND placement → neurological/substance differential.

Board facts:
- Reality testing is preserved in depersonalisation/derealisation.
- Dissociative fugue is an ICD-11 qualifier, not a separate disorder.

Exam/current split:
- ICD-11 includes dissociative neurological symptom disorder in the dissociative grouping; DSM-5-TR places FND under Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders.

Common traps:
- Confusing depersonalisation with psychosis.
- Diagnosing dissociation because scans/EEG are normal.
- Assuming trauma must be identified before diagnosis.

## Q35. How do you distinguish somatic symptom disorder from illness anxiety disorder?

Examiner is asking: distinguish symptom-focused distress from illness preoccupation, explicitly reject the obsolete “medically unexplained symptoms” requirement, and acknowledge DSM/ICD non-equivalence.

Must cover:
- DSM-5-TR somatic symptom disorder (SSD): one or more distressing/disruptive bodily symptoms plus disproportionate/excessive thoughts, anxiety or behaviour related to them.
- The symptoms do not have to be medically unexplained; SSD can coexist with genuine medical disease.
- DSM illness anxiety disorder (IAD): dominant preoccupation with having/acquiring serious illness while bodily symptoms are absent or mild, with high health anxiety and checking/reassurance-seeking or maladaptive avoidance.
- If substantial symptom burden itself is central, SSD is more fitting than IAD.
- ICD-11 does not map one-to-one onto these DSM categories. Bodily distress disorder combines distressing bodily symptoms with excessive attention/preoccupation and impairment, also without requiring absence of medical explanation.
- ICD-11 hypochondriasis/health anxiety is placed primarily within OCRD (cross-listed with anxiety/fear-related disorders), not bodily distress disorders.
- Management: validate symptoms without endorsing catastrophic interpretation, use continuity with a consistent clinician/team, proportionate rather than repetitive investigations, planned follow-up, treat psychiatric/medical comorbidity and construct a collaborative biopsychosocial formulation. New/changing red flags still warrant medical reassessment.

Recall spine: SSD = symptoms + excessive response → IAD = illness fear with minimal symptoms → medical disease may coexist → ICD BDD/health anxiety differ → continuity/proportionate investigation.

Must-know board facts:
- “Medically unexplained” is not required for DSM SSD or ICD bodily distress disorder.

Exam/current split:
- DSM SSD/IAD and ICD-11 bodily distress disorder/hypochondriasis overlap but are not direct equivalents.

Common traps:
- Diagnosing SSD because tests are negative.
- Dismissing genuine medical illness after a somatic diagnosis.
- Reassurance/investigation loops without planned longitudinal care.

## Q36. How do you diagnose and manage functional neurological disorder?

Examiner is asking: demonstrate the modern positive rule-in model, appropriate neurological differential, non-dualistic explanation and phenotype-specific multidisciplinary treatment.

Must cover:
- FND is a positive diagnosis, not simply a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Major phenotypes include functional weakness/gait or movement disorders, sensory symptoms, speech/communication symptoms and functional seizures.
- Positive signs show internal inconsistency or incongruity with recognised neurological disease in the correct clinical context; normal tests alone are insufficient.
- Examples: Hoover/hip-abductor sign for functional weakness; distractibility, entrainment and variability for functional tremor. Do not use a single sign mechanically outside the appropriate phenotype.
- Functional seizures require careful semiology; when diagnostic uncertainty remains, video-EEG of typical events is useful where feasible, and coexisting epilepsy must be considered.
- Psychological trauma or an acute stressor is not required for diagnosis. Psychological factors may contribute in some patients but should not be forced into the formulation.
- Treatment begins with a clear positive diagnostic explanation.
- Motor disability often benefits from FND-informed physiotherapy/rehabilitation aimed at retraining automatic movement, redirecting abnormal self-focused attention, demonstrating preserved normal movement and reducing maladaptive movement/avoidance patterns.
- Occupational/speech therapy may be appropriate by phenotype; psychological therapy is used when indicated for functional symptoms or comorbidities.
- Neurology and psychiatry/psychology should share care rather than treating FND as exclusively belonging to the other specialty.

Recall spine: phenotype → positive sign → exclude/coexisting neurology appropriately → no required trauma → explain diagnosis → FND-specific rehab + psychological care as indicated.

Must-know board facts:
- FND is involuntary and diagnosed by positive clinical features.
- Psychological stress/trauma is not required.

Exam/current split:
- ICD-11 places the counterpart under dissociative neurological symptom disorder; DSM-5-TR places FND under Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders.

Common traps:
- “All tests normal, therefore FND.”
- Equating FND with malingering.
- Forcing trauma/stress into every case.
- Referring the patient between neurology and psychiatry without active treatment ownership.

### Q36a. How do you explain the diagnosis to the patient?

Answer points:
- Name the diagnosis directly.
- State that symptoms are real, involuntary and potentially disabling.
- Explain why the diagnosis was made using positive signs where possible rather than saying “nothing is wrong” or “the scan is normal.”
- Explain altered nervous-system function/control and preserved capacity rather than irreversible structural destruction; positive signs can demonstrate potential reversibility.
- Avoid “all psychological,” “caused by trauma,” or blame-based explanations unless specifically supported by the person’s formulation.
- Use a biopsychosocial/non-dualistic account and link the explanation immediately to treatment/retraining.
- “Software versus hardware” can be used as an optional analogy if useful, but it is not canonical and can oversimplify.

### Q36b. How do you distinguish factitious disorder, malingering and FND?

Answer points:
- FND: symptoms are not intentionally produced; positive functional signs show altered function and do not prove deception.
- Factitious disorder: intentional falsification/induction of symptoms with deception while presenting self/another as ill, without obvious external reward. “Sick role” is a useful traditional shorthand, but motivation need not be psychodynamically proven.
- Malingering: intentional symptom production/exaggeration for identifiable external incentives; it is not itself a psychiatric disorder.
- Intent and motive cannot reliably be inferred from inconsistency, unusual symptoms, secondary gain or clinician suspicion alone. Accusations require evidence of deliberate deception.
