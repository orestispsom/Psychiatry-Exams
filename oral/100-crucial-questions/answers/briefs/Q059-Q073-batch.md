## Q59. Adult ADHD: diagnosis and common mimics

**Recall spine:** developmental disorder → childhood symptoms before 12 → cross-situational pattern → current functional impairment → differential/comorbidity → collateral where available → treatment after diagnosis.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with executive dysfunction in an adult, but diagnosis requires a coherent developmental pattern rather than current symptoms alone.
- Current anchor: **some ADHD symptoms before age 12**. Childhood diagnosis, school reports or parental collateral are useful but are **not mandatory** if unobtainable; reconstruct development carefully and acknowledge uncertainty.
- Symptoms should be evident across multiple important settings and cause clinically important current impairment.
- Assess occupational/educational, domestic, relational/social and practical functioning; substance use, sleep and medication history.
- Important mimics/comorbidity: anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, sleep disorders, substance use, autism, personality pathology, TBI/other cognitive causes.
- Rating scales support but do not establish diagnosis.
- If medication is indicated in adults, current NICE high-level sequence: **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first-line; atomoxetine principal non-stimulant after intolerance/nonresponse to both stimulants**. Before treatment assess cardiovascular history, pulse/BP, weight, comorbidity, drug interactions and misuse/diversion risk. Routine ECG is not required in everyone.

**Q59a genuine follow-up — no clear childhood impairment:**
- Distinguish absence of documentation/recognition from absence of childhood symptoms.
- High structure, high ability or family scaffolding can obscure impairment.
- True de novo adult-onset ADHD is **not an established DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 diagnosis**; apparent late onset should prompt reassessment for missed childhood ADHD and alternative explanations.

**Board facts:** childhood symptoms before 12; childhood paperwork is not mandatory; diagnosis is specialist/developmental/cross-situational; rating scales alone are insufficient.

**Exam/current split:** old ICD-10 “before age 7” teaching → current DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 anchor is symptoms before 12.

**Traps:** requiring a childhood diagnosis; diagnosing from an adult rating scale; calling any concentration problem ADHD; assuming stimulants are absolutely contraindicated by every anxiety/tic/SUD history.

## Q60. Adult autism assessment

**Recall spine:** lifelong developmental pattern → social communication/interaction → restricted/repetitive/sensory features → developmental collateral → function/adaptation → differential/comorbidity → masking.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition; adult diagnosis requires a coherent developmental pattern, not a new adult-onset syndrome.
- Assess persistent social-communication/social-interaction differences together with restricted/repetitive patterns, insistence on sameness/restricted interests and/or sensory differences, with functional impact.
- Reconstruct childhood/adolescent development where possible and assess education, employment, relationships, daily living and sensory/environmental demands.
- Informants/records can help but are not always available.
- Differential/comorbidity: social anxiety, ADHD, OCD, schizoid/schizotypal traits, psychosis and intellectual disability; assess mood/anxiety and other common co-occurring disorders.
- Structured instruments/questionnaires can support assessment but no AQ, ADOS, CAT-Q or other scale independently proves or excludes autism.

**Q60a genuine follow-up — masking/camouflaging:**
- Camouflaging = suppressing, concealing or compensating for observable autistic features, e.g. rehearsed scripts, imitation, deliberate social-rule learning, forced eye contact or suppression of stimming.
- It can reduce visible signs during a short structured assessment and contribute to delayed recognition.
- It is **not itself a diagnosis or criterion** and should not become a stereotype that “female autism equals masking.”

**Board facts:** developmental onset; both social-communication and restricted/repetitive/sensory domains matter; masking can obscure presentation but does not replace diagnostic evidence.

**Traps:** diagnosing from CAT-Q/ADOS alone; treating masking as proof of autism; overlooking developmental history.

## Q61. New psychiatric symptoms in an adult with intellectual disability

**Recall spine:** establish baseline → identify change → communication/adaptive profile → medical causes first → psychiatric/environmental causes → medication review → collateral → risk/safeguarding → least-restrictive plan.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Current classification requires significant limitations in **intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour** originating in the developmental period; severity should not be reduced to old IQ bands alone.
- Establish baseline communication, behaviour, adaptive function, sleep and support needs; obtain carer/family collateral.
- Avoid **diagnostic overshadowing**: new behaviour is not automatically “because of ID.”
- Search actively for pain, infection, dental disease, constipation, urinary retention, seizures, sleep disorder, injury, reflux, medication toxicity/withdrawal and other physical causes; then assess psychiatric illness and environmental/communication triggers.
- Review medication burden, recent changes, anticholinergic effects, akathisia/EPS and antiseizure-drug behavioural effects.
- Formulate behaviour in context and use environmental/psychological interventions where possible.
- Antipsychotics are **not routine treatment for behaviour that challenges**. They are reserved for severe risk or when appropriate physical/psychiatric/psychological/environmental interventions have failed or are insufficient, with an explicit target, minimum effective dose and early review.

**Q61a genuine follow-up — minimally verbal adult with aggression + insomnia:**
- Treat abrupt aggression/insomnia as a possible expression of pain or illness until demonstrated otherwise.
- Prioritise dental pain, constipation, urinary problems/UTI, infection, injury, seizures, medication effects/withdrawal, sleep disruption and environmental/abuse concerns; use carers’ knowledge of behavioural baseline.
- Do not jump directly to sedation/antipsychotic treatment.

**Board facts:** adaptive functioning is central; diagnostic overshadowing; “medical cause first.”

**Exam/current split:** old IQ-bin severity → current intellectual + adaptive functioning/support-needs model.

## Q62. Anorexia nervosa: diagnosis and treatment

**Recall spine:** restriction/low weight → weight-gain prevention/fear → weight/shape psychopathology → physical/psychiatric risk → nutritional rehabilitation → ED-focused psychotherapy → medication limited.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Current diagnosis should **not** be reduced to BMI <17.5 or amenorrhoea.
- Core architecture: significantly low weight relative to context; persistent restriction or behaviours preventing weight restoration; and core weight/shape psychopathology such as fear of weight gain or disturbed/overvalued experience of weight/shape.
- Assess rate of weight loss, dietary restriction, exercise, binge/purge behaviours, physical complications, suicidality/self-harm, comorbidity and functional impairment.
- Physical assessment includes vitals, hydration, ECG/electrolytes and other investigations according to severity.
- Treatment backbone = **nutritional rehabilitation/weight restoration + eating-disorder-focused psychotherapy + physical monitoring**. Adult evidence-based approaches include CBT-ED, MANTRA and SSCM.
- Medication is not a stand-alone treatment for core AN; treat clear comorbidity where appropriate.

**Q62a genuine follow-up — ARFID:**
- Avoidance/restriction may be driven by **sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences, or low interest/appetite**.
- Consequences may include low weight/weight loss, nutritional deficiency, supplement/enteral dependence or marked psychosocial interference.
- Key distinction from AN: **not driven by weight/shape overvaluation or fear of fatness**.
- Adult treatment evidence is less mature; use multidisciplinary nutritional/medical correction plus mechanism-targeted behavioural/CBT-style work rather than claiming one universal branded psychotherapy.

**Board facts:** amenorrhoea not required; no fixed BMI diagnostic cut-off; ARFID lacks weight/shape psychopathology.

**Exam/current split:** ICD-10 BMI/amenorrhoea framing → current diagnosis without those universal requirements.

## Q63. Medically compromised anorexia nervosa

**Recall spine:** medical emergency → multidomain risk → vitals/ECG/biochemistry/glucose → admit/escalate when unstable → refeeding risk → thiamine/electrolytes → monitored refeeding.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Treat as a medical emergency when physiological instability or organ compromise is present; psychiatric and medical teams must work together.
- Current framework: **MEED supersedes MARSIPAN** and uses multidomain risk rather than one BMI/vital sign.
- Red flags: marked bradycardia, hypotension/postural instability, hypothermia, dehydration, syncope, ECG abnormalities, electrolyte disturbance, hypoglycaemia, rapid weight loss, severe restriction, weakness, infection or organ dysfunction.
- Useful adult “red” examples that may appear as board facts: **BMI <13 kg/m², waking pulse <40/min, temperature <35.5°C, glucose <3.0 mmol/L**. Present these as examples within overall risk assessment, not stand-alone admission rules.
- Urgent medical admission/escalation when unstable, seriously biochemically/ECG compromised, unable to maintain safe hydration/nutrition, at serious refeeding risk or otherwise medically unsafe.
- Refeeding syndrome can involve phosphate/potassium/magnesium shifts, fluid imbalance, thiamine deficiency, arrhythmia, neurological deterioration and organ failure.
- High-risk refeeding: give thiamine/multivitamin before/with feeding; closely monitor and correct phosphate, potassium, magnesium, glucose and fluid status.
- Modern approach is **risk-stratified, actively monitored refeeding**; do not teach prolonged extreme underfeeding as inherently safer. Very high-risk patients still require cautious specialist initiation.

**Board facts:** MEED replaces MARSIPAN; red examples above; refeeding electrolyte/thiamine risks.

**Exam/current split:** MARSIPAN/excessively slow universal feeding → MEED multidomain risk + monitored risk-stratified refeeding.

**Traps:** BMI alone deciding safety; rigid QTc cut-off; forgetting glucose/phosphate/thiamine; keeping a patient psychiatrically admitted when medically unstable.

## Q64. Bulimia nervosa

**Recall spine:** binge + loss of control → compensatory behaviour → weight/shape overvaluation → physical complications → CBT-based treatment → fluoxetine adjunct.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Recurrent binge eating with loss of control plus recurrent compensatory behaviours and undue influence/overvaluation of weight/shape.
- Differentiate from AN binge-eating/purging presentation: if significantly underweight with AN architecture, diagnose AN rather than BN; do not use BMI 17.5 as a rigid boundary.
- Assess vomiting, laxative/diuretic misuse, exercise/fasting, electrolytes, dehydration, dental/GI complications, menstrual/endocrine issues where relevant, mood, self-harm and substance use.
- Significant purging may cause hypokalaemia, other electrolyte disturbance, dehydration and arrhythmia/QT risk; obtain ECG/electrolytes when clinically indicated.
- CBT-based eating-disorder treatment is core. NICE often starts with bulimia-focused guided self-help and progresses to individual CBT-ED when needed.
- **Fluoxetine 60 mg/day** remains a valid adult board fact as an **adjunct to psychotherapy** to reduce bingeing/purging; not sole treatment.

**Board fact:** fluoxetine 60 mg/day adjunct in adult BN.

## Q65. Chronic insomnia

**Recall spine:** define chronic insomnia → adequate sleep opportunity → daytime impairment → exclude causes → CBT-I → selected medication → reassess.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep or early waking despite adequate opportunity, with meaningful daytime consequences; assess schedule, duration, sleep opportunity, naps, substances/caffeine, medications, mood/anxiety, pain, OSA/RLS/circadian disorders and other causes.
- Sleep diary can clarify pattern.
- **CBT-I is preferred first-line long-term treatment**; core components can include stimulus control, sleep restriction/compression, cognitive work and relapse prevention.
- Sleep hygiene is supportive but **not sufficient stand-alone therapy** for chronic insomnia.
- Hypnotics may be short-term/selected treatments when CBT-I is unavailable, unsuitable, declined or insufficient; chronic benzodiazepine/Z-drug use is not routine default care.
- **Daridorexant** is a current EU-authorised option for chronic insomnia with daytime impairment; NICE positions it when CBT-I has failed or is unavailable/unsuitable. Keep detail brief. It is contraindicated in narcolepsy.

**Q65a genuine follow-up — delayed sleep-wake phase disorder:**
- Circadian delay: difficulty sleeping/waking at conventional times but **normal sleep quality/duration when allowed the preferred late schedule**.
- Distinguish from insomnia, where sleep remains difficult despite appropriate opportunity/timing.
- Management uses schedule regularisation and appropriately timed circadian interventions such as melatonin/light at high level; do not give a rigid universal protocol.

**Exam/current split:** older CBT-I/Z-drug framing → daridorexant is now a genuine European option, while CBT-I remains preferred long-term first-line.

## Q66. Excessive daytime sleepiness / narcolepsy

**Recall spine:** confirm true sleepiness → exclude insufficient sleep/OSA/drugs/circadian/depression → cataplexy and REM phenomena → PSG/MSLT → type 1 vs 2 → specialist treatment.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Start with differential: insufficient sleep, obstructive sleep apnoea, sedatives/medications, circadian disorder, depression/medical illness and other hypersomnolence disorders.
- Narcolepsy features irresistible sleepiness/sleep attacks; cataplexy strongly points to type 1. Sleep paralysis and hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations are supportive but not obligatory.
- Narcolepsy type 1 = cataplexy and/or hypocretin/orexin deficiency in the appropriate diagnostic framework; type 2 lacks cataplexy and confirmed hypocretin deficiency.
- Overnight PSG followed by MSLT is the standard objective pathway in appropriate patients; avoid memorising a hypocretin numerical threshold unless specifically asked.
- Treatment is specialist and combines scheduled sleep/behavioural measures with wake-promoting/cataplexy-targeted pharmacotherapy as appropriate; no need for exhaustive drug list.

**Q66a genuine follow-up — REM sleep behaviour disorder:**
- Dream-enactment behaviour due to loss of normal REM atonia; diagnosis supported/confirmed by polysomnographic REM sleep without atonia.
- Prioritise injury prevention and review provoking drugs/other sleep disorders.
- Immediate-release melatonin or clonazepam remain conditionally supported symptomatic options in suitable patients.
- Strong association with future/current **alpha-synucleinopathies** such as Parkinson disease/DLB; this is prognostically important.
- No established neuroprotective therapy should be claimed.

**Board facts:** cataplexy → narcolepsy type 1; RBD = dream enactment + REM without atonia + synucleinopathy association.

## Q67. Sleep stages and EEG characteristics

**Recall spine:** N1 → N2 → N3 → REM → cycle.

**Model answer:** concise board-retrieval answer, not 500 words.
- N1: light transitional sleep, predominantly theta activity.
- N2: **sleep spindles + K-complexes**.
- N3: slow-wave sleep with high-amplitude **delta/slow-wave activity**.
- REM: low-voltage mixed-frequency EEG, rapid eye movements and skeletal-muscle atonia apart from expected exceptions.
- Sleep cycles alternate NREM/REM across the night; avoid unnecessary numerical staging trivia unless already assigned.

**Q67a follow-up — depression sleep architecture:**
- Classic associations: **shortened REM latency, increased REM density, reduced slow-wave sleep**.
- These are research/classic exam findings, **not diagnostic tests**. Do not retain exact “REM latency <60 min” as a universal fact.

## Q68. Personality disorder classification and formulation

**Recall spine:** enduring dysfunction → assess severity → ICD-11 traits → formulation → risk/comorbidity → avoid categorical over-labelling.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Personality disorder = enduring, pervasive maladaptive pattern involving self/interpersonal functioning, arising by adolescence/early adulthood and causing clinically important dysfunction/impairment.
- Current **ICD-11 is primarily dimensional**: diagnose PD, then rate **mild/moderate/severe** severity and describe trait domains.
- Five trait domains: **Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, Anankastia**.
- Optional **Borderline Pattern qualifier** can also be applied.
- DSM-5-TR Section II still retains traditional named categorical diagnoses/Cluster A-B-C, so explicitly distinguish systems rather than blending them.
- Formulation remains essential: developmental history, attachment/relationships, trauma/adversity where relevant, temperament, schemas/coping/interpersonal patterns, strengths, comorbidity and risk.

**Q68a follow-up — avoidant PD vs social anxiety:**
- Avoidant personality pattern is pervasive across self-concept, relationships and interpersonal functioning; social anxiety is an anxiety syndrome centred on feared scrutiny/negative evaluation. They overlap and may coexist.
- “Avoidant PD” remains DSM/legacy terminology; ICD-11 represents similar phenotype dimensionally, often Negative Affectivity + Detachment.

**Q68b follow-up — OCPD vs OCD:**
- Anankastic/OCPD pattern = pervasive perfectionism, rigidity, order/control and overconscientiousness.
- OCD requires obsessions and/or compulsions.
- Ego-syntonic vs ego-dystonic is a **useful tendency, not an absolute rule**.
- ICD-11 no longer has a separate OCPD/anankastic PD category; **Anankastia** is a trait qualifier, while OCD remains a separate OCRD.

**Board facts:** severity + five ICD-11 traits + optional borderline qualifier.

**Exam/current split:** ICD-10 categorical PDs/DSM clusters → ICD-11 dimensional severity + traits.

## Q69. Borderline personality disorder management

**Recall spine:** risk + therapeutic frame → structured psychotherapy → crisis plan → treat comorbidity → avoid medication for core disorder/polypharmacy.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Clinical pattern includes instability of relationships, self-image and affect; impulsivity; recurrent self-harm/suicidality; abandonment sensitivity; anger; and possible transient stress-related paranoia/dissociation.
- Begin with risk assessment, collaborative formulation, clear boundaries/therapeutic frame and crisis planning.
- **Structured specialist psychotherapy is primary treatment**. DBT, MBT and other coherent evidence-based programmes are valid; do not imply one model is mandatory for every patient.
- Medication is **not treatment for BPD itself or core instability/self-harm symptoms**. Avoid chronic antipsychotic treatment and long-term polypharmacy for the personality disorder itself.
- Treat clearly diagnosed comorbid disorders appropriately and review existing ineffective/unnecessary medication.
- Very short-term crisis medication may occasionally be used for a specific target with overdose/misuse safety, one drug where possible and a clear stop/review plan; do not make it the centre of the answer.

**Q69a genuine follow-up — BPD affective instability vs bipolar disorder:**
- BPD shifts are often rapid and reactive to interpersonal/environmental events within a chronic pattern of identity/interpersonal dysregulation.
- Bipolar disorder requires distinct sustained episodes representing a clear departure from baseline; assess decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, increased goal-directed activity, episodicity and inter-episode baseline.
- Duration helps but is not sufficient alone; BPD and bipolar disorder can coexist.

**Exam/current split:** medication/polypharmacy-centred older practice → psychotherapy primary; medication not for core BPD.

## Q70. ASPD versus psychopathy

**Recall spine:** formal diagnosis vs forensic construct → behaviour vs affective/interpersonal traits → developmental history → risk.

**Model answer must cover:**
- ASPD is a formal DSM diagnosis; current ICD-11 represents antisocial/dissocial phenotypes dimensionally within personality disorder rather than a separate psychopathy diagnosis.
- ASPD is relatively behaviourally weighted: persistent disregard for others/rules, deceitfulness, impulsivity/aggression/irresponsibility and developmental antisocial history where applicable.
- **Psychopathy is not an independent DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 diagnosis**; it is a personality/forensic construct emphasising superficial charm, manipulativeness, callousness, shallow affect, lack of empathy/remorse plus antisocial lifestyle/behaviour.
- PCL-R can be mentioned as a specialist forensic assessment tool, not a routine general-psychiatry diagnostic test.
- Both require careful risk, substance-use, developmental and forensic assessment; do not equate criminality with psychopathy.

**Board fact:** psychopathy ≠ formal DSM/ICD diagnosis.

## Q71. Sexual dysfunction

**Recall spine:** define domain → biopsychosocial/medication causes → focused history/exam/investigations → treat cause → psychosexual/relationship work ± specific treatment.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Organise by desire, arousal, orgasm and pain/penetration domains, while recognising overlap and relationship/context effects.
- Assess onset, lifelong/acquired, generalized/situational pattern, distress, partner/relationship context, mood/anxiety, trauma where relevant, medical/endocrine/neurological disease, alcohol/substances and medication.
- Important psychiatric-medication causes include SSRI-related desire/orgasm dysfunction and prolactin-raising antipsychotics; consider prolactin/endocrine assessment when clinically indicated.
- Physical examination/investigation should be guided by presentation; treat reversible medical/drug causes.
- Management may include medication adjustment, psychosexual/relationship approaches and condition-specific medical treatment such as PDE5 inhibitors where appropriate.
- Current ICD-11 places sexual dysfunctions under **conditions related to sexual health**, not simply “non-organic mental disorders.”

**Exam/current split:** ICD-10 psychiatric/non-organic framing → ICD-11 sexual-health placement.

## Q72. Paraphilic disorder and risk

**Recall spine:** interest ≠ disorder → consent/harm/distress → sexual/forensic history → victim/access/risk → comorbidity → specialist treatment/risk management.

**Model answer must cover:**
- Atypical sexual interest/paraphilia is **not automatically a mental disorder**.
- Current ICD-11 focuses disorder status on patterns involving **non-consenting individuals**, marked distress that is not merely due to stigma/social rejection, and/or substantial direct risk of injury/death depending on the condition.
- Assess fantasies, urges, behaviours, consent status, potential victims/access, escalation, prior sexual/non-sexual offending, disinhibiting substances, mental disorder, self-control, protective factors and engagement.
- Safeguarding/public-protection response depends on actual risk and jurisdiction.
- Specialist psychotherapy/relapse and risk-management work is central.
- SSRIs can be considered in selected lower/moderate-risk compulsive/intrusive presentations or relevant comorbidity.
- Antiandrogenic treatment or **GnRH analogues** are specialist interventions for selected high-risk/severe cases, requiring consent/legal framework and endocrine/metabolic/bone/cardiovascular monitoring.
- Never present hormonal suppression as routine treatment for consensual atypical sexuality.

**Board fact:** paraphilia ≠ paraphilic disorder.

**Exam/current split:** older category-driven framing → current ICD-11 explicitly avoids pathologising consensual atypical interests without qualifying harm/distress.

## Q73. Adult gender dysphoria / gender incongruence

**Recall spine:** identity not pathology → clarify distress/goals → respectful history → comorbidity/risk without causal assumption → capacity/support → appropriate specialist referral.

**Model answer must cover:**
- **Gender diversity itself is not psychopathology.** Do not diagnose mental illness from identity, expression or gender nonconformity.
- ICD-11 moved **Gender Incongruence of Adolescence and Adulthood** out of mental disorders into **Conditions related to sexual health**.
- Explore the person’s experience of incongruence/dysphoria, goals, social context, support, discrimination/minority stress, physical-health considerations and any mental-health symptoms.
- Assess depression, anxiety, neurodevelopmental conditions, trauma, substance use, self-harm/suicide risk etc. on their own merits; do not assume they explain or invalidate gender identity.
- Assess decision-making ability/capacity for proposed interventions in the ordinary clinically relevant way and support informed autonomous decision-making.
- Offer appropriate specialist gender-health referral/support where relevant without inventing Greek service pathways that were not verified.
- The psychiatrist’s role is not to “prove” identity or pathologise diversity, but to assess distress, safety, comorbidity, capacity and support needs respectfully.

**Exam/current split:** ICD-10 mental-disorder framing → ICD-11 gender incongruence under sexual health.

**Traps:** pathologising trans/gender-diverse identity; assuming comorbidity invalidates identity; inventing unverified Greek pathway rules.
