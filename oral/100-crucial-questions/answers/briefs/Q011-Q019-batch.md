## Q11. A 23-year-old presents with persecutory ideas, auditory hallucinations and social withdrawal. How would you approach the case?

Examiner is asking: demonstrate a safe first-episode-psychosis assessment that separates syndrome description, immediate risk, secondary causes, differential diagnosis, early treatment and longitudinal diagnostic caution.

Must cover:
- Treat this as first-episode psychosis, not automatically schizophrenia.
- Immediate priorities: risk to self/others, severe agitation, self-neglect, inability to care for self, intoxication/withdrawal, delirium/catatonia/medical instability; decide need for urgent containment/admission according to clinical need and risk rather than by diagnosis alone.
- Full psychiatric history and mental-state examination: onset/course, positive/disorganised/negative/affective/catatonic symptoms, insight, functioning and collateral history.
- Substance/medication history is essential; positive toxicology alone does not prove causation.
- Physical and neurological examination plus targeted investigations for clinically suspected secondary causes; routine structural neuroimaging is not required in every uncomplicated FEP.
- Differential: primary schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, mood disorder with psychotic symptoms/schizoaffective presentation, substance/medication-induced psychosis, and medical/neurological causes.
- Refer to/engage early-intervention-in-psychosis services without delay where available; provide multidisciplinary assessment.
- Treatment of established first-episode psychosis should combine an oral antipsychotic with psychological/family intervention; use shared decision-making and lower-end licensed dosing principles.
- Diagnosis remains longitudinal; evolution over time may change the final category.

Recall spine: safety → syndrome/course → collateral/substances → physical/secondary causes → differential → early intervention/treatment → longitudinal diagnosis.

Follow-up Q11a: heavy cannabis use — when substance-induced rather than primary?
Answer points:
- Require plausible temporal relation to intoxication or withdrawal and symptoms beyond ordinary intoxication/withdrawal effects.
- Favour primary psychotic disorder if psychosis predates relevant cannabis exposure, persists for a substantial period after cessation, or there were prior independent psychotic episodes.
- Assess pattern/intensity of use, abstinence course, collateral history and wider longitudinal phenotype.
- No universal fixed abstinence interval should be recited mechanically; current ICD-11 uses persistence for a “substantial period” as evidence against substance-induced psychosis.

Common traps:
- Calling the presentation schizophrenia immediately.
- Assuming cannabis causation from a urine test.
- Ordering routine brain imaging without an indication.
- Treating psychosocial intervention as something only for the chronic phase.

Deliberate exclusions: detailed schizophrenia diagnostic criteria, detailed antipsychotic adverse-effect tables, full rapid-tranquillisation protocol.

## Q13. How do you differentiate schizophrenia from schizoaffective disorder and mood disorder with psychotic features?

Examiner is asking: organise the differential by the temporal relationship and syndromic independence of psychosis and mood episodes, while explicitly avoiding DSM/ICD blending.

Must cover:
- Start with longitudinal chronology, not cross-sectional symptom content alone.
- Schizophrenia: characteristic schizophrenia syndrome with mood symptoms possible, but mood episodes are not the organising feature of the illness.
- Mood disorder with psychotic features: psychosis occurs in the context of a mood episode; outside that episode the psychotic syndrome required for schizophrenia is not established.
- Schizoaffective is the difficult middle category and differs materially by classification system.
- ICD-11 is episode-based: full schizophrenia requirements and a moderate/severe depressive, manic or mixed episode occur concurrently for at least 1 month, with near-simultaneous onset; psychosis outside mood symptoms is not required.
- DSM-5-TR uses a lifetime/longitudinal architecture: at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode at some point, while major mood episodes occupy the majority of the total illness duration.
- Therefore the classic “2 weeks psychosis without mood symptoms” rule is DSM-specific, not universal.
- Duration/course matters; do not mix ICD-11’s 1-month episode logic with DSM schizophrenia’s 6-month overall-disturbance framework.
- Reassess diagnosis as the longitudinal course becomes clearer.

Recall spine: map mood chronology → ask whether full schizophrenia syndrome exists → psychosis only during mood? → concurrent syndromes? → classification-system check → longitudinal reassessment.

Must-know board facts:
- DSM schizoaffective anchor: ≥2 weeks psychosis without a major mood episode at some point, plus mood episodes for the majority of the illness.
- ICD-11 schizoaffective anchor: concurrent full schizophrenia + moderate/severe mood episode for ≥1 month; no psychosis-without-mood requirement.

Exam/current split:
- Do not present the DSM 2-week rule as an ICD-11 criterion. The systems can classify the same longitudinal history differently.

Common traps: using psychotic content to distinguish diagnoses; calling any mood symptoms in schizophrenia “schizoaffective”; blending DSM and ICD requirements.

## Q14. What do we know about the neurobiology of schizophrenia?

Examiner is asking: give a coherent multilevel model rather than a list of disconnected abnormalities; distinguish established group-level findings from hypotheses and from clinically usable biomarkers.

Must cover:
- Schizophrenia is biologically heterogeneous and highly polygenic; no single gene is necessary or sufficient.
- Modern genomics supports extensive polygenicity with both common and rare variants; avoid memorising obsolete locus counts as the centre of the answer.
- Genetic liability interacts with environmental/developmental risk; cannabis, migration/ethnic-minority status, urbanicity, childhood adversity and obstetric/developmental exposures are probabilistic associations, not deterministic individual causes.
- Neurodevelopmental model remains a useful organising framework: developmental/synaptic biology and premorbid cognitive/social/motor abnormalities can precede psychosis, but it does not explain every course pattern.
- Dopamine: increased striatal dopamine synthesis/release is one of the most robust group-level findings and is particularly relevant to positive psychosis; it is not universal and not a diagnostic biomarker.
- Glutamate/NMDA-hypofunction models remain strongly supported research frameworks but should not be presented as one proven primary mechanism with dopamine merely downstream.
- Structural/functional/connectivity abnormalities are reproducible at group level, including hippocampal/thalamic/cortical and dysconnectivity findings, but overlap substantially with controls and other disorders.
- GABA/interneuron, serotonin and immune/inflammatory findings may be mentioned as secondary/interacting systems, with evidence less definitive than the core dopamine/polygenic/neurodevelopmental framing.
- No routine MRI, PET, genetic test or laboratory biomarker currently diagnoses schizophrenia in an individual patient.

Recall spine: genes/development → environment → circuits/connectivity → dopamine → glutamate → secondary systems → no diagnostic biomarker.

Common traps: dopamine-only answer; presenting associations as causes; quoting one brain scan finding as diagnostic; treating glutamate hierarchy as settled fact.

Deliberate exclusions: long lists of genes/CNV percentages, historical psychodynamic causation, detailed receptor pharmacology.

## Q15. What determines prognosis in schizophrenia?

Examiner is asking: describe prognosis as multidimensional and probabilistic, identify the most defensible prognostic factors, and avoid deterministic classic lists.

Must cover:
- Prognosis must be separated into symptomatic remission/relapse, functional recovery, cognition/negative symptoms, physical health/mortality and suicide risk.
- Symptomatic improvement does not guarantee occupational/social recovery; functioning may remain impaired despite reduced psychosis.
- Better baseline/global functioning and good adherence are among the stronger practical favourable signals; poorer baseline functioning, higher symptom burden, more prior admissions and poor adherence predict worse remission outcomes.
- Longer duration of untreated psychosis remains associated with poorer symptomatic/functional outcome, but call this a prognostic association rather than proven independent causation.
- Persistent negative symptoms and cognitive impairment contribute strongly to disability; substance misuse and poor engagement/adherence worsen outcome clinically.
- Male sex has some association with poorer symptomatic remission, but younger age at onset should not be taught as a strong independent prognosis rule.
- Family/social environment matters: high expressed emotion increases relapse risk; environmental deprivation/understimulation can worsen negative-looking disability.
- Recovery is possible, including later recovery; avoid a single timeless recovery percentage because estimates depend heavily on definitions and follow-up.
- Mortality is markedly increased from both natural disease and suicide/non-natural causes, with roughly 15–20 years of premature mortality as a current broad anchor; avoid a single fixed lifetime suicide percentage in the main answer.
- Prognostic factors have modest individual predictive power; never present them as destiny.

Recall spine: define outcome domains → baseline/function → DUP → symptoms/cognition → adherence/substances → social environment → mortality/suicide → probabilistic not deterministic.

Board facts:
- Keep DUP as a high-yield association.
- Keep excess mortality and 15–20 years premature mortality as a broad current anchor if useful.

Common traps: equating symptom remission with recovery; quoting old recovery percentages as universal; treating early age of onset as a dominant independent predictor.

## Q16. How do you treat an acute episode of schizophrenia?

Examiner is asking: show safe acute-phase management from assessment/setting through antipsychotic trial, severe agitation, psychosocial treatment and early response review.

Must cover:
- Reassess diagnosis, severity, risk, substance use, medical causes, adherence and current medications; choose inpatient/crisis/home setting according to risk and clinical need.
- Shared decision-making for antipsychotic choice, considering previous response and individual metabolic, EPS, cardiovascular/QT, prolactin/hormonal and subjective adverse-effect priorities.
- Use one oral antipsychotic as an explicit therapeutic trial: start at lower end of licensed range, titrate gradually, define target symptoms and adverse effects, and monitor adherence/response.
- If tolerated, assess an optimum-dose trial over about 4–6 weeks; early improvement at 2 weeks has prognostic value but lack of 20% improvement is not a mandatory switch rule.
- Avoid loading/rapid neuroleptisation and routine antipsychotic polypharmacy; brief overlap during switching is different.
- Severe agitation/rapid tranquillisation is a separate emergency-management issue; benzodiazepines are not routine augmentation for schizophrenia.
- In first episode and acute recurrence, combine antipsychotic treatment with CBT for psychosis and family intervention; psychological work may begin in the acute phase according to ability/need.
- Monitor physical health and treatment-emergent adverse effects from the start.
- If inadequate response, before switching/escalating check dose, duration, adherence, diagnosis, substances and secondary factors; repeated failures lead into Q18/TRS logic.

Recall spine: reassess/risk/setting → shared antipsychotic choice → start low/titrate → 4–6-week therapeutic trial → manage agitation separately → psychosocial treatment → monitor/reassess.

Board fact/exam-current split:
- Older 2-week/20% non-response rules are predictors, not a mandatory switch point; current NICE trial framing is 4–6 weeks at optimum tolerated dose.

Common traps: polypharmacy by default; using high antipsychotic doses simply for sedation; delaying psychosocial treatment until chronic phase.

Deliberate exclusions: exact drug doses, rapid-tranquillisation protocol, TRS/clozapine detail.

## Q17. How do you manage schizophrenia long term?

Examiner is asking: integrate relapse prevention, medication strategy, adherence/LAIs, physical health, psychosocial rehabilitation, negative/cognitive symptoms and shared long-term planning.

Must cover:
- Establish shared longitudinal plan with relapse-signature monitoring, adherence, substance use, suicide risk, functioning, carer/family needs and recovery goals.
- Continue effective antipsychotic maintenance after improvement; use the lowest effective/tolerated regimen and avoid routine intermittent/targeted-only maintenance.
- First-episode duration is not one universal number: WHO recommends at least 7–12 months after remission; NICE highlights high relapse risk if stopping in the first 1–2 years. Present this as shared decision-making rather than a dogmatic single duration.
- Recurrent illness generally favours longer maintenance, individualised to episode number/severity, relapse consequences, residual symptoms, adherence, adverse effects and preference.
- If discontinuing, withdraw gradually and monitor closely; NICE recommends continued monitoring for at least 2 years after withdrawal.
- LAIs should be considered for patient preference as well as when poor/covert/uncertain adherence is a priority; initiation/oral-overlap rules are product-specific.
- Physical/metabolic monitoring: weight, waist, BP/pulse, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement disorders and broader physical health; ECG when clinically/SPC indicated. Do not overload oral prose with every interval unless used as a board-fact box.
- Psychosocial care is core: CBTp, family intervention/psychoeducation, coordinated specialty/early psychosis care, vocational rehabilitation/supported employment and social recovery work.
- Persistent withdrawal/inactivity: distinguish primary negative symptoms from residual psychosis, depression, EPS/parkinsonism, sedation/other medication effects, substance use, cognitive impairment and environmental deprivation before labelling treatment resistance.
- Cognitive impairment is clinically important but has no established routine medication specifically correcting it; rehabilitation and functional support matter.

Follow-up Q17a answer spine: residual positive symptoms → depression → EPS/parkinsonism → sedation/medication → substances → cognition → environmental deprivation/understimulation → only then primary/persistent negative syndrome.

Board facts:
- WHO first-episode maintenance minimum 7–12 months after remission.
- NICE: high relapse risk if stopping in next 1–2 years; gradual withdrawal; ≥2 years monitoring after withdrawal.

Common traps: LAIs framed only as coercion/adherence enforcement; equating negative symptoms with failed antipsychotic efficacy; neglecting physical-health mortality.

## Q18. When do you call schizophrenia treatment-resistant, and what must you exclude first?

Examiner is asking: define genuine TRS operationally, show that pseudoresistance must be excluded, and know when clozapine should follow.

Must cover:
- Practical definition: persistent clinically significant schizophrenia symptoms despite at least two adequate antipsychotic trials, after excluding pseudoresistance.
- Adequate trial: TRRIP minimum operational benchmark is ≥6 weeks at therapeutic dose for each antipsychotic; do not turn the 600-mg chlorpromazine-equivalent fallback into a mandatory target for every patient.
- Two sequential failed adequate trials remain the mainstream threshold; NICE specifies two different antipsychotics, at least one a non-clozapine second-generation antipsychotic.
- Before diagnosing TRS reassess diagnosis and target symptoms; distinguish persistent positive symptoms from primary negative/cognitive symptoms and from affective/other pathology.
- Verify dose and duration, adherence/exposure, pharmacokinetic factors/interacting medicines, substance use and relevant medical illness.
- Non-adherence is a major cause of pseudoresistance; systematic adherence assessment is essential. Plasma levels and/or an LAI/directly observed exposure can reduce uncertainty when feasible, but are not universal regulatory prerequisites.
- Confirm appropriate psychosocial treatment and environmental/functional factors have also been addressed.
- Once genuine TRS is established, clozapine should not be delayed through serial additional non-clozapine trials.
- TRRIP’s tighter research framing includes at least moderate persistent symptoms and functional impairment over the adequate trial period; useful as a board fact, not necessary to recite in full spoken prose.

Recall spine: persistent target symptoms → 2 adequate trials → dose/duration → adherence/exposure → diagnosis/substances/interactions/medical → psychosocial factors → genuine TRS → clozapine.

Board facts:
- ≥2 adequate trials.
- ≥6 weeks each at therapeutic dose as TRRIP minimum operational benchmark.

Common traps: calling intolerance TRS without clarifying; diagnosing TRS without checking adherence; cycling through many non-clozapine antipsychotics after genuine TRS.

## Q19. Tell me about clozapine: indications, major risks and monitoring.

Examiner is asking: give a high-stakes practical clozapine answer with indication, safe initiation/monitoring, major toxicities, interaction/TDM principles and interruption rules. Accuracy outranks brevity here.

Must cover — indications:
- Main clinical indication: treatment-resistant schizophrenia after two adequate antipsychotic failures.
- European product indication also includes schizophrenia with severe untreatable neurological adverse reactions to other antipsychotics and psychosis in Parkinson disease after standard treatment failure.
- Guideline-supported additional uses: persistent substantial suicide risk despite other treatment; persistent substantial aggression may also justify clozapine. Keep regulatory versus guideline status conceptually distinct.

Must cover — haematology/current EU-Greece framework:
- 2025 EU revision uses ANC-only monitoring.
- Initiation: ANC ≥1.5 ×10^9/L; confirmed benign ethnic/Duffy-null neutropenia ≥1.0 ×10^9/L.
- General ANC <1.0 ×10^9/L: immediate discontinuation and no re-exposure; for confirmed BEN/Duffy-null neutropenia corresponding stop threshold <0.5 ×10^9/L.
- Mild neutropenia 1.0–1.5 ×10^9/L can be managed with enhanced monitoring under the revised framework.
- Routine ANC schedule: weekly first 18 weeks → monthly next 34 weeks to 1 year → every 12 weeks after year 1 if no neutropenia → annually after 2 years if no neutropenia in first 2 years.
- Infection symptoms require prompt assessment and immediate ANC testing.
- Do not reproduce obsolete lifelong-monthly European monitoring as current practice.

Must cover — interruption/restart:
- Keep blood-monitoring restart rules separate from pharmacological re-titration.
- If >2 days since last dose: restart 12.5 mg once or twice on day 1, then re-titrate according to tolerability; never simply resume the old full dose because of hypotension/collapse risk.
- Detailed ANC-schedule restart rules after interruption may be placed in board facts rather than the spoken core unless space permits.

Must cover — major toxicities:
- Neutropenia/agranulocytosis.
- Myocarditis/cardiomyopathy: highest myocarditis risk early, especially first 2 months; suspect with unexplained persistent tachycardia, chest pain, dyspnoea, heart-failure/flu-like symptoms; stop promptly and obtain urgent cardiology assessment if suspected.
- Baseline + weekly CRP/troponin for approximately the first 4 weeks is supported by expert consensus as a useful screening strategy, but is not one universally mandated EU laboratory schedule; phrase accordingly if included.
- Gastrointestinal hypomotility/constipation can progress to faecal impaction, ileus, megacolon and ischaemia; actively ask about and treat constipation, especially with anticholinergics/opioids or bowel-risk history.
- Dose/exposure-related seizures; manage by reassessing dose/exposure and anticonvulsant treatment when indicated.
- Metabolic effects/weight gain, sedation and hypersalivation are common practical burdens.

Must cover — smoking/infection/TDM:
- Cigarette smoke induces CYP1A2; abrupt smoking cessation raises clozapine concentrations/toxicity risk, while resumption can lower exposure and risk relapse.
- Monitor clozapine level when smoking changes; expert consensus may support an initial ~25–33% reduction after abrupt cessation in a stable heavy smoker, but do not present this as a mechanical universal rule.
- Serious infection, especially pneumonia, can suppress CYP1A2 via inflammation and markedly increase exposure; infection plus smoking cessation can compound the rise.
- During pneumonia/serious infection: assess urgently for toxicity, treat infection, check ANC, obtain clozapine level when possible, review interacting medicines and reduce/hold according to severity with clinical/TDM guidance. Expert guidance often uses approximately halving the dose during severe infection, but this is not a universal regulatory formula.
- TDM is especially useful for apparent non-response, adherence uncertainty, toxicity, smoking change, interacting drugs and serious infection/pneumonia.
- Before declaring clozapine non-response, TRRIP uses trough ≥350 ng/mL as an optimum exposure threshold; do not imply a single universal toxic concentration.

Follow-up Q19a — pneumonia + abrupt smoking cessation:
- Both changes increase clozapine exposure simultaneously: inflammatory CYP1A2 suppression + removal of smoke-induced CYP1A2 induction.
- Anticipate rapid toxicity: sedation, confusion/delirium, hypersalivation/aspiration, hypotension and seizures, with potential worsening pneumonia risk.
- Priorities: urgent physical assessment/infection treatment; ANC; clozapine level; review interacting antibiotics/medicines; promptly reduce or temporarily hold clozapine according to severity rather than continuing the pre-illness smoking dose; close monitoring and later re-adjustment/re-titration as inflammation resolves and smoking status stabilises.

Recall spine: indication → ANC/start/schedule → myocarditis → bowel → seizures/metabolic/salivation → smoking/infection/TDM → interruption/restart.

Board facts:
- EU start ANC ≥1.5 ×10^9/L; BEN/Duffy-null ≥1.0 ×10^9/L.
- General stop/no-re-exposure <1.0 ×10^9/L; BEN/Duffy-null <0.5 ×10^9/L.
- ANC schedule: weekly 18 weeks → monthly to 1 year → q12 weeks after year 1 → annually after 2 years if no neutropenia.
- >2-day interruption: restart 12.5 mg once/twice day 1 and re-titrate.
- TRRIP optimum exposure before declaring clozapine non-response: trough ≥350 ng/mL.

Common traps:
- Using obsolete WBC+ANC/lifelong-monthly EU rules.
- Restarting the prior full dose after >2 days off.
- Forgetting constipation/ileus or myocarditis.
- Ignoring smoking cessation and infection as major causes of acute clozapine toxicity.
- Applying consensus dose-reduction percentages mechanically without clinical/TDM context.

Exam/current split:
- Older European exam material on clozapine haematological thresholds and lifelong monthly monitoring is materially outdated after the 2025 EMA/PRAC revision communicated by Greek EOF. Use the current EU framework above.
