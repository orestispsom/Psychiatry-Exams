## Q52. Severe self-neglect / squalor with refusal of help

### Examiner is asking
Show that you can distinguish severe self-neglect from eccentric lifestyle choice, identify psychiatric/neurological drivers and immediate risks, assess decision-specific capacity/refusal, and use the Greek involuntary-admission framework correctly.

### Recall spine
- Immediate physical/environmental risk
- Cause: dementia / delirium / depression / psychosis / substances / hoarding / frontal dysfunction
- Collateral + home/environmental assessment
- Decision-specific capacity and valid refusal
- Multidisciplinary least-restrictive plan
- Greek involuntary-admission criteria, not self-neglect alone

### Model-answer core
1. Start with safety: nutrition/hydration, infection, untreated medical illness, falls, fire/gas/pest/structural hazards, medication neglect, risk to dependants/others.
2. Clarify whether this is autonomous choice or impaired decision-making driven by mental disorder, neurocognitive disorder, delirium, severe depression, psychosis, substance use, hoarding or frontal/executive dysfunction.
3. Obtain collateral and, where possible, assess the home/environment directly with social/community services.
4. Assess decision-making ability for the concrete decision at hand; capacity/refusal is decision-specific and should be documented functionally. Greek law does not codify the UK four-part MCA test as such.
5. If the patient has capacity and the refusal is valid, an apparently unwise lifestyle does not by itself justify forced treatment or compulsory admission. Continue engagement, harm reduction, practical support and repeated review.
6. If capacity is impaired, use the applicable Greek consent/emergency framework and involve family/legal/social mechanisms as appropriate. Emergency treatment without consent is a separate narrow exception.
7. Severe self-neglect alone does not satisfy Greek involuntary-admission law. A mental disorder plus one of the statutory Law 2071/1992 routes must be present.
8. Greece does not have one clean statutory analogue of the UK adult-safeguarding framework for a capacitous adult living in squalor; mechanisms are fragmented across health, social-care, municipal and judicial systems.

### Board facts
- Self-neglect alone ≠ automatic involuntary psychiatric admission in Greece.
- Valid refusal depends on the concrete decision and the Greek consent framework, not on whether clinicians think the choice is wise.

### Traps
- “Diogenes syndrome” is descriptive, not a sufficient aetiological diagnosis.
- Do not equate refusal with incapacity.
- Do not promise a general Greek power for forced home cleaning/social intervention; none was verified.

### Exam/current distinction
Older UK-derived capacity language may appear in the base source. Current Greek answer must use Greek consent/involuntary-admission law and treat the UK four-part test only as a possible clinical heuristic, not Greek statute.

## Q53. Autoimmune encephalitis red flags in a psychiatric presentation

### Examiner is asking
Recognise when an apparently primary psychiatric syndrome is actually a rapidly evolving encephalitic process and know the urgent diagnostic/treatment pathway.

### Recall spine
- Rapid/subacute progression
- Seizures / catatonia / cognitive decline
- Dyskinesias / language change / reduced consciousness
- Autonomic instability / focal signs
- EEG + MRI + CSF + serum/CSF antibodies
- Treat early: steroids / IVIG / plasma exchange; tumour search/removal

### Model-answer core
1. Open with the red-flag principle: new psychiatric illness progressing over days to weeks with neurological features is not routine primary psychosis until proven otherwise.
2. Red flags: new seizures, catatonia, rapidly progressive cognitive decline, reduced/fluctuating consciousness, abnormal movements or orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability, language dysfunction, focal neurological signs, and marked behavioural change out of proportion to a typical psychiatric course.
3. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is the prototypical psychiatric presentation; other antibodies such as LGI1/CASPR2 may be relevant depending on phenotype, but do not catalogue them.
4. Investigations: neurological examination, EEG, MRI brain, CSF including inflammatory studies and neuronal-antibody testing, plus serum antibodies; do not rely on serum alone when CSF testing is feasible because serum-only neuronal-antibody results can mislead, especially for NMDAR antibodies.
5. Search for an associated tumour where appropriate to the syndrome/antibody profile.
6. Once infection is reasonably excluded, first-line immunotherapy is high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG and/or plasma exchange. Treat an associated tumour when present. Escalation to second-line immunotherapy is specialist neurology territory and can be mentioned only briefly.
7. Antipsychotics may sometimes be needed symptomatically for dangerous agitation, but they are not the treatment of the encephalitis and should not delay neurological work-up/immunotherapy.

### Board facts
- CSF as well as serum assessment is important in suspected autoimmune encephalitis.
- First-line treatment: corticosteroids, IVIG and/or plasma exchange, plus tumour treatment where relevant.

### Traps
- Do not diagnose schizophrenia/mania solely from the psychiatric phenotype when neurological red flags are present.
- Do not wait for a complete “classic syndrome.”
- Do not make an antibody catalogue the answer.

### Exam/current distinction
Oxford’s anti-NMDAR paragraph is insufficient as a modern answer; current practice requires a red-flag, CSF/EEG/MRI and early-immunotherapy pathway.

## Q54. Epilepsy and psychiatry

### Examiner is asking
Organise psychiatric phenomena by temporal relation to seizures, recognise major comorbidity and treatment interactions, and distinguish epileptic from functional/dissociative events.

### Recall spine
- Ictal / peri-ictal / interictal
- Depression, anxiety, suicide risk
- Postictal psychosis + lucid interval
- Interictal psychosis
- ASM psychiatric adverse effects
- Psychotropic seizure threshold + enzyme induction
- Functional/dissociative seizures differential

### Model-answer core
1. Structure by timing: ictal, peri-ictal/postictal, and interictal psychiatric syndromes.
2. Ictal phenomena can include fear, experiential phenomena, autonomic symptoms and hallucination-like experiences, especially in temporal-lobe seizures.
3. Postictal states include confusion/agitation and postictal psychosis. A lucid interval remains characteristic, but do not freeze it at exactly 24–48 hours; contemporary descriptions span hours to several days, usually with psychosis emerging within a week after seizure clusters.
4. Interictal problems include depression, anxiety and chronic psychosis; depression and suicide risk require active screening.
5. Review antiseizure medication effects: levetiracetam can cause irritability/aggression, depression, agitation and psychotic symptoms. Avoid exhaustive newer-ASM tables.
6. Psychotropics: clozapine lowers seizure threshold in a dose-related way; bupropion is contraindicated in people with current/past seizure disorder; SSRIs are generally among the preferred antidepressants in epilepsy. Enzyme-inducing ASMs can lower psychotropic exposure and require interaction review.
7. Functional/dissociative seizures are an important differential. Current terminology favours functional/dissociative seizures rather than assuming “psychogenic”; episodes are not intentionally produced. Diagnosis is clinical and often supported by video-EEG capture of typical events when needed.
8. Management is collaborative neurology-psychiatry care: optimise seizure control, treat psychiatric comorbidity, review ASM/psychotropic interactions, and address psychosocial consequences.

### Board facts
- Postictal psychosis: lucid interval is characteristic but not rigidly 24–48 h.
- Levetiracetam can produce significant psychiatric adverse effects.
- Clozapine: dose-related seizure-threshold reduction; bupropion contraindicated with seizure disorder.

### Traps
- Gastaut–Geschwind syndrome should be framed as historical/contested, not an established personality syndrome.
- Do not infer functional/dissociative seizures are voluntary.
- Do not quote obsolete suicide-risk percentages.

### Exam/current distinction
Classic 24–48 h lucid-interval teaching should be softened to hours-to-days. “PNES” is increasingly replaced by functional/dissociative seizures terminology.

## Q55. Psychiatric consequences of traumatic brain injury

### Examiner is asking
Recognise acute and chronic neuropsychiatric syndromes after TBI, assess their relationship to injury severity/location and formulate a rehabilitation-oriented management plan.

### Recall spine
- Acute delirium / PTA
- Cognitive-executive deficits
- Depression / irritability / aggression / lability
- Apathy / personality-behaviour change
- Suicide / substances
- Rehab + environment + family
- Medication sensitivity

### Model-answer core
1. Separate acute post-traumatic confusion/PTA from persistent neuropsychiatric sequelae.
2. PTA remains clinically important for injury severity and rehabilitation planning, but do not reproduce Oxford’s exact duration-bin severity table as universal doctrine.
3. Chronic syndromes include cognitive/executive dysfunction, depression, irritability/aggression, emotional lability, apathy/inertia, disinhibition/personality change, substance misuse and increased suicide risk.
4. Frontal injury can produce distinct behavioural patterns: orbitofrontal disinhibition/impulsivity versus dorsolateral/medial frontal apathy and poor initiation. Treat these as useful neurobehavioral patterns, not rigid lesion-localisation rules.
5. Assessment: injury history and PTA, neurological/medical status, cognition/executive function, mood, aggression/impulsivity, substance use, suicide risk, medications, family/carer burden and functional goals.
6. During PTA/agitation, prioritise low-stimulation environmental management, orientation, sleep regulation and rehabilitation principles.
7. Longer-term treatment is multimodal: neurorehabilitation, occupational/cognitive/behavioural interventions, family education and symptom-targeted pharmacotherapy. Evidence for specific drugs for post-TBI agitation is heterogeneous, so do not present one universal drug algorithm.
8. Use psychotropics cautiously because sedation, anticholinergic burden, cognitive effects and seizure threshold may be more consequential after TBI.

### Board facts
- PTA remains clinically important but exact old duration bins should not be memorised as universal modern classification.
- Neuropsychiatric presentation may reflect frontal-executive network injury rather than primary psychiatric disease.

### Traps
- Do not diagnose primary personality disorder without considering acquired personality/behaviour change after TBI.
- Do not reduce all post-TBI low motivation to depression.
- Do not provide a rigid medication algorithm for agitation.

## Q56. Liaison psychiatry in the medically ill inpatient

### Examiner is asking
Show a disciplined consultation-liaison approach rather than repeating a generic psychiatric history.

### Recall spine
- Clarify referral question
- Delirium / capacity / suicide / withdrawal
- Medication- and illness-related psychiatric syndromes
- Functional/somatic presentations
- Biopsychosocial formulation
- Direct recommendations to treating team

### Model-answer core
1. Begin by clarifying exactly what the medical/surgical team is asking and what decision must be made now.
2. Common high-value problems: delirium, decision-making capacity/consent or refusal, self-harm/suicide risk, substance withdrawal, depression/anxiety/adjustment, medication-induced psychiatric syndromes and functional/somatic presentations.
3. Review the medical illness, physiological derangements, medications/interactions, recent procedures/ICU exposure, pain, sleep, substance use and pre-existing psychiatric history.
4. Obtain collateral where necessary and integrate physical illness, treatment burden, psychiatric history, coping, behaviour and social context into a biopsychosocial formulation.
5. Capacity questions must be decision-specific. In Greece, do not present the UK MCA four-part statutory test as Greek law; distinguish clinical assessment of decision-making ability from legal validity of consent/refusal under Greek law.
6. Communicate concise, operational recommendations directly to the treating team: what syndrome is present, immediate risk, what investigations/treatments should change, how to manage behaviour/communication, and what follow-up is required.
7. Liaison psychiatry adds value by integrating psychiatric reasoning with the medical context, not by taking ownership away from the primary team.

### Board facts
- Clarifying the referral question is part of the consultation itself.
- Capacity in a medical inpatient is decision-specific and jurisdiction-sensitive.

### Traps
- Do not call every distressed medically ill patient “adjustment disorder.”
- Do not miss delirium or medication/substance effects.
- Do not use UK capacity law as current Greek law.

### Exam/current distinction
Oxford’s capacity framework is UK-shaped; current Greek answers must use the verified Greek consent/legal framework.

## Q57. Apathy versus depression

### Examiner is asking
Discriminate reduced goal-directed motivation from a depressive mood syndrome, especially in neurological and neurodegenerative disease.

### Recall spine
- Apathy = reduced goal-directed activity/motivation
- Behavioural/cognitive + emotional + social dimensions
- Depression = sadness/guilt/hopelessness/suicidality
- Anhedonia overlaps but is not equivalent
- Frontal-subcortical disease context

### Model-answer core
1. Apathy is primarily a reduction in goal-directed activity/motivation rather than a mood disorder. Depression is defined by a depressive syndrome with negative affect/cognitions.
2. In apathy, the patient may show reduced initiation, reduced spontaneous behaviour/cognition, emotional blunting and social disengagement without pervasive sadness, guilt, hopelessness or suicidal thinking.
3. Depression is more likely when there is sustained low mood, self-reproach, guilt, hopelessness, depressive cognitions, biological symptoms and suicidality.
4. Anhedonia can occur in both and therefore does not by itself distinguish them.
5. Apathy is common in frontal-subcortical disorders including Parkinson disease, stroke, TBI and neurodegenerative disease. Medication effects and delirium/cognitive disorder should also be reviewed.
6. Current consensus conceptualises apathy across behavioural/cognitive, emotional and social dimensions; this can be mentioned briefly, but no scale needs to be memorised for the viva.
7. They can coexist, so assess both rather than forcing a binary diagnosis.

### Board facts
- Apathy is reduced goal-directed activity, not simply “depression without sadness.”
- Sadness, guilt, hopelessness and suicidality strongly favour depression.

### Traps
- Low patient distress does not prove apathy, but it can be a clue.
- Do not use anhedonia as a one-item discriminator.

## Q58. Charles Bonnet syndrome versus psychosis/delirium/neurodegeneration

### Examiner is asking
Recognise visual-release hallucinations in visual impairment and avoid misdiagnosing primary psychosis or dementia.

### Recall spine
- Significant visual impairment
- Recurrent visual hallucinations, often complex/formed
- Insight usually preserved
- No delirium / primary psychotic syndrome
- Differentiate DLB/PD/epilepsy/drugs
- Reassure + optimise vision

### Model-answer core
1. Charles Bonnet syndrome is recurrent visual hallucination in the setting of significant visual impairment, without a primary psychotic disorder or delirium explaining the phenomenon.
2. Hallucinations are often vivid and complex/formed, such as people, faces, animals or scenes, and are typically purely visual.
3. Preserved insight is characteristic but should not be made an absolute diagnostic rule: initial uncertainty or partial/fluctuating insight can occur. Persistent absent insight should prompt another diagnosis.
4. Assess cognition/attention and neurological features. Delirium gives acute fluctuating inattention; DLB/PDD adds cognitive decline, fluctuations, parkinsonism and RBD; epilepsy, migraine, medications/substances and focal neurological disease may also cause visual phenomena.
5. Management is mainly explanation/reassurance, optimisation of vision and environmental/behavioural strategies. Treat ophthalmological disease where possible and review triggering medications/illness.
6. Routine antipsychotic treatment is not supported for uncomplicated CBS; pharmacotherapy evidence is limited and reserved for exceptional specialist cases.

### Board facts
- Preserved insight is characteristic, not absolutely mandatory at every moment.
- Routine antipsychotic treatment for uncomplicated CBS is not standard care.

### Traps
- Do not diagnose schizophrenia from isolated visual hallucinations in a visually impaired older adult.
- Do not ignore DLB/delirium when cognition or attention is abnormal.

### Exam/current distinction
Older “intact insight mandatory” wording is too absolute; current framing allows partial/initial uncertainty while treating persistent absent insight as a red flag for another diagnosis.
