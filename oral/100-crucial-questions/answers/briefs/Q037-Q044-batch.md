## Q37. How do you assess and formulate a substance use disorder?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner is asking for a structured addiction assessment plus formulation, not a checklist recital.

Must cover:
- substances, quantity/frequency, route, duration, context and polysubstance use;
- intoxication/withdrawal, craving/loss of control and dependence features;
- psychiatric, physical, social, occupational/legal consequences;
- overdose, seizures, self-harm/suicide, violence, neglect/safeguarding and driving/occupational risk;
- prescribed/OTC drugs and collateral/investigations where indicated;
- previous treatments/abstinence, motivation, goals/readiness;
- formulation integrating substance syndrome, comorbidity, maintaining factors and harm-reduction needs.

### Current verification
- Current ICD-11 goes beyond the old ICD-10 shorthand of harmful use + dependence syndrome: it includes single harmful episodes, harmful patterns, dependence, intoxication, withdrawal and substance-induced disorders; hazardous use is a non-disorder category.
- Do not present the old ICD-10 3-of-6 dependence checklist as the only current ICD framework.

### Recall spine
Substances/pattern → intoxication/withdrawal/dependence → harms/comorbidity → risk → motivation/goals → formulation/harm reduction.

### Board facts
- Dependence remains clinically recognisable through loss of control, craving, prioritisation, tolerance/withdrawal and persistence despite harm, but current ICD-11 architecture is broader than ICD-10.

### Common traps
- Focusing only on quantity and missing consequences/risk.
- Ignoring prescribed sedatives/opioids and polysubstance use.
- Treating motivation as fixed rather than assessable/changeable.

### Exam/current split
- Historical exam shorthand: ICD-10 harmful use/dependence syndrome.
- Current: broader ICD-11 substance-specific architecture.

## Q37a. How do you approach tobacco cessation in schizophrenia, including important psychotropic interactions?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner is testing smoking cessation plus the clozapine/olanzapine interaction.

Must cover:
- actively offer cessation treatment in schizophrenia;
- tobacco-smoke PAHs, not nicotine, induce CYP1A2;
- clozapine is the key high-risk interaction; olanzapine also affected;
- abrupt smoking reduction/cessation can raise clozapine exposure within days;
- NRT/nicotine vaping does not preserve smoke-induced CYP1A2 induction;
- use specialist review, toxicity monitoring and TDM rather than one universal percentage dose reduction;
- restarting smoking can reduce exposure and efficacy;
- varenicline/NRT are legitimate cessation options; bupropion may be used with individual contraindication assessment.

### Recall spine
Offer cessation → smoke PAHs/CYP1A2 → clozapine/olanzapine → stopping raises levels → NRT ≠ smoking → TDM/monitor → restart lowers levels.

### Board facts
- A smoke-free admission can materially raise clozapine levels even if nicotine replacement is provided.
- Clinically meaningful clozapine rises may begin within roughly 3–5 days of cessation.

### Common traps
- Saying nicotine induces CYP1A2.
- Assuming NRT prevents the interaction.
- Memorising a fixed clozapine percentage reduction as universal.

### Exam/current split
- Old psychiatric-safety alarm around varenicline should not be repeated as a blanket contraindication.

## Q38. How do you recognise and manage alcohol withdrawal?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants recognition of uncomplicated withdrawal, severe complications, setting and safe pharmacological management.

Must cover:
- tremor, autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, nausea/perceptual symptoms;
- seizures and delirium tremens as major complications;
- history of severe withdrawal/DT/seizures, comorbidity, frailty, poor support and concurrent sedative withdrawal as reasons to lower inpatient threshold;
- benzodiazepine reducing regimens as standard treatment;
- fixed-dose suitable in community; symptom-triggered requires trained staff/frequent monitoring and is mainly inpatient/residential;
- CIWA-Ar as adjunct only;
- liver disease may favour lorazepam/oxazepam or careful dose reduction;
- thiamine, hydration/medical assessment and escalation for seizures/delirium/autonomic instability/suspected Wernicke.

### Recall spine
Recognise → predict severity → choose setting → benzodiazepine regimen → thiamine/medical support → monitor/escalate.

### Board facts
- CIWA-Ar does not replace clinical judgement.
- Symptom-triggered withdrawal treatment is not automatically superior in every setting.

### Common traps
- Managing previous DT/seizure history casually as outpatient.
- Using a score despite delirium or inability to communicate reliably.

## Q38a. How do you recognise and treat Wernicke encephalopathy?

### Tiny question brief
This is a high-stakes emergency answer.

Must cover:
- suspect in alcohol misuse/malnutrition with confusion/mental-status change, ocular signs, ataxia/gait disturbance or compatible presentation;
- do not require full classic triad;
- immediate parenteral thiamine, preferably IV in hospital;
- current board-level regimen: IV thiamine 300–500 mg three times daily for 3–5 days; if still symptomatic, 300–500 mg IV daily for a further 3–5 days or while improvement continues, then oral thiamine;
- give thiamine before or alongside carbohydrate where feasible, but never delay emergency glucose for hypoglycaemia;
- check/correct hypomagnesaemia, especially if poor response;
- untreated disease can progress to Korsakoff syndrome.

### Recall spine
Suspect early → incomplete triad common → IV thiamine now → carbohydrate safely → magnesium → prevent Korsakoff.

### Board facts
- Waiting for confusion + ophthalmoplegia + ataxia is unsafe.
- Wernicke treatment should not await imaging or confirmation.

### Common traps
- Oral thiamine alone for suspected Wernicke.
- Delaying glucose in hypoglycaemia.

### Exam/current split
- Historical complete-triad teaching is inadequate for current emergency practice.

## Q39. How do you manage alcohol use disorder after withdrawal?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants relapse prevention after detoxification, with psychosocial and pharmacological treatment matched to goals.

Must cover:
- structured psychosocial care: motivational/CBT/behavioural approaches, social-network/family work, mutual aid where useful;
- explicit goal: abstinence versus reduction;
- acamprosate or oral naltrexone as principal relapse-prevention medicines after withdrawal;
- choose according to liver/renal status, opioid use/need, adherence and prior response;
- disulfiram is selected/later-line for abstinence when first choices unsuitable or preferred, with supervision and safety cautions;
- nalmefene remains a reduction-oriented option for a defined subgroup who do not require immediate detoxification;
- about 6 months is a useful acamprosate/naltrexone treatment anchor, but not a mandatory stop date.

### Recall spine
Goal → psychosocial plan → acamprosate/naltrexone → selected disulfiram/nalmefene → monitor physical/social recovery.

### Board facts
- Withdrawal treatment alone is not AUD treatment.
- Pharmacotherapy should usually sit within psychosocial relapse-prevention care.

### Common traps
- Listing medications without matching them to goal/contraindications.
- Treating disulfiram as routine first-line medication.

## Q40. How do you diagnose and manage opioid use disorder?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants overdose recognition, evidence-based long-term treatment, harm reduction and current Greek naloxone update.

Must cover:
- overdose: depressed consciousness + respiratory depression, often miosis; airway/ventilatory support + naloxone;
- repeat dosing/observation may be required because naloxone may wear off before the opioid;
- withdrawal syndrome can be recognised clinically but detoxification alone should not be the default;
- methadone or buprenorphine maintenance/OAT is evidence-based standard care;
- methadone induction is high risk; cautious increases and interaction/respiratory/QT assessment matter;
- buprenorphine can precipitate withdrawal if started while sufficient full agonist effect remains; start when clinically significant withdrawal is present rather than memorising a universal hour/COWS cutoff;
- take-home/community naloxone and overdose education;
- Greece: since July 2025 intranasal and prefilled injectable naloxone may be prescribed by physicians of any specialty, dispensed through private pharmacies and administered by anyone in an overdose emergency;
- OKANA programmes use methadone/buprenorphine within integrated treatment; do not invent UK-style supervision rules;
- infectious-disease and injecting-harm assessment: HBV/HCV/HIV, vaccination/testing, skin/endocarditis risk, needle/syringe services where relevant.

### Recall spine
Overdose/naloxone → withdrawal → OAT not detox-first → methadone/buprenorphine safety → take-home naloxone → Greek pathway → harm reduction.

### Board facts
- Detoxification reduces tolerance and can increase subsequent overdose risk.
- OAT is maintenance treatment, not merely a detoxification bridge.

### Common traps
- Calling detoxification the preferred default.
- Using one fixed buprenorphine timing rule for all opioids.
- Inventing Greek dispensing/supervision rules.

### Exam/current split
- Historical detox-centred framing versus current OAT/maintenance standard for many patients.
- Greek naloxone access materially changed in July 2025.

## Q41. What cannabis-related psychiatric syndromes do you need to recognise, and how do you manage cannabis use disorder?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis differential and treatment.

Must cover:
- intoxication may include anxiety/panic, perceptual disturbance and psychotic symptoms;
- withdrawal exists and belongs in current ICD-11 cannabis architecture;
- cannabis-induced psychosis requires plausible temporal relationship to intoxication/withdrawal and symptoms beyond ordinary intoxication effects;
- independent psychosis, psychosis preceding cannabis use or persistence well beyond the expected substance-related period argues for primary psychosis;
- cannabis exposure, especially frequent/high-potency use, is associated with increased psychosis risk and poorer course/relapse in established psychosis; frame as association/modifiable risk, not deterministic causation;
- psychosocial treatment is mainstay: brief intervention, CBT/MET/family approaches;
- no routinely licensed pharmacotherapy is standard;
- synthetic cannabinoids can cause more severe/unpredictable agitation, psychosis and medical toxicity.

### Recall spine
Intoxication → withdrawal → cannabis-induced psychosis vs primary → psychosis risk/course → psychosocial treatment → synthetic cannabinoids.

### Board facts
- ICD-11 separately recognises synthetic-cannabinoid disorders.
- No single abstinence-duration cutoff proves cannabis causation versus primary psychosis.

### Common traps
- Saying cannabis “causes schizophrenia” deterministically.
- Assuming persistent psychosis after cannabis use is automatically substance-induced.

## Q42. How do you recognise and manage stimulant-related disorders?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants cocaine/amphetamine intoxication, psychosis, medical emergencies, withdrawal and current longer-term treatment.

Must cover:
- sympathomimetic intoxication: agitation, autonomic activation, paranoia/psychosis, hyperthermia, seizures, cardiovascular/cerebrovascular complications;
- benzodiazepines as first-line/core acute treatment for agitation/hyperadrenergic states with cooling, fluids/supportive care and targeted management of complications;
- red flags: chest pain/ischemia, severe hypertension, arrhythmia, hyperthermia, seizure, focal neurology, altered consciousness, rhabdomyolysis/renal injury, severe psychosis/suicidality;
- old blanket beta-blocker prohibition is too absolute: benzodiazepines/vasodilators remain preferred acutely, but if beta blockade is needed during intoxication, combined alpha/beta agents such as labetalol/carvedilol are preferred;
- withdrawal/crash: fatigue, sleep change, dysphoria/depression, anhedonia/craving; assess suicide risk;
- contingency management is the best-supported psychosocial treatment; CBT/other behavioural approaches can be added;
- no single universally approved pharmacotherapy.

### Recall spine
Toxidrome → medical red flags → benzodiazepines/supportive care → psychosis → crash/suicide risk → contingency management.

### Board facts
- “Never use any beta-blocker after cocaine” is outdated.
- Contingency management is the strongest current psychosocial treatment signal for stimulant-use disorder.

### Common traps
- Treating psychosis while missing hyperthermia/MI/stroke/seizure risk.
- Teaching old absolute beta-blocker contraindication.

### Exam/current split
- Historical blanket beta-blocker prohibition versus current conditional alpha/beta-blocker use when clinically necessary.

## Q43. How do you manage benzodiazepine dependence and withdrawal?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants safe deprescribing of a physically dependent patient.

Must cover:
- abrupt cessation can cause severe withdrawal including seizures and delirium;
- use collaborative, gradual, individualised taper;
- useful starting principle: about 5–10% dose reduction every 2–4 weeks, usually not exceeding 25% every 2 weeks; slower schedules may be needed, including 5–10% every 6–8 weeks or slower in long-term/high-dose/sensitive patients;
- pause or slow taper if withdrawal becomes problematic;
- switching to diazepam/another longer-acting benzodiazepine is optional and situation-dependent, not mandatory; hepatic impairment may favour lorazepam/oxazepam or remaining with a shorter-acting agent;
- complicated withdrawal or major medical/psychiatric risk may require inpatient/residential care;
- treat underlying anxiety/insomnia and offer CBT/CBT-I or relevant behavioural support;
- do not reproduce fixed Ashton/one-eighth schedules as universal standards.

### Recall spine
Do not stop abruptly → assess risk → slow individualised taper → optional long-acting conversion → pause/slow if needed → psychosocial treatment.

### Board facts
- Modern tapering is symptom-responsive, not schedule-worship.
- Diazepam substitution is a tool, not a requirement.

### Common traps
- Abrupt stop because the current dose appears low.
- Treating equivalence tables as precise biological truth.

### Exam/current split
- Classic Ashton-style schedules are historical templates, not universal current standards.

## Q44. How do you diagnose and manage gambling disorder?

### Tiny question brief
Examiner wants behavioural-addiction phenomenology, harms, mania differential and current treatment.

Must cover:
- ICD-11 places gambling disorder under disorders due to addictive behaviours;
- core pattern: impaired control, increasing priority over other activities, continuation/escalation despite negative consequences, with significant impairment;
- chasing losses and cognitive distortions are useful classic phenomenology;
- assess financial/debt crisis, occupational/interpersonal/legal harms, safeguarding/dependants and suicide risk;
- gambling limited to manic/hypomanic episodes should prompt bipolar assessment; persistent pathology outside episodes can coexist with bipolar disorder;
- gambling-specific CBT is first-line; group CBT is standard when acceptable, individual CBT when group unsuitable/unwanted; motivational work and relapse prevention may help;
- practical financial-harm reduction: self-exclusion/limit-setting/access-to-funds and financial/social/legal support;
- pharmacotherapy is not first-line, but specialist off-label naltrexone may be considered after adequate psychological treatment fails or repeated relapse occurs.

### Recall spine
Impaired control/priority/continuation → harms/debt/suicide → mania differential → CBT → financial harm reduction → selected naltrexone.

### Board facts
- Gambling disorder is an addictive-behaviour disorder in ICD-11.
- Naltrexone has a defined specialist second-line role in current NICE guidance, but is not routine first-line pharmacotherapy.

### Common traps
- Diagnosing gambling disorder from gambling during mania alone.
- Ignoring suicide and financial safeguarding.

### Exam/current split
- Older impulse-control framing versus ICD-11 addictive-behaviour classification.
- Older “no medication role” teaching versus current specialist second-line off-label naltrexone after psychological-treatment failure/relapse.
