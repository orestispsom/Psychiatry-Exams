## Q74. Core PK/PD principles

**Recall spine:** ADME → half-life/steady state → protein binding/Vd → CYP induction/inhibition → renal/hepatic clearance → receptor action/therapeutic index.

**Model-answer content:** Explain absorption/bioavailability, distribution and protein binding, hepatic Phase I/II metabolism, renal elimination, half-life and steady state. Steady state occurs after several elimination half-lives, commonly around 4–5. Distinguish receptor agonism, antagonism and partial agonism; link dose–response and therapeutic index to safety. High-yield modifiers: age, hepatic/renal impairment, smoking and interacting drugs.

**Board facts:** Tobacco-smoke PAHs—not nicotine—induce CYP1A2 and can lower clozapine/olanzapine exposure. Fluoxetine/paroxetine are clinically important CYP2D6 inhibitors. Carbamazepine is a classic enzyme inducer. CYP inhibition usually appears faster than induction/de-induction. Routine commercial pharmacogenomic panels are not a universal prerequisite for prescribing.

**Trap:** Do not build a CYP catalogue or imply a universal dose rule for organ impairment.

## Q75. Important psychotropic interactions

**Recall spine:** CYP → serotonin toxicity → QT → CNS depression → lithium → valproate/lamotrigine → bleeding.

**Model-answer content:** Organise by mechanism and clinical consequence. Include smoking/CYP1A2; irreversible MAOI + serotonergic drug combinations; additive QT risk; benzodiazepines with opioids/alcohol; lithium with NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and especially thiazides; valproate increasing lamotrigine exposure; enzyme-inducing ASMs lowering susceptible psychotropics; SSRI/SNRI bleeding risk with NSAIDs/antiplatelets/anticoagulants.

**Board facts:** NRT does not reproduce tobacco-smoke CYP1A2 induction. Interaction risk is cumulative and patient-specific.

**Trap:** Do not say a PPI is always required with SSRI + NSAID/anticoagulant.

## Q76. Choosing an antipsychotic

**Recall spine:** efficacy/previous response → adverse-effect vulnerability → physical comorbidity/interactions → adherence/formulation → preference → clozapine after 2 failures.

**Model-answer content:** Individualise choice. Consider previous response, metabolic burden, EPS, prolactin, QT, sedation, comorbidity, drug interactions, adherence, LAI preference and patient priorities. Clozapine remains the treatment for schizophrenia after failure of two adequate antipsychotic trials.

**Board facts:** Clozapine/olanzapine: high metabolic liability. Risperidone/paliperidone/amisulpride: prolactin-elevating examples. Aripiprazole is relatively prolactin-sparing. LAIs are appropriate when preferred or when adherence is a clinical priority—not merely as coercive treatment.

**Trap:** Do not create a rigid ordinary first-line efficacy ranking.

## Q77. EPS and management

**Recall spine:** dystonia → parkinsonism → akathisia → tardive dyskinesia.

**Model-answer content:** For each syndrome state typical timing/phenotype and management. Acute dystonia: urgent antimuscarinic treatment; laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency. Parkinsonism: reassess dose/need, switch if possible, short-term anticholinergic only when needed; avoid automatic chronic prophylaxis. Akathisia: dose reduction/switch when feasible; propranolol is a major option. Tardive dyskinesia: minimise dopamine-blocker exposure, stop unnecessary anticholinergics, consider lower-risk strategy and VMAT2 inhibition where appropriate.

**Board fact:** Deutetrabenazine received EU-wide authorisation in Jan 2026 for moderate–severe tardive dyskinesia in adults. Greek routine availability/reimbursement was not verified.

## Q77a. Akathisia vs agitation

Akathisia requires **subjective inner restlessness/urge to move plus observable motor restlessness**, usually temporally linked to starting/increasing a drug. Differentiate from anxiety, mania and psychotic agitation by phenomenology, timing and the patient’s description. Pure akathisia is not generally treated with anticholinergics; propranolol is a major pharmacological option when not contraindicated.

## Q78. Antipsychotic metabolic/endocrine/CV adverse effects and monitoring

**Recall spine:** weight/waist → BP/pulse → glucose/HbA1c → lipids → prolactin → ECG/QT → clozapine myocarditis.

**Model-answer content:** Discuss metabolic syndrome, hyperglycaemia/diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hyperprolactinaemia/sexual effects, QT risk and drug-specific tendencies. Use baseline and repeated metabolic monitoring. NICE schizophrenia monitoring includes baseline weight, waist, BP/pulse, glucose/HbA1c, lipids and prolactin; follow-up schedules vary by guideline/indication.

**Board facts:** Baseline ECG is risk/SmPC/setting dependent rather than mandatory for every outpatient. QTc >500 ms is a major high-risk threshold requiring urgent reassessment of QT-prolonging drugs, electrolytes and cardiac risk. Clozapine myocarditis is a high-stakes early risk; troponin/CRP surveillance protocols are specialist protocols, not universally legislated mandatory schedules.

## Q79. Choosing an antidepressant

**Recall spine:** diagnosis/severity → previous response → comorbidity → sleep/appetite/pain → sexual/cardiac/overdose risk → interactions/pregnancy → preference.

**Model-answer content:** SSRIs are a major first-line class because of efficacy/tolerability/overdose safety, but SNRIs and mirtazapine are also standard options in appropriate patients. TCAs are limited by anticholinergic/cardiac/overdose toxicity; MAOIs are specialist options. Tailor choice to symptom profile, physical illness, interaction burden and patient preference.

**Trap:** Do not present routine pharmacogenomic testing as required.

## Q80. Antidepressant adverse effects, interactions, switching and discontinuation

**Recall spine:** serotonin syndrome → hyponatraemia → sexual dysfunction → bleeding → BP/QT → withdrawal → switching/washout.

**Model-answer content:** Mention clinically important class effects and switching safety. Withdrawal can be prolonged; paroxetine and venlafaxine have higher liability, fluoxetine lower because of long half-life. Taper individually. Switching can involve direct switch, cross-taper or washout depending on pair; do not cross-taper blindly with MAOIs.

**Board facts:** At least **5 weeks** after stopping fluoxetine before starting an irreversible non-selective MAOI. At least **2 weeks** after stopping an irreversible MAOI before fluoxetine, according to current fluoxetine product information.

**Trap:** Do not teach a universal four-week taper.

## Q81. Lithium

**Recall spine:** indications → level → baseline tests → ongoing monitoring → renal/thyroid/calcium effects → toxicity → interactions → illness/dehydration.

**Model-answer content:** Indications include acute mania, bipolar maintenance and antidepressant augmentation. Mention evidence for reducing suicide/self-harm risk without overclaiming. Standard NICE maintenance target when initiating for bipolar disorder: **0.6–0.8 mmol/L**; **0.8–1.0 mmol/L** may be considered with relapse/persistent subthreshold symptoms. Levels are conventionally sampled about **12 h post-dose**.

Baseline: renal function/U&Es, thyroid, calcium, weight/BMI, pregnancy status when relevant; ECG if clinically indicated. Ongoing NICE pattern: lithium level every 3 months in year 1, then 6-monthly, or 3-monthly in higher-risk groups; renal/electrolytes including calcium and thyroid generally 6-monthly.

Adverse effects: tremor, GI effects, weight gain, hypothyroidism, nephrogenic DI, hyperparathyroidism/hypercalcaemia and chronic renal risk. Toxicity: coarse tremor, GI symptoms, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, seizures/coma in severe cases. NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, diuretics and dehydration can raise levels.

**Trap:** Do not teach one serum concentration as an automatic dialysis threshold; EXTRIP integrates level, renal function, symptoms and expected elimination.

## Q81a. Gastroenteritis + NSAID

Significant vomiting/diarrhoea/dehydration plus NSAID exposure creates a high risk of lithium accumulation. **Withhold lithium, urgently assess lithium level, U&Es/renal function and hydration, and arrange acute medical review according to symptoms/severity.** Do not wait for an arbitrary serum threshold in a symptomatic patient.

## Q82. Valproate

**Recall spine:** indications → hepatic/haematological/pancreatic/metabolic risks → monitoring → reproductive regulation.

**Model-answer content:** Describe use in bipolar disorder where appropriate, major adverse effects including weight/metabolic effects, tremor, thrombocytopenia/haematological abnormalities, hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis, and baseline/ongoing monitoring per product/guideline.

**Current reproductive facts:** For bipolar disorder, valproate is contraindicated during pregnancy. In women/girls able to become pregnant, do **not** state an unconditional universal ban: use is contraindicated unless stringent Pregnancy Prevention Programme conditions are met and alternatives are unsuitable/ineffective/not tolerated under specialist management. Current European/Greek-communicated precautions also apply to men: counselling about potential paternal neurodevelopmental risk, contraception considerations during treatment and for **3 months after stopping**, and no sperm donation during that interval.

**Trap:** Do not reproduce old teratogenicity percentages unless explicitly required.

## Q83. Lamotrigine

**Recall spine:** bipolar depression/maintenance → not antimanic → rash/SJS/TEN → slow titration → valproate interaction → contraception/pregnancy.

**Model-answer content:** Lamotrigine is important for bipolar depression/maintenance rather than acute mania. Rash/SJS/TEN risk requires slow titration and urgent review of concerning rash. Valproate inhibits lamotrigine glucuronidation, increasing exposure and requiring slower/lower titration. Enzyme inducers lower lamotrigine exposure.

**Board facts:** Estrogen-containing combined hormonal contraception can approximately double lamotrigine clearance; starting/stopping it can materially alter levels. Lamotrigine concentrations may fall in pregnancy and rise rapidly postpartum.

**Trap:** Do not invent exact titration schedules not supplied here.

## Q84. Benzodiazepines and hypnotics

**Recall spine:** indications → sedation/falls/cognition → dependence/tolerance → respiratory interactions → withdrawal → short-term use → taper.

**Model-answer content:** Benzodiazepines are GABA-A positive allosteric modulators. Risks include sedation, falls, cognitive impairment, tolerance, dependence, paradoxical disinhibition and withdrawal including seizures. Opioids/alcohol markedly increase respiratory-depression risk. Use short term where possible; Z-drugs carry similar dependence/sedation principles.

**Board fact:** “2–4 weeks” is a useful prescribing heuristic, not a universal statutory ceiling. Tapers should be individualized.

## Q85. Adult ADHD pharmacotherapy

**Recall spine:** baseline assessment → methylphenidate/lisdexamfetamine → atomoxetine → monitoring → misuse/diversion.

**Model-answer content:** Reuse verified adult ADHD sequence: lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate are NICE first-line pharmacological options when medication is indicated; switch between them after an adequate ineffective trial; atomoxetine is the principal non-stimulant after intolerance/nonresponse to both. Baseline cardiovascular history, pulse/BP, weight and substance-misuse/diversion assessment are essential. Routine ECG is not required in everyone; use when clinically indicated. Monitor BP/pulse and weight over treatment.

## Q85a. Previous psychosis or SUD

A history of psychosis or SUD is **not automatically an absolute stimulant contraindication**. Active psychosis/mania requires stabilization and reassessment before ADHD medication. In SUD, assess diversion/misuse risk, favour long-acting/prodrug formulations when appropriate, consider non-stimulant treatment and monitor closely.

## Q86. Pregnancy and reproductive psychopharmacology

**Recall spine:** maternal illness/relapse risk ↔ fetal/neonatal risk → avoid abrupt stopping → drug-specific risk → breastfeeding.

**Model-answer content:** Start with individualized risk–benefit assessment; untreated severe psychiatric illness also carries maternal/fetal risk. Avoid reflexive abrupt discontinuation of effective medication solely because pregnancy occurs. Use the minimum effective regimen and specialist perinatal input when risk is high.

Key current points: valproate has stringent reproductive restrictions and is contraindicated in pregnancy for bipolar disorder. Lithium is not simply “forbidden because of Ebstein anomaly”; current evidence supports a small increased cardiac-malformation risk, lower than historic teaching implied, balanced against relapse risk, with selected continuation possible under specialist monitoring. SSRIs can be used when clinically indicated; neonatal adaptation effects and a possible small absolute PPHN risk should be framed proportionately. Lamotrigine clearance changes during pregnancy/postpartum. Antipsychotic/benzodiazepine/breastfeeding decisions are drug-specific.

**Trap:** Do not turn this into a drug-by-drug pregnancy encyclopaedia or quote obsolete risk percentages.

## Q87. NMS vs serotonin syndrome

**Recall spine:** culprit → onset → neuromuscular signs → autonomic/hyperthermia → CK → management.

**Model-answer content:** NMS usually follows dopamine blockade/dopamine withdrawal and features hyperthermia, autonomic instability, mental-state change and marked generalized rigidity; CK is often raised. Serotonin syndrome follows serotonergic excess, usually evolves faster, and **clonus/hyperreflexia** are especially discriminating. Stop causative drugs and provide aggressive supportive care, fluids/cooling and ICU escalation when severe.

Specific agents are adjunctive, not universal antidotes: bromocriptine/amantadine/dantrolene may be used in selected/severe NMS; cyproheptadine may be used for serotonin syndrome but high-quality efficacy evidence is limited.

**Trap:** Do not diagnose serotonin syndrome from fever/confusion alone or make dantrolene/bromocriptine mandatory in every NMS case.

## Q88. TCA overdose

**Recall spine:** anticholinergic/CNS toxicity → sodium-channel blockade → ECG/QRS → seizures/hypotension/arrhythmias → sodium bicarbonate → ICU.

**Model-answer content:** TCA overdose causes anticholinergic toxicity, CNS depression/seizures, hypotension and ventricular conduction disturbance through fast sodium-channel blockade. ECG is central. **QRS >100 ms remains a useful major-toxicity/seizure-risk anchor.** IV sodium bicarbonate is first-line for significant QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia or hypotension. Manage airway, seizures, circulation and acid–base status in a critical-care/toxicology framework.

**Board facts:** Avoid class Ia/Ic sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmics and physostigmine in acute cardiotoxic TCA poisoning.

**Trap:** Do not present a single bicarbonate dose as the whole management algorithm.

## Q89. ECT

**Recall spine:** indications → rapid-response situations → procedure → unilateral/bilateral → adverse effects → continuation/maintenance.

**Model-answer content:** ECT is a highly effective treatment for severe depression, especially psychotic depression, catatonia and situations needing a rapid high-probability response; it can also be used in selected severe/refractory mania. It is delivered under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation. Electrode placement/stimulus strategy balances efficacy and cognitive burden. Main adverse effects include transient headache/confusion and anterograde/retrograde, including autobiographical, memory impairment. Medical conditions are generally risk factors requiring anaesthetic/medical assessment rather than a simplistic absolute-contraindication list. Continuation/maintenance ECT can be considered when relapse risk is high.

## Q89a. Consent/incapacity/Greek law

**Approved current position:** A capacitous adult ordinarily must give prior informed consent to ECT under Greek general medical-consent law. Involuntary psychiatric admission **does not itself authorise ECT** and does not establish global incapacity. Greek law contains general substitute-consent and emergency-treatment provisions and a psychiatry-specific provision on treatment against the patient’s will in serious circumstances.

**Critical limitation:** `UNRESOLVED — Greek ECT-specific authorisation procedure when the patient lacks capacity or urgent non-consensual ECT is contemplated.` No current official rule was verified specifying whether court/prosecutor/judicial supporter/independent second opinion or another special safeguard is required.

**Writer instruction:** Do not invent the missing procedure. Do not import UK Mental Health Act rules. State the verified principles and explicitly flag the ECT-specific incapacity/emergency authorisation pathway as unresolved.

## Q89b. rTMS

rTMS is an established non-invasive treatment for depression, generally delivered without general anaesthesia. Seizure risk is low but real. It is a treatment option in treatment-resistant depression. Do not simply call it “weaker ECT”: ECT remains preferable when a rapid, high-probability response is required in severe, psychotic or catatonic illness.
