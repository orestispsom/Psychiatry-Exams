## Q45 — How do you recognise, assess and manage delirium?

### Examiner is asking
Recognise delirium as an acute fluctuating disorder of attention/awareness caused by medical, substance or medication factors; find and treat causes; distinguish it from dementia/depression/psychosis; manage safely without reflexive antipsychotic use.

### Must cover
- Acute/subacute onset over hours to days, fluctuating course.
- Impaired attention with altered awareness/other cognition as the central syndrome.
- Hyperactive, hypoactive and mixed presentations; hypoactive delirium is easily missed.
- Common precipitating/contributing factors: infection, hypoxia, dehydration/metabolic disturbance, pain, constipation/urinary retention, medication effects, intoxication/withdrawal, sensory impairment and environmental disruption according to presentation.
- Assessment: collateral, medication/substance review, physical/neurological examination and targeted investigations to identify cause(s).
- Current general-setting screening anchor: 4AT; CAM-ICU/ICDSC in critical care/post-anaesthetic settings.
- Management backbone: treat cause(s), reorient, optimise sleep-wake cues, hydration/nutrition, mobilisation, glasses/hearing aids, familiar carers, avoid unnecessary moves/restraints.
- Antipsychotics do not treat the underlying delirium; consider only for severe distress or risk when de-escalation/non-drug measures fail or are inappropriate.
- If haloperidol is required: lowest appropriate dose, shortest duration, usually no more than about 1 week; PD and DLB are contraindications in current UK safety/product guidance.

### Useful differential structure
- Dementia: usually insidious/progressive; attention/awareness relatively preserved early.
- Depression: low mood/psychomotor change without acute fluctuating inattention.
- Primary psychosis: consciousness/attention usually clearer; medical precipitants and fluctuation favour delirium.
- Catatonia may overlap clinically and should remain in differential when motor/behavioural signs dominate.

### Recall spine
- Acute + fluctuating.
- Attention/awareness impaired.
- Hypoactive exists.
- Find/treat cause.
- 4AT general setting.
- Non-drug measures first.
- Antipsychotic only for severe distress/risk.

### Board facts
- 4AT is the current NICE-preferred general hospital/long-term-care tool when delirium indicators are present.
- CAM-ICU or ICDSC is preferred in critical care/post-anaesthetic recovery.
- Haloperidol is not a routine delirium treatment and is contraindicated in Parkinson disease/DLB.

### Common traps
- Calling all agitation in an older patient delirium without demonstrating acute/fluctuating inattention.
- Missing hypoactive delirium.
- Treating a screening tool as a substitute for diagnosis or cause-finding.
- Using antipsychotics as if they shorten or cure delirium.
- Treating “sundowning” as a defining criterion.

### Exam/current distinction
Older CAM-centred teaching may still appear in classic sources; current NICE uses 4AT first in general hospital/long-term-care settings.

## Q46 — How do you assess a patient with suspected major neurocognitive disorder / dementia?

### Examiner is asking
Give a structured dementia assessment that establishes cognitive decline, functional impact, trajectory, subtype clues, reversible/contributing causes and safety issues.

### Must cover
- Patient history plus collateral history.
- Onset, trajectory and pattern of decline.
- Cognitive domains: attention, executive function, learning/memory, language, perceptual-motor/visuospatial and social cognition as useful organising domains.
- ADLs and especially IADLs to establish loss of independence.
- Behavioural/neuropsychiatric symptoms.
- Medication/anticholinergic burden, alcohol/substances and medical/neurological contributors.
- Exclude delirium and major depressive syndromes where relevant.
- Physical and neurological examination.
- Validated brief cognitive testing; a normal brief score does not exclude dementia.
- Neuropsychological testing if diagnosis/subtype remains unclear.
- Targeted laboratory work-up for reversible/contributing causes; avoid one rigid universal panel.
- Structural imaging is generally offered unless diagnosis/subtype is already clear; MRI is preferred when vascular/subtype detail matters, CT acceptable where MRI is unavailable/contraindicated.
- Specialist biomarkers may clarify uncertain AD and now have treatment implications in selected patients: CSF amyloid/tau and validated plasma biomarkers in specialist settings. They complement, not replace, comprehensive assessment.
- Address driving, medication/financial safety, capacity and safeguarding according to individual risk and jurisdiction.

### Recall spine
- History + collateral.
- Trajectory.
- Cognition + function.
- ADLs/IADLs.
- Exclude delirium/depression/secondary causes.
- Exam + targeted labs.
- Structural imaging.
- Biomarkers only when specialist clarification changes management.

### Board facts
- Functional loss is central to dementia/major NCD rather than mild cognitive impairment.
- A normal brief cognitive test does not exclude dementia.
- Plasma AD biomarkers are now entering specialist practice but have not replaced clinical assessment or structural imaging universally.

### Common traps
- Diagnosing dementia from a cognitive score alone.
- Ignoring collateral history and functional decline.
- Presenting “major neurocognitive disorder” as the only current terminology.
- Ordering biomarker tests before doing a proper clinical assessment.

### Exam/current distinction
“Major neurocognitive disorder” is DSM terminology. ICD-11 retains neurocognitive disorders as a grouping and dementia diagnoses by aetiology; “dementia” remains current clinical terminology.

## Q47 — How do you diagnose and manage Alzheimer disease?

### Examiner is asking
Recognise the typical Alzheimer syndrome, distinguish it from other dementias and delirium, describe symptomatic management, and show awareness of the new restricted disease-modifying anti-amyloid era without overclaiming access or benefit.

### Must cover
- Insidious progressive decline, often initially amnestic with impaired new learning/episodic memory.
- Later cortical deficits may include language, praxis, recognition, visuospatial and executive problems.
- Functional decline and collateral history.
- Differential: DLB, FTD, vascular cognitive impairment, depression and delirium.
- Amyloid plaques and tau pathology as classic pathological hallmarks; keep genetics brief unless useful.
- Diagnosis is clinical plus aetiological assessment; biomarkers increase certainty when phenotype is atypical/uncertain or when anti-amyloid treatment is being considered.
- Symptomatic treatment: donepezil, rivastigmine or galantamine for mild–moderate AD; memantine for moderate AD when AChE inhibitors are contraindicated/not tolerated and for severe AD; can be combined with an AChE inhibitor in moderate/severe disease according to current NICE guidance.
- Non-drug management: function, routines, cognition, comorbidity, safety and carer support.
- Current update: lecanemab and donanemab are EU-authorised for carefully selected early symptomatic AD (MCI/mild dementia due to AD) with confirmed amyloid pathology, restricted in the EU to APOE ε4 non-carriers or heterozygotes.
- They slow decline modestly; they do not reverse established dementia or restore lost function.
- ARIA-E/ARIA-H and MRI monitoring are central safety issues.
- Do not imply routine Greek availability, reimbursement or access; that remains unverified.

### Recall spine
- Insidious progressive amnestic syndrome.
- Functional decline + differential.
- Biomarker support when needed.
- AChE inhibitor ± memantine symptomatic treatment.
- Carer/safety management.
- Anti-amyloid therapy only in selected biomarker-confirmed early disease.
- ARIA/MRI safety.

### Board facts
- Lecanemab and donanemab now have EU authorisation for selected early AD.
- Both require confirmed amyloid pathology and exclude APOE ε4 homozygotes under current EU indication.
- ARIA is the defining safety issue.

### Common traps
- Saying there is still no disease-modifying treatment at all.
- Presenting anti-amyloid therapy as routine for all AD or moderate/severe dementia.
- Equating EMA authorisation with routine Greek availability.
- Treating biomarker positivity alone as the whole clinical diagnosis.

### Exam/current distinction
Classic exam-source teaching that AD has no disease-modifying treatment is no longer literally current. The current treatments are restricted, modestly disease-slowing and specialist, not curative.

## Q48 — How do you distinguish dementia with Lewy bodies from Parkinson disease dementia?

### Examiner is asking
Recognise Lewy-body cognitive disease, contrast timing of dementia and parkinsonism, know the modern DLB core features and antipsychotic sensitivity.

### Must cover
- DLB cognitive pattern may emphasise attention/executive/visuoperceptual deficits rather than purely amnestic onset.
- Four current core clinical DLB features:
  1. fluctuating cognition;
  2. recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations;
  3. spontaneous parkinsonism;
  4. REM sleep behaviour disorder.
- RBD may precede cognitive decline by years and is now a core feature, not merely supportive.
- Supportive/autonomic features may include falls, syncope/orthostasis and other autonomic dysfunction where clinically useful.
- Severe antipsychotic sensitivity is a major safety warning.
- 1-year rule: DLB when dementia develops before, concurrently with, or within 1 year of parkinsonism; PDD when dementia develops after established PD beyond that interval.
- Explain that this remains an operational classification convention rather than a biologically validated boundary; both lie on a Lewy-body-disease spectrum.
- Indicative biomarkers such as reduced dopamine-transporter uptake may support uncertain cases; do not over-detail.
- Donepezil/rivastigmine are established for mild–moderate DLB; memantine may be considered when AChE inhibitors are not tolerated/contraindicated. PDD similarly has support for cholinesterase inhibition, especially rivastigmine.

### Recall spine
- Fluctuations.
- Visual hallucinations.
- Parkinsonism.
- RBD.
- Neuroleptic sensitivity.
- 1-year rule.
- Cholinesterase inhibition.

### Board facts
- RBD is now one of four DLB core clinical features.
- The 1-year rule remains useful operationally but is not a biological dividing line.

### Common traps
- Teaching only the old triad and leaving RBD as supportive.
- Treating the 1-year rule as mechanistically proven.
- Using potent D2-blocking antipsychotics casually in DLB.

### Exam/current distinction
Older teaching: DLB core triad plus supportive RBD. Current consensus: RBD is a core clinical feature.

## Q48a — How do you approach visual hallucinations in Parkinson disease?

### Follow-up answer must cover
- First assess delirium, infection, metabolic illness, medication toxicity and visual/sensory impairment.
- If hallucinations are well tolerated and not distressing/risky, medication is not automatically required.
- Review and rationalise provoking antiparkinsonian medication with Parkinson-specialist input, balancing motor worsening and withdrawal risk.
- Quetiapine can be considered in selected PD patients without cognitive impairment; evidence is weaker than clozapine but motor worsening is usually limited relative to potent D2 blockers.
- Clozapine is the evidence-based specialist option when standard treatment fails; requires mandatory haematological monitoring and much lower doses than schizophrenia treatment.
- Avoid olanzapine and substantial D2 blockade because of motor worsening.
- Pimavanserin is a US-oriented option; no centrally authorised EU product for PD psychosis was identified and Greek availability was not verified. Do not present it as routine European/Greek care.

### Trap
Do not jump directly from “visual hallucinations in PD” to antipsychotic treatment without first excluding delirium/medical triggers and reviewing antiparkinsonian medication.

## Q48b — What do you need to know about dopamine agonist–related impulse-control disorders?

### Follow-up answer must cover
- Classic behaviours: gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping/spending and binge eating.
- Punding/dopamine dysregulation syndrome are useful associated compulsive/reward phenomena but are not required to define the ICD syndrome.
- Dopamine agonists are the major medication risk factor.
- Ask proactively and consider collateral because behaviours may be hidden/minimised.
- First-line management is specialist-guided gradual dopamine-agonist reduction where feasible.
- Do not stop abruptly: monitor for dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (DAWS).
- If medication modification is insufficient, specialist CBT targeted at the ICD can be considered.

### Trap
Do not moralise or frame the behaviour as simple lack of willpower; it may be treatment-emergent and concealed because of shame.

## Q49 — How do you recognise and manage frontotemporal dementia?

### Examiner is asking
Recognise bvFTD as an early behavioural/executive dementia, distinguish it from psychiatric illness and AD, and avoid generic dementia pharmacotherapy.

### Must cover
- Early behavioural disinhibition.
- Apathy/inertia.
- Loss of empathy/sympathy.
- Perseverative/compulsive/stereotyped behaviour.
- Hyperorality/dietary change.
- Executive dysfunction.
- Language variants can occur; keep them concise unless useful.
- Relative preservation of episodic memory/visuospatial function is a common early pattern but not absolute; memory impairment does not exclude bvFTD.
- Differential from bipolar disorder, depression/apathy, personality change, psychosis and Alzheimer disease.
- Structural MRI may show frontal/anterior temporal atrophy; FDG-PET/perfusion SPECT may support uncertain diagnosis. Normal imaging alone does not rule it out.
- No established disease-modifying or cognitive-enhancing drug.
- Do not offer cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine as routine FTD treatment.
- SSRIs or trazodone may be used symptomatically for disinhibition, compulsive behaviour, irritability or hyperorality, but evidence is limited and treatment is symptom-targeted.
- Emphasise carer education, environmental/behavioural management, safety and multidisciplinary support.

### Recall spine
- Behaviour first.
- Disinhibition/apathy/empathy loss.
- Stereotypy + hyperorality.
- Executive dysfunction.
- MRI frontal/anterior temporal pattern.
- No AChEI/memantine routine treatment.
- Symptom-targeted/supportive care.

### Board facts
- Early memory sparing is common, not mandatory.
- AChE inhibitors and memantine should not be carried over from generic dementia algorithms into FTD.

### Common trap
Rejecting FTD because episodic memory is impaired.

### Exam/current distinction
Older generic “anti-dementia drug” thinking is inappropriate in FTD; current NICE specifically advises against AChE inhibitors and memantine.

## Q50 — How do you diagnose and manage vascular cognitive impairment / vascular dementia?

### Examiner is asking
Recognise the spectrum of vascular cognitive impairment, establish a credible vascular relationship, distinguish it from AD and mixed disease, and focus management on vascular prevention and function.

### Must cover
- VCI is the broader modern construct; vascular dementia is the dementia-level end of the spectrum.
- Course may be post-stroke, stepwise or gradual/progressive; stepwise decline is classic but not required.
- Executive dysfunction and slowed processing may be prominent, especially in subcortical/small-vessel disease.
- Focal neurological signs, gait disturbance and vascular risk factors support the diagnosis where present.
- Structural imaging should show cerebrovascular burden consistent with the clinical syndrome; imaging alone is not enough.
- Mixed Alzheimer/vascular pathology is common; do not force a false either/or diagnosis.
- Management: optimise vascular risk factors and secondary stroke prevention, treat comorbidity, promote rehabilitation, function and safety.
- Do not imply cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine are routine treatment for pure vascular dementia unless a comorbid/mixed neurodegenerative indication is present.

### Recall spine
- VCI spectrum.
- Vascular temporal/imaging relationship.
- Executive/slowed processing pattern.
- Focal signs/gait/risk factors.
- Stepwise not obligatory.
- Mixed pathology common.
- Prevention/rehabilitation central.

### Board facts
- “Stepwise decline” is a classic phenotype, not a diagnostic requirement.
- VCI is broader than vascular dementia.

### Common traps
- Diagnosing vascular dementia solely from white-matter changes.
- Treating a gradual course as evidence against vascular cognitive impairment.
- Treating AD and vascular disease as mutually exclusive.

### Exam/current distinction
Classic exam descriptions often emphasise stepwise deterioration; current VCI encompasses gradual, post-stroke and mixed trajectories.

## Q51 — How do you assess and manage behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)?

### Examiner is asking
Show that BPSD is a syndrome requiring cause-finding and non-drug management first, with tightly restricted antipsychotic use when severe distress or risk remains.

### Must cover
- Spectrum: agitation/aggression, psychosis, depression/anxiety, apathy, sleep disturbance, wandering and disinhibition.
- Before labelling behaviour as primary BPSD, look for reversible drivers: delirium, pain, infection, constipation, urinary retention, medication adverse effects, sensory impairment, sleep disruption, environmental stress and unmet needs.
- Assess risk to patient/carers/others and carer burden.
- Nonpharmacological first: person-centred environmental modification, routines, meaningful activity, communication strategies, sensory correction, sleep/comfort measures and carer support.
- Treat specific syndromes/comorbidities when indicated rather than sedating globally.
- Antipsychotics only when there is severe distress or risk of harm and benefits outweigh risks.
- Discuss cerebrovascular and mortality risk qualitatively; do not reproduce old fixed risk ratios.
- Use the lowest effective dose, shortest possible exposure, with regular review and deprescribing attempts when clinically appropriate.
- Current risperidone licensing anchor: short-term treatment, up to 6 weeks, for persistent aggression in moderate–severe Alzheimer dementia after non-drug measures fail.
- Do not teach a universal 6–12 week duration rule for all dementia antipsychotic use.

### Recall spine
- Define behaviour.
- Search for reversible drivers.
- Risk + carer burden.
- Non-drug/person-centred first.
- Treat specific syndromes.
- Antipsychotic only severe distress/harm risk.
- Review and deprescribe.

### Board facts
- Antipsychotics increase stroke/cerebrovascular and mortality risk in dementia; avoid outdated fixed relative-risk numbers.
- Risperidone’s specific licensed Alzheimer-aggression course is up to 6 weeks.

### Common traps
- Treating agitation before checking pain, delirium, infection, constipation or urinary retention.
- Using antipsychotics for convenience or routine wandering.
- Continuing antipsychotics indefinitely because they were once helpful.
- Applying the 6-week risperidone licensing limit as if it were a universal duration rule for every antipsychotic/indication.

### Exam/current distinction
Older teaching may cite broad 6–12 week antipsychotic courses and fixed stroke/mortality risk ratios. Current practice is shortest possible exposure, review at least every 6 weeks, and a specific up-to-6-week risperidone licence for persistent aggression in moderate–severe Alzheimer dementia.
