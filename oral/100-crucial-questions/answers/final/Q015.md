# Q15. What determines prognosis in schizophrenia?

## Recall spine

1. **Define the outcome domains** — symptoms, function, cognition/negative symptoms, physical health, suicide.
2. **Baseline function and illness burden**
3. **Duration of untreated psychosis**
4. **Negative symptoms and cognition**
5. **Adherence, engagement and substances**
6. **Family/social environment**
7. **Mortality and suicide**
8. **Probabilistic, not deterministic**

## Model oral answer

Prognosis in schizophrenia is **multidimensional and probabilistic**. I would not reduce it to a single question of whether hallucinations and delusions remit. Symptomatic relapse and remission, occupational and social recovery, negative and cognitive disability, physical health and mortality, and suicide risk can follow different trajectories in the same patient.

One of the most important practical distinctions is between **symptom improvement and functional recovery**. Psychosis may improve substantially while occupational, social or independent functioning remains impaired. Better baseline or global functioning is therefore a favourable signal, whereas poorer baseline functioning, greater symptom burden and more previous admissions are associated with worse remission outcomes.

**Adherence and engagement** are major practical influences. Good adherence is associated with better outcomes, while poor adherence, poor engagement and substance misuse increase the likelihood of relapse and poorer course. Persistent negative symptoms and cognitive impairment are also major contributors to long-term disability even when positive symptoms are relatively controlled.

A longer **duration of untreated psychosis** is associated with poorer symptomatic and functional outcomes. It is a useful prognostic marker, but it should be described as an association rather than as proof that duration of untreated psychosis is an independently causal determinant of outcome in every patient.

The **social and family environment** also matters. High expressed emotion is associated with increased relapse risk. Environmental deprivation or understimulation can compound withdrawal and inactivity and can make negative-looking disability worse, so prognosis is not determined by illness biology alone.

Some traditional demographic rules need to be treated cautiously. Male sex has some association with poorer symptomatic remission, but younger age at onset should not be taught as a dominant independent prognostic rule. Likewise, no single classic favourable-versus-unfavourable list predicts an individual patient with high precision.

Recovery remains possible, including later in the course of illness. A single timeless “recovery percentage” is misleading because estimates depend strongly on how recovery is defined and how long patients are followed.

Finally, prognosis includes **physical health and mortality**, not only psychiatric symptoms. Mortality is markedly increased from natural disease as well as suicide and other non-natural causes, with a broad current estimate of roughly **15–20 years of premature mortality**. Suicide risk therefore remains part of longitudinal prognosis and risk management even when the immediate psychosis is controlled.

Overall, prognostic factors can inform clinical formulation and modifiable treatment targets, but their individual predictive power is modest. They inform probability; they do not determine destiny.

## Must-know board facts

- **Duration of untreated psychosis:** longer DUP is associated with poorer symptomatic and functional outcome; treat it as a prognostic association, not proven independent causation.
- **Mortality:** schizophrenia is associated with markedly increased mortality, with roughly **15–20 years of premature mortality** as a useful broad anchor.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Equating symptomatic remission with recovery.
- Quoting old recovery percentages as universal.
- Treating younger age at onset as a dominant independent predictor.
- Presenting prognostic factors as deterministic for an individual patient.
