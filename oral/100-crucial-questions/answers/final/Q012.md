# Q12. Describe the clinical picture and diagnosis of schizophrenia.

## Recall spine

1. **Syndrome by domains** — positive, negative, disorganised, cognitive.
2. **Clinical expression** — acute positive/disorganised prominence; persistent negative/cognitive disability.
3. **Longitudinal diagnosis** — pattern, course, duration and functioning.
4. **Exclude alternatives** — mood, substance/medication, medical/neurological causes.
5. **Know the classics in context** — first-rank symptoms and historical subtypes.

## Model oral answer

Schizophrenia is a heterogeneous primary psychotic syndrome characterised by combinations of positive, negative, disorganised and cognitive abnormalities. Diagnosis is syndromic and longitudinal rather than based on any single psychotic symptom: the overall pattern, persistence and course of illness, its functional impact, and exclusion of alternative causes all matter. A first episode of psychosis is therefore not automatically schizophrenia.

The **positive-symptom** domain includes delusions and hallucinations. Auditory hallucinations are common and classically prominent, but are not uniquely defining. Passivity and thought-interference phenomena—such as thought insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting—are also classic examples of schizophrenic psychopathology, but none is sufficient for diagnosis in isolation.

**Disorganisation** includes formal thought disorder or disorganised speech, together with disorganised or inappropriate behaviour; affective incongruity may also be seen. The clinical picture can therefore involve abnormalities not only in the content of thought and perception, but also in the organisation and expression of thought and behaviour.

The **negative-symptom** domain is particularly important because it may dominate persistent functional disability. The principal domains are diminished emotional expression or blunting, alogia, avolition, anhedonia and asociality. Apparent negative symptoms should not automatically be assumed to be primary: withdrawal, inactivity or reduced expression may instead reflect depression, medication effects, active psychosis or environmental deprivation.

**Cognitive impairment** is also common and functionally important, involving attention, working memory, learning and memory, executive function and social cognition. It may contribute substantially to impaired functioning, but it is not itself diagnostic of schizophrenia. Affective symptoms and psychomotor or catatonic phenomena can also occur and must be interpreted within the overall syndrome.

The balance of these domains varies clinically. Acute presentations often foreground positive symptoms and disorganisation, whereas longer-term disability may be dominated by negative and cognitive difficulties. This is a useful description of clinical expression rather than a rigid subtype or staging system.

Diagnosis requires a **longitudinal assessment**. There must be a persistent characteristic syndrome rather than a transient or isolated psychotic phenomenon, with attention to course, duration and relevant functional change. Current diagnostic systems differ in their exact architecture; as a concrete DSM-5-TR anchor, schizophrenia requires an overall disturbance lasting at least six months, including an active-phase component. That threshold is useful for recall, but the diagnosis still depends on the whole clinical picture rather than duration alone.

Alternative explanations must be actively excluded. Prominent or sustained mood syndromes raise the possibility of an affective psychosis or schizoaffective presentation, while substance- or medication-induced states and medical or neurological causes must also be considered. The evolution of a first psychotic episode over time is therefore often diagnostically decisive.

Finally, **Schneiderian first-rank symptoms** remain important classic phenomenology, but they are neither pathognomonic nor diagnostically privileged in current classification. Likewise, the old paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic and residual schizophrenia subtypes are historical; current classification favours dimensional and longitudinal description.

## Must-know board facts

- **Classic Schneiderian phenomena to recognise** — historical lists vary, but key groupings include:
  - audible or echoed thoughts;
  - third-person voices commenting or discussing;
  - thought insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting;
  - somatic passivity;
  - delusional perception;
  - made/passivity experiences involving affect, impulse or action.
  These are classic descriptive phenomena, not pathognomonic signs.
- **DSM-5-TR duration anchor:** an overall disturbance of at least six months, including an active-phase component.
- **Classical subtypes:** paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic and residual labels are historical rather than current formal subtypes.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling first-rank symptoms pathognomonic.
- Diagnosing schizophrenia from a single cross-sectional psychotic episode.
- Presenting classical subtypes as current formal diagnoses.
- Equating withdrawal or inactivity automatically with primary negative symptoms.
- Treating cognitive impairment as a diagnostic criterion.
- Turning Q12 into the full schizophrenia-versus-schizoaffective-versus-mood-psychosis differential.

## Exam answer vs current practice

- **Classification update:** Older classifications used classical schizophrenia subtypes. Current classification no longer retains that subtype scheme; use a dimensional and longitudinal description while recognising the historical labels if asked.
- **First-rank symptoms:** Historically they were given substantial diagnostic weight. They remain useful descriptive psychopathology and classic examination material, but current classification does not treat them as pathognomonic or diagnostically privileged.
