# Q14. What do we know about the neurobiology of schizophrenia?

## Recall spine

1. **Polygenic vulnerability and development**
2. **Environmental/developmental risk**
3. **Brain circuits and connectivity**
4. **Dopamine**
5. **Glutamate/NMDA**
6. **Secondary interacting systems**
7. **No individual diagnostic biomarker**

## Model oral answer

Schizophrenia is best understood as a **biologically heterogeneous, highly polygenic disorder with neurodevelopmental and circuit-level abnormalities**, rather than as a disease explained by one gene, one neurotransmitter or one brain region. The strongest model is therefore multilevel: genetic liability interacts with developmental and environmental influences, producing alterations in brain systems that can contribute to psychosis and other symptom dimensions. None of these findings currently provides a diagnostic biomarker for an individual patient.

Genetically, schizophrenia is **highly polygenic**. Modern genomic findings support contributions from many common variants together with rarer variants; no single gene is necessary or sufficient. Genetic liability interacts with environmental and developmental exposures rather than determining illness in a simple way.

A **neurodevelopmental framework** remains useful because developmental and synaptic biology appear relevant, and cognitive, social or motor abnormalities can precede the onset of psychosis. Environmental and developmental associations include cannabis exposure, migration or ethnic-minority status, urbanicity, childhood adversity and obstetric or other developmental exposures. These are probabilistic risk associations, not deterministic explanations for why a particular individual develops schizophrenia, and the neurodevelopmental model does not account equally well for every illness course.

At the neurochemical level, **dopamine** remains one of the most robust findings. Increased striatal dopamine synthesis and release is a reproducible group-level abnormality and is particularly relevant to positive psychotic symptoms. But it is not universal across all patients and cannot be used as a diagnostic test.

**Glutamatergic and NMDA-receptor hypofunction models** are also important research frameworks. They offer a broader way of thinking about symptoms and circuit dysfunction, but they should not be presented as a single proven primary mechanism with dopamine merely downstream. The hierarchy between these mechanisms remains less settled than a simple pathway would imply.

Structural, functional and connectivity studies also show reproducible **group-level abnormalities**, including findings involving hippocampal, thalamic and cortical systems and broader dysconnectivity. Their limitation clinically is substantial overlap with healthy controls and with other psychiatric disorders, so they do not identify schizophrenia reliably in an individual patient.

Other systems—including **GABA/interneuron, serotonin and immune or inflammatory mechanisms**—may contribute or interact with the core processes, but their evidential status is less definitive than the polygenic, neurodevelopmental and dopamine findings.

The clinically important conclusion is that schizophrenia has a strong biological basis but marked heterogeneity. No routine MRI, PET, genetic test or laboratory biomarker currently diagnoses schizophrenia in an individual patient.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Giving a dopamine-only answer.
- Presenting environmental associations as deterministic causes.
- Treating one structural or functional imaging finding as diagnostic.
- Presenting the glutamate–dopamine hierarchy as settled fact.
