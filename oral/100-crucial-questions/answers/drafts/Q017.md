# Q17. How do you manage schizophrenia long term?

## Recall spine

1. **Shared longitudinal relapse/recovery plan**
2. **Maintenance antipsychotic strategy**
3. **Duration and discontinuation decisions**
4. **Adherence and LAIs**
5. **Physical-health monitoring**
6. **Psychological, family and vocational rehabilitation**
7. **Reassess persistent withdrawal before calling it primary negative symptoms**

## Model oral answer

Long-term management of schizophrenia should be a **shared, longitudinal recovery and relapse-prevention plan**, not simply continued prescribing. I would monitor each patient’s relapse signature, adherence, substance use, suicide risk, social and occupational functioning, carer or family needs and personal recovery goals, and adjust treatment as these change over time.

After improvement, an effective **antipsychotic maintenance regimen** should usually be continued, using the lowest effective and tolerated regimen rather than routine intermittent or symptom-triggered treatment. The duration is individualised. After a first episode there is not one universal number that fits every patient: current guidance includes at least **7–12 months after remission**, while other guidance emphasises that relapse risk remains high if treatment is stopped within the first **1–2 years**. These figures should support shared decision-making rather than become an automatic stopping date.

Recurrent illness generally favours **longer maintenance**, taking account of the number and severity of episodes, consequences of previous relapse, residual symptoms, adherence, adverse effects and patient preference. If discontinuation is chosen, antipsychotics should be withdrawn gradually and the patient monitored closely. Continued monitoring for at least **2 years after withdrawal** is a useful practical anchor.

Adherence should be discussed openly. **Long-acting injectable antipsychotics** are an option both when the patient prefers them and when poor, covert or uncertain adherence is a priority. They should not be framed only as an enforcement strategy; product-specific initiation and oral-overlap requirements need to be followed for the chosen preparation.

Long-term care must also address **physical health**. Monitoring should include weight and waist, blood pressure and pulse, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement disorders and broader physical health, with ECG assessment when clinically or product-information indicated. Long-term outcome includes physical morbidity and mortality, not only relapse prevention.

**Psychosocial treatment is core care.** CBT for psychosis, family intervention and psychoeducation, coordinated specialty or early-psychosis care, vocational rehabilitation or supported employment, and work on social recovery all contribute to the long-term plan.

Persistent withdrawal or inactivity needs careful reassessment. Before calling it a primary or persistent negative syndrome, I would look for residual positive symptoms, depression, extrapyramidal symptoms or parkinsonism, sedation or other medication effects, substance use, cognitive impairment and environmental deprivation or understimulation. Apparent negative symptoms caused by these factors require different management and should not be mistaken for antipsychotic treatment resistance.

Cognitive impairment is clinically important, but there is no established routine medication that specifically corrects it. Rehabilitation and functional support therefore remain important components of care.

## Must-know board facts

- **After first-episode remission:** a useful current minimum is **7–12 months** of maintenance treatment.
- **Stopping early:** relapse risk is high if treatment is withdrawn within the first **1–2 years**.
- **If discontinuing:** withdraw gradually and continue close monitoring; at least **2 years of monitoring after withdrawal** is a useful anchor.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q17a. The patient remains withdrawn and inactive despite improvement in psychosis. What do you consider?

Work through secondary explanations before diagnosing a primary negative syndrome: **residual positive symptoms → depression → EPS/parkinsonism → sedation or other medication effects → substances → cognitive impairment → environmental deprivation or understimulation → only then primary/persistent negative symptoms.**

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Framing LAIs only as coercion or adherence enforcement.
- Equating persistent withdrawal with failed antipsychotic efficacy.
- Labelling negative symptoms without excluding secondary negative-like states.
- Neglecting physical-health morbidity and mortality in long-term care.
