# Q40. How do you diagnose and manage opioid use disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise overdose and give naloxone**
2. **Assess withdrawal and dependence**
3. **Prefer maintenance treatment over detoxification alone**
4. **Use methadone or buprenorphine safely**
5. **Provide take-home naloxone and overdose education**
6. **Apply the current Greek naloxone pathway**
7. **Address injecting and infectious-disease harms**

## Model oral answer

Opioid use disorder should be managed as a chronic relapsing condition with **overdose prevention, opioid agonist treatment and harm reduction**, not with detoxification as the default goal. I would first identify any acute overdose, then assess dependence and withdrawal, offer evidence-based maintenance treatment with methadone or buprenorphine where appropriate, and address infectious, injecting and psychosocial harms.

In **opioid overdose**, the classic clinical picture is depressed consciousness with **respiratory depression**, often with miosis. Management prioritises airway and ventilatory support together with **naloxone**. Because naloxone may wear off before the opioid effect has resolved, repeat dosing and continued observation may be necessary.

Withdrawal can be recognised clinically, but detoxification alone should not be presented as the preferred treatment. Reducing or stopping opioids lowers tolerance, and subsequent relapse can therefore carry increased overdose risk. For many patients, **opioid agonist treatment with methadone or buprenorphine is standard long-term care**, not merely a bridge toward detoxification.

Both medicines require safe induction. **Methadone induction is a high-risk period**, so increases should be cautious and should take account of respiratory risk, interactions and QT considerations. With **buprenorphine**, starting while substantial full-agonist opioid effect remains can precipitate withdrawal. The correct principle is to begin when clinically significant withdrawal is present, rather than memorising one universal number of hours or one fixed COWS cutoff for every opioid and patient.

Overdose prevention should be routine. Patients and, where appropriate, people close to them should receive **overdose education and take-home or community naloxone**. In Greece, there was an important access change in **July 2025**: intranasal and prefilled injectable naloxone may be prescribed by physicians of any specialty, dispensed through private pharmacies and administered by anyone in an overdose emergency. That update should be stated directly without inventing additional dispensing or supervision requirements.

Within the Greek treatment system, **OKANA programmes use methadone and buprenorphine as part of integrated treatment**. The relevant viva point is the availability of evidence-based opioid agonist treatment; I would not import supervision or pharmacy rules from another jurisdiction unless specifically verified.

I would also assess harms related to injecting and chronic opioid use: **HBV, HCV and HIV risk, vaccination and testing needs, skin and soft-tissue infection, endocarditis risk and access to needle/syringe services** where relevant. Psychiatric comorbidity, suicide risk, housing, social stability and concurrent sedative or alcohol use also influence overdose risk and treatment planning.

The overall approach is therefore acute overdose treatment when required, followed by maintenance-oriented care with methadone or buprenorphine, naloxone access, infectious-disease and injecting-harm reduction, and longitudinal psychosocial treatment rather than a detoxification-only model.

## Must-know board facts

- **Detoxification lowers tolerance** and can increase later overdose risk if opioid use resumes.
- **Opioid agonist treatment is maintenance treatment**, not simply a detoxification bridge.
- **Greece, July 2025:** intranasal and prefilled injectable naloxone may be prescribed by physicians of any specialty, dispensed through private pharmacies and administered by anyone in an overdose emergency.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Presenting detoxification as the preferred default treatment.
- Using one fixed buprenorphine timing or COWS rule for all opioids.
- Forgetting airway and ventilatory support in overdose while focusing only on naloxone.
- Inventing Greek methadone or buprenorphine dispensing and supervision rules.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Treatment model:** Older detoxification-centred teaching is inadequate for many patients. Current care emphasises maintenance opioid agonist treatment, overdose prevention and harm reduction.

**Greek naloxone access:** The July 2025 change materially expanded prescribing, pharmacy dispensing and emergency administration of naloxone.
