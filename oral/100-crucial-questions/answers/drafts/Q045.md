# Q45. How do you recognise, assess and manage delirium?

## Recall spine

1. **Acute and fluctuating course**
2. **Impaired attention and awareness**
3. **Recognise hyperactive, hypoactive and mixed forms**
4. **Find and treat the cause**
5. **Use 4AT in general settings**
6. **Non-drug measures first**
7. **Antipsychotic only for severe distress or risk**

## Model oral answer

Delirium is an **acute or subacute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with associated cognitive change**, usually caused by one or more medical, medication-related, intoxication or withdrawal factors. The priorities are to recognise it promptly, identify and treat the cause, and manage the patient safely with supportive and environmental measures. It is important not to miss **hypoactive delirium**, which may present with reduced activity and quiet withdrawal rather than agitation.

The course is usually over **hours to days** and fluctuates during the day. Impaired attention is central: the patient may be distractible, unable to sustain or shift attention appropriately, and variably aware of the environment. Other cognitive or perceptual abnormalities can accompany this. Clinically, delirium may be hyperactive, hypoactive or mixed, so agitation is neither necessary nor sufficient for the diagnosis.

Assessment should focus immediately on **cause-finding**. I would obtain collateral to establish baseline cognition and the time course, review all prescribed and non-prescribed medication and substance exposure, and perform physical and neurological examination. Common contributors include infection, hypoxia, dehydration or metabolic disturbance, pain, constipation or urinary retention, medication effects, intoxication or withdrawal, sensory impairment and environmental disruption. Investigations should be targeted to the presentation rather than used as an indiscriminate panel.

For screening in a general hospital or long-term-care setting, the current practical anchor is the **4AT**. In critical care or post-anaesthetic recovery, CAM-ICU or ICDSC may be more appropriate. These tools support recognition but do not replace the clinical diagnosis or the search for an underlying cause.

Management is primarily **treatment of the precipitating factors plus supportive care**. I would optimise hydration and nutrition, mobilise when possible, provide glasses and hearing aids, maintain normal sleep-wake and daylight cues, reorient regularly, involve familiar carers, reduce unnecessary ward moves and avoid restraints where possible. The environment should be calm and safe without excessive stimulation or deprivation.

**Antipsychotics do not treat the underlying delirium.** They should only be considered when the patient has severe distress or poses a significant risk and de-escalation or other non-drug measures have failed or are inappropriate. If haloperidol is required, use the lowest appropriate dose for the shortest possible period, usually no more than about one week. Parkinson disease and dementia with Lewy bodies are important contraindications in current safety guidance.

The main differentials are dementia, depression, primary psychosis and sometimes catatonia. Dementia is usually more insidious and progressive; depression does not typically produce an acute fluctuating inattention syndrome; and primary psychosis usually occurs with clearer attention and consciousness. The combination of acute onset, fluctuation and impaired attention should always push delirium high on the differential.

## Must-know board facts

- **4AT** is the current general hospital/long-term-care screening anchor when delirium is suspected.
- **CAM-ICU or ICDSC** are preferred in critical care/post-anaesthetic settings.
- Haloperidol is not routine delirium treatment and is contraindicated in **Parkinson disease and DLB**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Missing **hypoactive delirium**.
- Calling any agitation in an older patient delirium without showing acute fluctuating inattention.
- Treating a screening score as the diagnosis.
- Using antipsychotics as though they cure or shorten delirium.
- Treating “sundowning” as a defining criterion.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Screening update:** Older teaching often centres on CAM. In current general hospital and long-term-care practice, **4AT** is the main screening anchor when delirium indicators are present.
