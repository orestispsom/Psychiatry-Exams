# Q48. How do you distinguish dementia with Lewy bodies from Parkinson disease dementia?

## Recall spine

1. **Fluctuating cognition**
2. **Well-formed visual hallucinations**
3. **Spontaneous parkinsonism**
4. **REM sleep behaviour disorder**
5. **Remember antipsychotic sensitivity**
6. **Apply the 1-year rule operationally**
7. **Use cholinesterase inhibition where appropriate**

## Model oral answer

Dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson disease dementia are closely related Lewy-body disorders and are distinguished mainly by the **timing of dementia relative to parkinsonism**. DLB should be recognised from its characteristic cognitive profile and four core clinical features, while the traditional one-year rule separates DLB from PDD operationally rather than biologically. Antipsychotic sensitivity is an important safety issue in both, especially DLB.

The cognitive pattern in **DLB** may be less purely amnestic than Alzheimer disease, with prominent problems in attention, executive function and visuoperceptual processing. The four current core clinical features are **fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism and REM sleep behaviour disorder**. RBD is particularly important because it may precede cognitive decline by years and is now a core feature rather than merely a supportive sign.

Other features can support the diagnosis, including falls, syncope or orthostatic symptoms and other autonomic dysfunction. When the clinical diagnosis remains uncertain, indicative biomarkers such as reduced dopamine-transporter uptake can support a Lewy-body diagnosis, but they should not replace the clinical syndrome.

The practical distinction from **Parkinson disease dementia** uses the one-year convention. If dementia develops before, at the same time as, or within one year of parkinsonism, the classification is DLB. If dementia develops after established Parkinson disease beyond that interval, it is classified as PDD. This is useful for communication and classification, but it is not a biologically validated boundary; the two conditions lie on a Lewy-body-disease spectrum.

Management should reflect the cognitive and neuropsychiatric burden while avoiding treatments likely to worsen motor function or cognition. **Donepezil or rivastigmine** are established treatments for mild to moderate DLB, and memantine may be considered when acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated or are contraindicated. Cholinesterase inhibition is also supported in PDD, particularly rivastigmine.

A major safety point is **severe antipsychotic sensitivity**. Potent dopamine D2 blockade can cause marked deterioration, so antipsychotics should never be used casually in DLB. If psychotic symptoms occur, first look for delirium, medical triggers and medication contributors, then use specialist-guided treatment when symptoms are distressing or dangerous.

The high-yield distinction is therefore not “DLB equals hallucinations and PDD equals parkinsonism.” Both can have cognitive, motor and psychotic symptoms. The diagnosis depends on the characteristic Lewy-body clinical syndrome, the modern four-feature DLB framework and the timing of cognitive decline relative to established Parkinson disease.

## Must-know board facts

- The four DLB core clinical features are **fluctuating cognition, recurrent visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism and REM sleep behaviour disorder**.
- The **1-year rule** is an operational classification convention, not a biological dividing line.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q48a. How do you approach visual hallucinations in Parkinson disease?

First exclude **delirium, infection, metabolic illness, medication toxicity and visual or sensory impairment**. If hallucinations are well tolerated and not causing distress or risk, medication is not automatically required.

Review potentially provoking antiparkinsonian treatment with Parkinson-specialist input, balancing the possible improvement in psychosis against motor deterioration and withdrawal risk. If antipsychotic treatment is needed, **quetiapine** can be considered in selected patients without cognitive impairment, although its evidence is weaker than clozapine. **Clozapine** is the evidence-based specialist option when standard treatment fails and requires mandatory haematological monitoring; doses used are much lower than in schizophrenia treatment.

Avoid olanzapine and substantial D2 blockade because of motor worsening. **Pimavanserin should not be presented as routine European or Greek care**; it is mainly a US-oriented option and centrally authorised EU treatment for Parkinson psychosis was not established.

### Q48b. What do you need to know about dopamine agonist–related impulse-control disorders?

Typical behaviours include **gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping or spending and binge eating**. Punding and dopamine dysregulation syndrome are related compulsive or reward phenomena but are not required to define the syndrome.

Dopamine agonists are the major medication risk factor. Ask proactively and consider collateral because patients may hide or minimise behaviours through shame. First-line management is **specialist-guided gradual reduction of the dopamine agonist where feasible**. Do not stop it abruptly because dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome can occur. If medication adjustment is insufficient, specialist CBT targeted to the impulse-control disorder can be considered.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Teaching only the old DLB triad and omitting RBD.
- Treating the 1-year rule as biologically proven.
- Using potent D2-blocking antipsychotics casually in DLB.
- Jumping directly to antipsychotics for Parkinson hallucinations before excluding delirium and reviewing medication.
- Abruptly stopping dopamine agonists or moralising treatment-emergent impulse-control behaviour.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**DLB update:** Older teaching often presents a core triad with RBD as supportive. Current consensus includes **REM sleep behaviour disorder as one of four core clinical features**.
