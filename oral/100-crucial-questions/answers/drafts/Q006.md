# Q06. How do you assess the risk of violence or aggression?

## Recall spine

1. **Previous violence**
2. **Current symptoms and substances**
3. **Threats, means and likely targets**
4. **Triggers and situational context**
5. **Static versus dynamic drivers**
6. **Scenario-based formulation**
7. **Operational management plan**

## Model oral answer

Violence risk assessment should be **structured, scenario-based and management-focused**. Previous violence is an important historical signal, but no single factor determines outcome; the task is to identify plausible near-term scenarios, the factors making them more or less likely, and the actions that can reduce risk.

I would start with the history of **previous aggression or violence**: what happened, severity, victims or targets, triggers, intoxication, consequences and whether there is a recurring pattern. I would then assess the current mental state, including symptoms that may be relevant to behaviour, substance use or intoxication, impulsivity, cognitive or communication problems, adherence and current social or environmental stressors.

Current **threats and intent** need to be explored directly. I would ask whether there is a specific person or group the patient is focused on, access to weapons or other means, how immediate the threat is, and what circumstances might trigger action. Likely victims and their safety should be considered where relevant.

The context matters as much as the diagnosis. I would identify **dynamic and environmental triggers** such as conflict, crowding, perceived provocation, substance availability or an interactional style that has previously escalated the patient. I would also ask what has helped before: more space, a particular staff approach, supportive people, medication, removal of a trigger or another de-escalation strategy.

I would distinguish relatively **static or background factors** from dynamic factors that can be changed now. The formulation should then use calibrated language: for example, what violent scenario is plausible, how imminent it appears, the likely severity and target, and which modifiable drivers are operating. I would avoid statements that the person “will” or “will not” become violent.

Structured tools may support assessment in defined settings. Short-term inpatient instruments such as the **BVC or DASA-IV** can be useful adjuncts, but they do not replace clinical formulation and should never be treated as deterministic.

The assessment must end in an **operational plan**: known triggers and warning signs, preferred de-escalation methods, observation and engagement strategy, management of intoxication or substance use, steps to protect potential victims, and clear contingencies if risk escalates. Relevant risk information and the reasoning behind the plan should be documented and communicated to receiving teams.

The central principle is that risk assessment is useful only if it changes management. A list of historical risk factors without a plausible scenario and a plan to reduce the modifiable drivers is incomplete.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Equating mental illness with dangerousness.
- Treating a structured or actuarial score as deterministic.
- Listing risk factors without translating them into management.
- Ignoring environmental and interactional triggers.
