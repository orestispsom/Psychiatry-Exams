# Q32. How do you diagnose and treat post-traumatic stress disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Establish qualifying trauma exposure**
2. **Re-experiencing in the present**
3. **Avoidance**
4. **Persistent sense of current threat**
5. **Assess impairment and differential**
6. **Recognise Complex PTSD when DSO is present**
7. **Use trauma-focused CBT or EMDR first-line**

## Model oral answer

Post-traumatic stress disorder follows exposure to an **extremely threatening or horrific event or series of events** and is defined by a trauma-specific syndrome, not by distress alone. In current ICD-11 the core architecture is deliberately compact: **re-experiencing in the present, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat**, together with clinically significant impairment. Treatment should prioritise trauma-focused psychotherapy, particularly trauma-focused CBT or EMDR.

The first domain is **re-experiencing in the present**. This is more than remembering or thinking about the trauma; the experience has a here-and-now quality, for example intrusive reliving, flashbacks or trauma-related nightmares. The second domain is **avoidance** of internal reminders such as thoughts and feelings or external reminders such as situations, people or places associated with the trauma. The third is a **persistent sense of current threat**, which may appear as hypervigilance or exaggerated startle.

I would then assess the degree of functional impairment, associated depression or substance use, dissociative symptoms where relevant, and immediate risk. The differential includes acute stress responses, adjustment disorder, depressive and other anxiety disorders, dissociative conditions, substance-related presentations and medical or neurological conditions when the clinical picture suggests them.

**Complex PTSD** in ICD-11 requires the full PTSD syndrome plus disturbances in self-organisation. These additional domains are **affect dysregulation, persistent negative self-concept and relationship difficulties**. It should not simply be used as a synonym for “very severe PTSD,” and it is not interchangeable with borderline personality disorder even though some clinical features can overlap.

For treatment, **individual trauma-focused CBT** is a first-line intervention. **EMDR** is also an established first-line trauma-focused treatment in adults. The treatment should directly address traumatic memories and their maintaining effects rather than relying on generic supportive therapy alone.

Medication should not routinely be preferred over trauma-focused psychotherapy when an effective psychological treatment is acceptable and available. If medication is preferred or clinically appropriate, an **SSRI or venlafaxine** is a reasonable option.

One important negative recommendation is that **routine single-session psychological debriefing should not be offered to prevent or treat PTSD**. Early support after trauma is not the same as compulsory debriefing, and the latter should not be presented as a standard therapeutic intervention.

The practical viva answer is therefore: confirm the trauma-specific syndrome, organise it around the three ICD-11 domains, look for Complex PTSD only when the additional self-organisation disturbances are present, and prioritise trauma-focused CBT or EMDR over routine pharmacological treatment.

## Must-know board facts

- **ICD-11 PTSD:** re-experiencing in the present + avoidance + persistent sense of current threat.
- **ICD-11 Complex PTSD:** PTSD plus affect dysregulation, persistent negative self-concept and relationship difficulties.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling any distress after trauma PTSD.
- Turning the answer into a long DSM symptom checklist.
- Calling routine psychological debriefing preventive treatment.
- Making medication the default first choice over trauma-focused psychotherapy.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification:** ICD-11 includes a formal Complex PTSD diagnosis. DSM-5-TR does not contain a directly equivalent CPTSD category.
