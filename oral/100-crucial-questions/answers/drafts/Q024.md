# Q24. How do you treat acute mania?

## Recall spine

1. **Risk, setting and medical differential**
2. **Stop or reconsider destabilising antidepressants**
3. **Antipsychotic, lithium or valproate/divalproex**
4. **Monotherapy versus early combination by severity**
5. **Short-term benzodiazepine adjunct if needed**
6. **ECT for severe or refractory mania**
7. **Apply current valproate reproductive restrictions**

## Model oral answer

Acute mania requires simultaneous assessment of **risk, cause and severity**, while antimanic treatment is started promptly. I would assess danger to self or others, behavioural control, psychosis, severe agitation, sleep deprivation, substance use, capacity, medical or neurological causes, current medication and the patient’s ability to adhere. Severe mania often requires urgent specialist or inpatient management, with containment and physical assessment proceeding in parallel where possible.

If mania or hypomania has emerged while the patient is taking an **antidepressant**, stopping or considering stopping the antidepressant is part of acute management. Substance intoxication or withdrawal and relevant medical causes also need active treatment rather than assuming the syndrome is purely psychiatric.

The main antimanic drug classes are **antipsychotics, lithium and valproate/divalproex**. There is no single universal hierarchy that applies to every guideline or patient. A practical viva answer is therefore principle-based: an antipsychotic is often a pragmatic initial choice, while lithium and valproate/divalproex are also established antimanic treatments. The choice should reflect previous response, current severity, comorbidity, tolerability, reproductive considerations and the need for ongoing maintenance.

For **less severe mania**, monotherapy may be reasonable. In **more severe mania**, or when a faster and more robust response is needed, combination treatment is appropriate early—typically an antipsychotic with lithium or valproate/divalproex. This is preferable to presenting combination therapy merely as a late rescue strategy after prolonged severe illness.

Short-term **benzodiazepines** may be used adjunctively for severe agitation or insomnia while definitive antimanic treatment takes effect, but they are not the core treatment of mania. Supportive management also matters: reduce stimulation, restore sleep, hydration and nutrition, manage substances and involve carers where appropriate.

**ECT** remains an important option when mania is prolonged, severe, refractory or life-threatening. It should not be excluded simply because the syndrome is manic rather than depressive.

Whenever **valproate** is considered, current reproductive safety restrictions are essential. It must **not be used for bipolar disorder during pregnancy**. In girls and women able to become pregnant, it should not be used unless the conditions of a Pregnancy Prevention Programme are met and alternative treatments are ineffective or not tolerated. For men, current European precautions require specialist review and counselling about a potential paternal-exposure neurodevelopmental signal, effective contraception for the man and female partner during treatment and for at least **3 months after stopping**, and avoidance of sperm donation during that period.

The overall approach is therefore: secure safety and appropriate setting, correct destabilising factors, start an evidence-based antimanic treatment, use combination therapy early when severity warrants it, and keep reproductive safety integral to any valproate decision.

## Must-know board facts

- **Severe mania:** antipsychotic plus lithium or divalproex may be appropriate early when a stronger or faster response is required.
- **Valproate:** prohibited for bipolar disorder during pregnancy and subject to current reproductive-safety precautions in women able to conceive and in men.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating agitation alone and forgetting the manic syndrome or medical differential.
- Continuing antidepressants uncritically during mania.
- Calling valproate “first line” without the current reproductive restrictions.
- Using benzodiazepines as the definitive antimanic treatment.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Treatment hierarchy:** Major guidelines differ in sequencing, so an old fixed antimanic ranking should not be presented as universal current truth.

**Valproate:** Older routine use without reproductive qualification is outdated. Current European reproductive restrictions must accompany any recommendation of valproate in bipolar disorder.
