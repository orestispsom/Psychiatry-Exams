# Q61. How do you assess new psychiatric or behavioural symptoms in an adult with intellectual disability?

## Recall spine

1. **Establish developmental and adaptive baseline**
2. **Define what has changed**
3. **Use collateral and communication supports**
4. **Search for physical causes first**
5. **Assess psychiatric and environmental causes**
6. **Review medication effects**
7. **Formulate risk and use least-restrictive treatment**

## Model oral answer

When an adult with intellectual disability develops new psychiatric or behavioural symptoms, I would first establish the person’s **baseline and the nature of the change**, rather than assuming the behaviour is part of the intellectual disability. Diagnostic overshadowing is a major risk. New aggression, withdrawal, sleep disturbance or apparent psychiatric symptoms may reflect pain, medical illness, medication effects, environmental change or a psychiatric disorder.

Intellectual disability itself is defined by significant limitations in **intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour** arising in the developmental period. Severity should therefore not be reduced to an IQ band. For the current assessment, I would clarify baseline communication, adaptive functioning, behaviour, sleep, independence and support needs, and obtain collateral from carers or family who know the person well.

The next question is: **what has changed, when, and in what context?** In someone whose verbal communication is limited, behavioural change may be the main expression of distress. I would actively look for pain and common physical causes such as dental disease, constipation, urinary retention or infection, other infection, injury, seizures, reflux and sleep disorder. Medication toxicity, withdrawal and other physical contributors also need review before attributing symptoms to a primary psychiatric disorder.

Medication review is particularly important because polypharmacy may alter behaviour or cognition. I would examine recent medication changes, anticholinergic burden, akathisia or other extrapyramidal effects, and behavioural adverse effects from antiseizure medication where relevant.

Once physical causes have been assessed, I would consider psychiatric syndromes using the person’s baseline and communication style rather than expecting a textbook verbal presentation. Mood, psychotic, anxiety and other symptoms may need to be inferred partly from behaviour and collateral, but the threshold for diagnosis should not be lowered simply because the patient has intellectual disability. Environmental and communication factors also matter: changes in routine, excessive demands, sensory stress, interpersonal conflict or unmet needs may maintain behaviour.

Risk assessment should include harm to self or others, vulnerability, neglect and safeguarding concerns. The formulation should integrate developmental level, communication, medical and psychiatric factors, medication, environment and the function of the behaviour.

Management should be **least restrictive and cause-directed**. Physical illness is treated, communication and environmental interventions are optimised, and psychological or behavioural strategies are used where possible. Antipsychotics are **not routine treatment for behaviour that challenges**. They are reserved for severe risk or when appropriate physical, psychiatric, psychological and environmental interventions have failed or are insufficient, with a clear target symptom, the minimum effective dose and early review.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q61a. How would you approach a minimally verbal adult with new aggression and insomnia?

I would treat an abrupt change in aggression and sleep as a possible sign of **pain or medical illness until shown otherwise**. I would prioritise dental pain, constipation, urinary problems or UTI, other infection, injury, seizures, medication effects or withdrawal, sleep disruption and environmental or abuse concerns, using carers’ knowledge of the person’s usual behaviour.

The key error would be to jump directly to sedation or antipsychotic treatment before establishing why the behaviour has changed.

## Must-know board facts

- **Adaptive functioning is central** to current intellectual-disability assessment.
- Avoid **diagnostic overshadowing**.
- With new behavioural change, think **medical cause first**.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older severity models centred heavily on IQ bands. Current assessment integrates intellectual functioning with **adaptive functioning and support needs**.
