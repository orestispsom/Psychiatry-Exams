# Q65. How do you assess and treat chronic insomnia?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm insomnia despite adequate opportunity**
2. **Establish daytime impairment**
3. **Identify schedule, substances, medication and comorbidity**
4. **Exclude other sleep disorders**
5. **Use CBT-I as core treatment**
6. **Use medication selectively**
7. **Reassess persistence and maintaining factors**

## Model oral answer

Chronic insomnia is persistent difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep or waking too early **despite adequate opportunity for sleep**, with meaningful daytime consequences. I would first establish the sleep pattern and exclude another sleep, medical, psychiatric, substance-related or medication-related explanation. **CBT-I is the preferred long-term first-line treatment; sleep hygiene alone is not an adequate treatment programme.**

I would clarify onset and duration, usual bedtime and wake time, time spent awake in bed, night-time awakenings, naps and variability between workdays and free days. A **sleep diary** can help distinguish insomnia from irregular scheduling or a circadian problem. I would also ask about caffeine, alcohol and other substances, prescribed and non-prescribed medication, pain, mood and anxiety symptoms, and environmental or behavioural factors that perpetuate poor sleep.

The differential includes insufficient sleep opportunity, obstructive sleep apnoea, restless legs syndrome, circadian rhythm disorders and sleep disturbance secondary to medical or psychiatric illness. The key point is to diagnose the sleep problem rather than simply treating the complaint of “not sleeping.”

The main treatment is **cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia**. Its components can include stimulus control, sleep restriction or compression, cognitive work around unhelpful sleep beliefs and relapse-prevention strategies. Sleep hygiene—regular routines, appropriate sleep environment and reduction of obvious disruptors—is useful support, but by itself usually does not address the learned arousal and behavioural cycles that maintain chronic insomnia.

Medication has a more selective role. Hypnotics may be considered when CBT-I is unavailable, unsuitable, declined or insufficient, but chronic benzodiazepine or Z-drug treatment should not be the routine default. Medication choice should be individualised to the clinical context rather than used as a substitute for assessment of the underlying sleep disorder.

A current European option is **daridorexant**, which is authorised for chronic insomnia with daytime impairment. It is positioned as an option when CBT-I has failed or is unavailable or unsuitable. I would keep its use within an appropriate clinical assessment rather than presenting it as replacing CBT-I. It is contraindicated in narcolepsy.

The overall approach is therefore to define the insomnia syndrome carefully, distinguish it from inadequate sleep opportunity and other sleep disorders, use CBT-I as the central long-term treatment, and add selected medication only when clinically justified.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q65a. How do you distinguish delayed sleep-wake phase disorder from insomnia?

Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is a **circadian delay**: the person has difficulty falling asleep and waking at conventional times but can have normal sleep quality and duration when allowed to follow the preferred later schedule. In insomnia, sleep remains difficult despite appropriate timing and adequate opportunity.

Management is based on schedule regularisation and appropriately timed circadian interventions such as melatonin and light at a high level. I would not give one rigid universal timing protocol without individual sleep-phase assessment.

## Exam answer vs current practice

CBT-I remains the preferred long-term first-line treatment. A current update is that **daridorexant is now a genuine European pharmacological option** for selected chronic insomnia, rather than the older framework being limited to CBT-I and traditional hypnotics.
