# Q85. How do you use medication for adult ADHD?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm diagnosis and indication**
2. **Baseline cardiovascular assessment**
3. **Methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine first-line**
4. **Switch between stimulant options if needed**
5. **Atomoxetine after both are unsuitable/ineffective**
6. **Monitor BP, pulse and weight**
7. **Misuse and diversion risk**

## Model oral answer

When medication is indicated for adult ADHD, the current high-level sequence is **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate as first-line pharmacological options**, followed by a trial of the alternative stimulant if an adequate trial is ineffective or not suitable. **Atomoxetine** is the principal non-stimulant option after intolerance or nonresponse to both stimulants. Treatment should follow a confirmed developmental ADHD diagnosis and be accompanied by cardiovascular, weight and misuse/diversion assessment and monitoring.

Before starting medication I would review the patient’s medical and psychiatric history, current medicines and substance use. Baseline assessment should include a **cardiovascular history, pulse and blood pressure, weight**, and assessment of substance misuse or diversion risk. A routine ECG is **not required in every patient**; it is used when the history, examination or other clinical factors make it indicated.

Choice between methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine should be individualised. If one has been given an adequate trial and is ineffective or poorly tolerated, the other can be tried rather than concluding immediately that all stimulant treatment has failed. If both are ineffective or not tolerated, atomoxetine is the principal non-stimulant option in the current sequence.

Follow-up should establish that medication is improving **clinically meaningful functioning**, not merely changing a questionnaire score. I would monitor **blood pressure and pulse**, weight and adverse effects over treatment, while reviewing adherence and whether the chosen formulation fits the patient’s daily needs.

Misuse and diversion risk should be assessed explicitly but without assuming that every adult requesting treatment is misusing medication. The treatment plan can be adapted to the risk profile, including choice of formulation and closer follow-up when appropriate.

Psychiatric comorbidity also matters. A history of anxiety, substance use or other psychiatric illness does not automatically exclude pharmacotherapy, but active conditions may change the order of treatment and the intensity of monitoring.

The concise viva answer is therefore: confirm adult ADHD properly, perform cardiovascular and misuse/diversion assessment, use methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine first, switch between them if needed, move to atomoxetine after both are unsuitable or ineffective, and monitor cardiovascular parameters, weight, response and misuse risk over time.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q85a. What if the patient has a previous psychosis or a substance use disorder?

Neither a history of psychosis nor a substance use disorder is automatically an **absolute stimulant contraindication**. The key is the current clinical state and the risk formulation.

If psychosis or mania is active, I would stabilise it first and then reassess the ADHD treatment plan. With a substance use disorder, I would assess current use, misuse and diversion risk, favour a **long-acting or prodrug formulation** when appropriate, consider non-stimulant treatment where it fits the case, and monitor closely. The diagnosis of ADHD and the substance-use problem should both be treated on their merits rather than using the history alone as an automatic exclusion.
