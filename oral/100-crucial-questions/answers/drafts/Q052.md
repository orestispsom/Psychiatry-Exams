# Q52. How do you assess and manage severe self-neglect or squalor when the patient refuses help?

## Recall spine

1. **Immediate physical and environmental risk**
2. **Identify the underlying cause**
3. **Collateral and home/environmental assessment**
4. **Decision-specific capacity and validity of refusal**
5. **Least-restrictive multidisciplinary plan**
6. **Greek involuntary-admission criteria, not self-neglect alone**

## Model oral answer

Severe self-neglect is not a diagnosis and it is not, by itself, a justification for involuntary psychiatric admission. The task is to establish the immediate risks, understand whether the behaviour reflects an autonomous but unwise choice or impaired decision-making due to mental or neurological disorder, and then use the least restrictive intervention compatible with safety and the Greek legal framework.

I would begin with **immediate physical and environmental risk**. That includes nutrition and hydration, infection or untreated medical illness, falls, medication neglect, and hazards such as fire, gas, pests or unsafe living conditions. I would also consider whether dependants or other people are being placed at risk.

The next step is to identify **what is driving the self-neglect**. Important possibilities include dementia or other neurocognitive disorder, delirium, severe depression, psychosis, substance use, hoarding and frontal or executive dysfunction. “Diogenes syndrome” can be used descriptively, but it does not explain the aetiology and should not stop further assessment.

Collateral information is often essential because the patient’s account may not capture the degree of functional decline or environmental risk. Where feasible, direct assessment of the home with community or social services can clarify the actual level of squalor, hazards and support needs.

Refusal of help then has to be assessed for the **specific decision in question**. Decision-making ability is not an all-or-none global label. The clinician should document functionally whether the patient can make the concrete decision about treatment, admission, home support or another intervention. In Greece, the UK Mental Capacity Act four-part statutory test should not be presented as Greek law.

If the patient has decision-making capacity and the refusal is legally valid, a lifestyle that clinicians regard as dangerous, eccentric or unwise does not automatically authorise forced treatment. The appropriate response is continued engagement, harm reduction, practical support, repeated review and attempts to reduce risk without unnecessary coercion.

If decision-making ability is impaired, management should follow the applicable **Greek consent and emergency framework**, with family, legal, health and social mechanisms involved as appropriate. Emergency treatment without consent is a separate and narrow exception; it should not be used as a general solution to chronic self-neglect.

For psychiatric admission, the key legal point is that **severe self-neglect alone is insufficient**. A mental disorder must be present together with one of the statutory routes under Greek involuntary-admission law. There is also no single verified Greek statutory equivalent of the broad UK adult-safeguarding framework for a capacitous adult living in squalor, so practical management may require coordinated health, social-care, municipal and judicial involvement.

## Must-know board facts

- **Self-neglect alone does not justify involuntary psychiatric admission in Greece.**
- Valid refusal depends on the **specific decision** and the Greek consent framework, not on whether clinicians consider the choice wise.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating “Diogenes syndrome” as an aetiological diagnosis.
- Equating refusal of help with incapacity.
- Importing the UK Mental Capacity Act test into Greek law.
- Assuming there is a general Greek power to force home cleaning or social intervention.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Legal framing:** UK-derived capacity language can be clinically useful as a heuristic, but it is not Greek statutory law. A current Greek answer should use the Greek consent and involuntary-admission framework and should not treat severe self-neglect alone as sufficient for compulsory admission.
