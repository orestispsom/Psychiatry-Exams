# Q12. Describe the clinical picture and diagnosis of schizophrenia.

## Recall spine

1. **Syndrome by domains** — positive, negative, disorganised, cognitive.
2. **Typical clinical expression** — acute positive/disorganised prominence; persistent negative/cognitive disability.
3. **Longitudinal diagnosis** — pattern, course, duration and functioning.
4. **Exclude mimics** — mood, substance/medication, medical/neurological causes.
5. **Place the classics historically** — Schneiderian first-rank symptoms and classical subtypes are exam-relevant, not current diagnostic shortcuts.

## Model oral answer

Schizophrenia is a heterogeneous primary psychotic syndrome characterised by combinations of positive, negative, disorganised and cognitive abnormalities. The diagnosis is not made from any single psychotic symptom; it depends on the overall syndrome, its longitudinal course and functional impact, together with exclusion of affective, substance-related and medical or neurological causes. A first episode of psychosis therefore should not automatically be labelled schizophrenia.

Clinically, I would organise the picture into four main domains. Positive symptoms include delusions and hallucinations, with auditory hallucinations being a central descriptive feature. Classic examples include passivity and thought-interference phenomena such as thought insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting, but these are examples of psychopathology rather than diagnostic shortcuts. Disorganisation includes formal thought disorder or disorganised speech, disorganised or inappropriate behaviour, and at times affective incongruity.

The negative syndrome is often particularly important for long-term disability. Its principal domains are diminished emotional expression or blunting, alogia, avolition, anhedonia and asociality. I would be careful before calling these primary negative symptoms, because a similar picture of withdrawal, reduced activity or reduced expression can result from depression, medication effects, active psychosis or environmental deprivation.

Cognitive dysfunction is also common and clinically important, affecting areas such as attention, working memory, learning and memory, executive function and social cognition. It contributes substantially to functioning but is not itself a diagnostic criterion. Affective symptoms and psychomotor or catatonic phenomena may also occur and have to be interpreted within the whole clinical picture rather than treated as defining features in isolation.

The balance of symptoms can change across the illness. Acute presentations often foreground positive symptoms and disorganisation, whereas persistent disability may be dominated by negative and cognitive difficulties. I would use that distinction descriptively, not as a rigid staging or subtype system.

For diagnosis, the key is longitudinal assessment. I would establish the persistence and course of the syndrome, relevant change in functioning, and the required duration framework, while actively excluding alternative explanations. A cross-sectional psychotic presentation may ultimately fall into a different diagnostic category depending on its duration, evolution and cause, which is why premature diagnosis at first presentation is a mistake. In particular, prominent or sustained mood syndromes require consideration of an affective psychosis or schizoaffective presentation; substance or medication effects and medical or neurological causes must also be excluded. The precise duration and functional architecture differs between current diagnostic systems, so in an oral answer I would emphasise the longitudinal principle rather than recite both manuals.

Finally, I would place the classical material in context. Schneiderian first-rank symptoms remain important phenomenology to recognise for examinations, but they are neither pathognomonic nor given privileged status in current classification. Likewise, the classical paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic and residual subtype labels are historical; current classification describes schizophrenia dimensionally and by course rather than through those formal subtypes.

## Must-know board facts

- **Schneiderian first-rank phenomena:** know the classic groupings—audible or echoed thoughts; third-person voices commenting or discussing; thought insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting; somatic passivity; delusional perception; and made/passivity experiences involving affect, impulse or action. They are classic phenomenology, not pathognomonic signs.
- **DSM-5-TR duration anchor:** for private board recall, schizophrenia requires an overall disturbance lasting six months, including an active-phase component; shorter-duration psychotic syndromes fall into other categories.
- **Cognition:** broad cognitive impairment is clinically important and often present from the first episode. At group level, the average deficit is roughly one standard deviation, with substantial individual heterogeneity; it is not a diagnostic threshold.
- **Classification:** current DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 do not retain the classical paranoid/hebephrenic/catatonic/residual subtype scheme.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling Schneiderian first-rank symptoms pathognomonic.
- Diagnosing schizophrenia from a single cross-sectional psychotic episode.
- Presenting the classical subtypes as current formal diagnoses.
- Equating withdrawal or inactivity automatically with primary negative symptoms.
- Treating cognitive impairment as a diagnostic criterion.
- Turning Q12 into the full schizophrenia-versus-schizoaffective-versus-mood-psychosis differential.

## Exam answer vs current practice

- **ICD classification:** the Oxford 2017 exam source teaches ICD-10 criteria and classical subtypes. Current ICD-11 has superseded that framework and no longer uses the classical subtype scheme. In the viva, recognise the historical labels if asked, but organise the main answer dimensionally and longitudinally.
- **Schneiderian first-rank symptoms:** they remain important classic examination phenomenology, but current classification does not give them privileged diagnostic status and they should not be described as pathognomonic.
