# Q88. How do you recognise and manage tricyclic antidepressant overdose?

## Recall spine

1. **Anticholinergic and CNS toxicity**
2. **Fast sodium-channel blockade**
3. **ECG and QRS widening**
4. **Seizures, hypotension and ventricular dysrhythmia**
5. **IV sodium bicarbonate**
6. **Airway, circulation and acid–base care**
7. **Critical-care/toxicology management**

## Model oral answer

Tricyclic antidepressant overdose is a **medical toxicology emergency** because it combines anticholinergic and CNS toxicity with potentially lethal cardiovascular sodium-channel blockade. I would obtain an ECG early, look specifically for QRS widening, hypotension, seizures and ventricular dysrhythmia, and use **IV sodium bicarbonate** when significant cardiotoxicity is present. Management belongs in a monitored critical-care/toxicology framework rather than a psychiatric ward protocol.

The toxic syndrome includes anticholinergic features together with CNS depression or agitation and **seizures**. The most dangerous mechanism is blockade of fast cardiac sodium channels, which slows ventricular conduction and can produce hypotension and malignant dysrhythmias.

The **ECG is central** because it gives an immediate measure of conduction toxicity. A **QRS duration greater than 100 ms** remains a useful major-toxicity and seizure-risk anchor. The ECG must still be interpreted with the patient’s clinical condition rather than treated as the only determinant of severity.

**IV sodium bicarbonate is first-line treatment** for significant QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia or hypotension in TCA poisoning. The principle is sodium loading and correction of the cardiotoxic acid–base environment; the exact toxicology dosing strategy should follow the acute-care protocol rather than being improvised as one memorised bolus.

Supportive treatment runs in parallel. Airway and ventilation must be secured when needed, seizures treated, circulation supported and acid–base status monitored. Because deterioration may be rapid, significant poisoning requires close monitoring and early critical-care or toxicology involvement.

Some apparently intuitive treatments can be dangerous. **Class Ia and Ic sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmics should be avoided** because they may worsen the underlying conduction problem. **Physostigmine should also be avoided in acute cardiotoxic TCA poisoning.**

The viva answer should therefore stay focused: TCA overdose produces anticholinergic/CNS toxicity plus sodium-channel cardiotoxicity; ECG and QRS widening are key; significant conduction disturbance, dysrhythmia or hypotension is treated with IV sodium bicarbonate; and the patient requires aggressive supportive and monitored medical care.

## Must-know board facts

- **QRS >100 ms** is a useful major-toxicity/seizure-risk anchor.
- **IV sodium bicarbonate** is first-line for significant QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia or hypotension.
- Avoid **class Ia/Ic sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmics** and **physostigmine** in acute cardiotoxic TCA poisoning.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Reducing management to one memorised bicarbonate dose.
- Missing the ECG while focusing on anticholinergic symptoms.
- Using another sodium-channel-blocking antiarrhythmic for the ventricular conduction disturbance.
