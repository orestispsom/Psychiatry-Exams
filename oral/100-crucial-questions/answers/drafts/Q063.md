# Q63. How do you manage medically compromised anorexia nervosa?

## Recall spine

1. **Treat physiological instability as a medical emergency**
2. **Use multidomain MEED risk assessment**
3. **Check vitals, ECG, biochemistry and glucose**
4. **Escalate or admit when medically unsafe**
5. **Recognise refeeding risk**
6. **Give thiamine and correct electrolytes**
7. **Use monitored, risk-stratified refeeding**

## Model oral answer

Medically compromised anorexia nervosa is a **medical emergency when there is physiological instability or organ compromise**, and management requires close coordination between medical and psychiatric teams. Current risk assessment should be multidimensional rather than based on BMI alone. I would identify immediate instability, obtain urgent physiological and biochemical assessment, decide the appropriate level of medical care, and start monitored nutritional rehabilitation with active prevention and treatment of refeeding complications.

The current framework is **MEED**, which replaces the older MARSIPAN approach and integrates multiple domains of risk. I would assess the rate and extent of weight loss, recent intake and restriction, hydration, weakness and functional decline alongside pulse, blood pressure and postural change, temperature, glucose, ECG and relevant biochemistry.

Features that increase concern include marked bradycardia, hypotension or postural instability, hypothermia, dehydration, syncope, ECG abnormalities, electrolyte disturbance, hypoglycaemia, rapid weight loss, severe restriction, infection, weakness and evidence of organ dysfunction. Useful adult examples of a **red-risk range** include BMI below 13 kg/m², waking pulse below 40/min, temperature below 35.5°C and glucose below 3.0 mmol/L. These are memorable warning examples, not isolated admission rules; overall clinical risk determines management.

Urgent medical admission or escalation is required when the patient is physiologically unstable, seriously biochemically or ECG compromised, unable to maintain safe hydration or nutrition, at serious refeeding risk, or otherwise medically unsafe. A medically unstable patient should not remain in an inappropriate psychiatric setting simply because the underlying illness is an eating disorder.

A major treatment hazard is **refeeding syndrome**. Refeeding can precipitate shifts in phosphate, potassium and magnesium, fluid imbalance and thiamine deficiency, with possible arrhythmia, neurological deterioration and organ failure. High-risk patients therefore require thiamine and multivitamin before or with feeding, close monitoring of phosphate, potassium, magnesium, glucose and fluid status, and active correction of abnormalities.

The modern principle is **risk-stratified, actively monitored refeeding** rather than prolonged extreme underfeeding as a universal safety strategy. Very high-risk patients still need cautious specialist initiation, but inadequate feeding also carries risk and should not be defended simply because it is slow.

The practical answer is therefore: recognise medical instability early, use MEED-style multidomain assessment, escalate to the correct medical setting, prevent refeeding complications with thiamine and electrolyte monitoring, and restore nutrition under specialist monitoring.

## Must-know board facts

- **MEED replaces MARSIPAN** as the current risk framework.
- Useful adult red-risk examples: **BMI <13 kg/m², waking pulse <40/min, temperature <35.5°C, glucose <3.0 mmol/L**.
- Refeeding risk includes **phosphate, potassium, magnesium, fluid and thiamine** abnormalities.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Using BMI alone to decide medical safety.
- Applying one rigid QTc threshold as the whole risk assessment.
- Forgetting glucose, phosphate or thiamine.
- Keeping a medically unstable patient in a purely psychiatric setting.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older MARSIPAN and very slow universal feeding approaches have been superseded by **MEED multidomain risk assessment and monitored risk-stratified refeeding**.
