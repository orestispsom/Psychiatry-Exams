# Q13. How do you differentiate schizophrenia from schizoaffective disorder and mood disorder with psychotic features?

## Recall spine

1. **Map the longitudinal chronology** of psychosis and mood episodes.
2. **Ask whether a full schizophrenia syndrome exists.**
3. **Psychosis only during mood episodes?** — think mood disorder with psychotic features.
4. **Concurrent full mood and schizophrenia syndromes?** — consider schizoaffective.
5. **Check the classification system** — ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR use different architectures.
6. **Reassess over time** as the illness course becomes clearer.

## Model oral answer

The key distinction is **longitudinal**, not the content or severity of an individual psychotic symptom. I would map the timing of psychosis against depressive, manic or mixed episodes and ask whether there is a characteristic schizophrenia syndrome that is independent of mood episodes, whether psychosis occurs only within a mood episode, or whether full psychotic and mood syndromes occur together in a pattern that meets the chosen system’s definition of schizoaffective disorder.

In **schizophrenia**, mood symptoms can certainly occur, but mood episodes are not the organising feature of the illness. The clinical picture is primarily a schizophrenia syndrome, and the diagnosis rests on its characteristic longitudinal course rather than on the mere presence or absence of depressive symptoms at one assessment.

In a **mood disorder with psychotic features**, the psychosis occurs in the context of the mood episode. Outside the depressive or manic episode, the psychotic syndrome required for schizophrenia is not established. So the question is not whether the delusions are bizarre, persecutory or mood-congruent; it is whether psychosis has syndromic independence from the mood disorder.

**Schizoaffective disorder is the difficult middle category**, and this is where it is important not to blend diagnostic systems. In ICD-11 the formulation is episode-based: the patient must meet the full requirements for schizophrenia and also have a moderate or severe depressive, manic or mixed episode occurring concurrently for at least one month, with the two syndromes beginning at approximately the same time. ICD-11 does not require a period of psychosis in the absence of mood symptoms.

DSM-5-TR uses a different, lifetime or longitudinal architecture. It requires a period of at least **two weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode** at some point in the illness, while major mood episodes are present for the majority of the total duration of the illness. That familiar two-week rule is therefore DSM-specific; it should not be presented as a universal definition of schizoaffective disorder.

Duration and course must also be kept conceptually clean. ICD-11’s one-month concurrent-episode logic for schizoaffective disorder should not be mixed with the DSM framework for schizophrenia, including its six-month overall-disturbance requirement.

Early presentations may remain diagnostically uncertain because the necessary chronology has not yet unfolded. Repeated assessment of mood episodes, psychosis outside mood disturbance, overall course and functioning is often what resolves the diagnosis. The most defensible approach is to construct the timeline first, identify which syndrome is primary or independent, and then apply one classification system consistently.

## Must-know board facts

- **DSM-5-TR schizoaffective anchor:** at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode at some point, with major mood episodes occupying the majority of the total illness duration.
- **ICD-11 schizoaffective anchor:** full schizophrenia requirements plus a moderate/severe depressive, manic or mixed episode occurring concurrently for at least 1 month, with near-simultaneous onset; psychosis outside mood symptoms is not required.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Using psychotic content to separate the diagnoses.
- Calling any mood symptoms in schizophrenia “schizoaffective”.
- Applying the DSM two-week rule as though it were an ICD-11 requirement.
- Mixing ICD-11 and DSM duration rules within the same formulation.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification-system distinction:** The classic “two weeks of psychosis without mood symptoms” rule belongs to DSM-5-TR, not ICD-11. ICD-11 uses a concurrent episode-based definition. The same longitudinal history can therefore be classified differently depending on the system being applied; state the system and use it consistently.
