# Q41. What cannabis-related psychiatric syndromes do you need to recognise, and how do you manage cannabis use disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise intoxication and withdrawal**
2. **Identify cannabis-induced psychosis carefully**
3. **Distinguish substance-related from primary psychosis longitudinally**
4. **Frame cannabis as a modifiable psychosis-risk factor, not deterministic causation**
5. **Use psychosocial treatment for cannabis use disorder**
6. **Remember synthetic cannabinoids can be more severe**

## Model oral answer

Cannabis can produce **intoxication, withdrawal and substance-induced psychiatric syndromes**, including psychotic presentations, but the central diagnostic task is to establish the temporal relationship and avoid assuming that every psychosis in a cannabis user is cannabis-induced. Cannabis exposure is associated with increased psychosis risk and poorer course in established psychosis, particularly with frequent or high-potency use, but this should be presented as a modifiable risk association rather than deterministic causation.

During **intoxication**, patients may develop anxiety or panic, perceptual disturbance and, in some cases, psychotic symptoms. Cannabis withdrawal is also a recognised syndrome in current classification and should be considered when symptoms emerge after reduction or cessation in a dependent user.

For **cannabis-induced psychosis**, I would look for a plausible temporal relationship to intoxication or withdrawal and for symptoms that go beyond the ordinary transient effects expected during uncomplicated intoxication. The diagnosis becomes less convincing when psychosis clearly preceded cannabis use, when there is strong evidence of an independent psychotic disorder, or when the psychotic syndrome persists well beyond the expected substance-related period.

There is no single abstinence-duration cutoff that can prove whether the illness is cannabis-induced or primary. The distinction is longitudinal and depends on chronology, previous episodes, collateral history, persistence of symptoms and the wider schizophrenia-spectrum or mood syndrome. This is particularly important in a first psychotic episode, where premature causal attribution to cannabis can delay recognition of an evolving primary disorder.

At the same time, cannabis use is clinically important even when it is not the sole cause of psychosis. More frequent and higher-potency exposure is associated with greater psychosis risk, and continued use in people with established psychosis is associated with a poorer course and more relapse. I would therefore treat reduction or cessation as a meaningful modifiable target without telling the patient that cannabis inevitably causes schizophrenia.

Management of **cannabis use disorder** is mainly psychosocial. Brief intervention, motivational enhancement approaches, CBT and family-based approaches can all be used according to the patient’s needs and treatment setting. There is no routinely licensed pharmacotherapy that should be presented as standard treatment for cannabis use disorder.

I would also ask specifically about **synthetic cannabinoids**, because they belong to a separate current substance category and may produce more severe and unpredictable agitation, psychosis and medical toxicity than conventional cannabis.

The practical answer is therefore to recognise intoxication and withdrawal, diagnose cannabis-induced psychosis only when chronology supports it, keep primary psychosis in the differential, frame cannabis as a modifiable risk factor rather than a deterministic cause, and manage the use disorder primarily with psychosocial treatment.

## Must-know board facts

- **Synthetic-cannabinoid disorders are separately recognised in ICD-11.**
- No single duration of abstinence proves cannabis causation versus primary psychosis.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Saying cannabis deterministically “causes schizophrenia.”
- Assuming persistent psychosis after cannabis use is automatically substance-induced.
- Ignoring ongoing cannabis use as a modifiable factor in relapse and course once a primary psychotic disorder is diagnosed.
