# Q51. How do you assess and manage behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)?

## Recall spine

1. **Define the behaviour and context**
2. **Search for reversible drivers first**
3. **Assess risk and carer burden**
4. **Use person-centred non-drug measures first**
5. **Treat specific syndromes rather than sedating globally**
6. **Reserve antipsychotics for severe distress or harm risk**
7. **Review regularly and deprescribe when possible**

## Model oral answer

Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia are common but should never be treated as a single indication for sedation. The first task is to **define the behaviour, identify reversible or environmental drivers, assess risk and carer burden, and use person-centred non-pharmacological measures first**. Antipsychotics are reserved for severe distress or significant risk of harm when the expected benefit outweighs cerebrovascular and mortality risks.

BPSD can include **agitation or aggression, psychosis, depression or anxiety, apathy, sleep disturbance, wandering and disinhibition**. The description needs to be specific: what exactly is happening, when, in which environment, with whom, and what precedes or reduces it. This often reveals a treatable trigger rather than a primary behavioural syndrome.

Before attributing symptoms to dementia itself, I would actively look for **delirium, pain, infection, constipation, urinary retention, medication adverse effects, sensory impairment, sleep disruption, environmental stress and unmet needs**. A patient who suddenly becomes aggressive because of pain or delirium requires treatment of the cause, not simply a psychotropic prescription.

Risk assessment should include harm to the patient, carers or others, wandering or unsafe behaviour, and the effect of sustained symptoms on carer capacity. Management should also consider the patient’s usual routines, communication ability and previous sources of comfort or meaningful activity.

The first-line approach is **person-centred and non-pharmacological**. I would modify the environment where possible, reduce avoidable triggers, preserve familiar routines, provide meaningful activity, improve communication, correct sensory problems, optimise sleep and comfort, and support carers. Specific comorbid syndromes such as depression, anxiety, pain or sleep problems should be treated in their own right rather than using one sedating drug for every behaviour.

**Antipsychotics are not routine BPSD treatment.** They should be considered only when there is severe distress or a meaningful risk of harm and when non-drug measures have failed or are insufficient. The cerebrovascular and mortality risks should be discussed qualitatively. If treatment is used, the principle is the **lowest effective dose for the shortest possible exposure**, with regular review and attempts to reduce or stop when clinically appropriate.

A useful specific licensing fact is **risperidone**, which has a short-term indication of up to **6 weeks** for persistent aggression in moderate to severe Alzheimer dementia after non-pharmacological approaches have failed. This should not be converted into a universal six-week or 6–12-week duration rule for every antipsychotic or every dementia-related indication.

The overall strategy is therefore cause-finding first, person-centred management second, targeted treatment of specific syndromes, and tightly restricted antipsychotic use only when severity or risk justifies it, followed by active review and deprescribing.

## Must-know board facts

- Antipsychotics in dementia increase **cerebrovascular and mortality risk**; avoid memorising outdated fixed risk ratios.
- **Risperidone:** licensed short-term use up to **6 weeks** for persistent aggression in moderate–severe Alzheimer dementia after non-drug measures fail.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating agitation before checking pain, delirium, infection, constipation or urinary retention.
- Using antipsychotics for convenience or routine wandering.
- Continuing antipsychotics indefinitely because they once appeared helpful.
- Applying the risperidone 6-week licence as though it were a universal antipsychotic-duration rule.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Antipsychotic duration:** Older teaching may quote broad 6–12-week courses. Current practice is **shortest possible exposure with regular review**, while the up-to-6-week rule is a specific risperidone licensing point for persistent aggression in moderate–severe Alzheimer dementia.
