# Q29. How do you diagnose and manage generalized anxiety disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Persistent, excessive worry across domains**
2. **Difficulty controlling worry with tension/autonomic symptoms**
3. **Establish several-month course and impairment**
4. **Exclude neighbouring psychiatric and secondary causes**
5. **CBT as core treatment**
6. **SSRI first-line; SNRI alternative**
7. **Pregabalin mainly when SSRI/SNRI are not tolerated**

## Model oral answer

Generalized anxiety disorder is a syndrome of **persistent, excessive and difficult-to-control worry across multiple areas of life**, associated with clinically significant distress or impairment. It is broader and more sustained than discrete panic attacks or situation-bound phobic anxiety. Diagnosis therefore depends on the pervasive pattern, its duration and functional effect, not simply on the patient describing themselves as anxious.

The worry is typically present on more days than not and may concern health, family, work, finances or other everyday domains. Associated symptoms can include restlessness or tension, autonomic anxiety, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability and sleep disturbance. The central clinical feature is the combination of generalized apprehensiveness and inability to disengage from worry rather than one specific feared object or situation.

I would establish the **course** carefully. Under current ICD-11 the syndrome should persist for at least **several months**. DSM retains a **6-month** duration requirement, but that figure should not be presented as a universal current rule across classification systems.

The differential is important because chronic worry occurs in many conditions. Depressive rumination is usually organised around depressive themes and the depressive syndrome; panic disorder centres on recurrent panic and fear of further attacks; OCD involves intrusive obsessional phenomena and compulsions; health anxiety is dominated by illness preoccupation. Substance or medication effects and relevant medical causes should also be considered when the presentation or chronology suggests them.

Management should be collaborative and matched to severity, impairment, preference and service context. **CBT** is a core evidence-based treatment. Applied relaxation and lower-intensity CBT-based interventions may also be useful depending on severity and how care is organised. Psychological treatment should not be presented merely as an adjunct to medication.

If pharmacotherapy is used, an **SSRI** is the principal first-line option. If the first SSRI is ineffective or not tolerated, another SSRI or an **SNRI** is a standard next strategy. **Pregabalin** is better framed as a guideline-supported alternative when SSRIs or SNRIs cannot be tolerated rather than as a routine co-equal first-line drug. Benzodiazepines should be restricted to short-term crisis use rather than becoming ongoing treatment for the disorder.

The overall approach is therefore to identify the chronic pervasive-worry syndrome, distinguish it from episodic or condition-specific anxiety and secondary causes, and use CBT and/or serotonergic pharmacotherapy as the main long-term treatments while avoiding routine benzodiazepine dependence.

## Must-know board facts

- **DSM:** 6-month duration requirement.
- **ICD-11:** symptoms for at least several months, on more days than not.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Teaching the DSM 6-month rule as universal.
- Equating GAD with an “anxious personality.”
- Missing depression, OCD, medical or substance-related causes.
- Presenting pregabalin or benzodiazepines as routine first-line equivalents to CBT/SSRIs.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Duration:** DSM uses 6 months; current ICD-11 uses at least several months. State the system rather than blending the two.
