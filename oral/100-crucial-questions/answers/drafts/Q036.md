# Q36. How do you diagnose and manage functional neurological disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Identify the functional neurological phenotype**
2. **Make a positive diagnosis from functional signs**
3. **Assess neurological differential and possible comorbidity**
4. **Do not require trauma or psychological stress**
5. **Explain the diagnosis clearly and non-dualistically**
6. **Use phenotype-specific rehabilitation**
7. **Share care across neurology and psychiatry/psychology**

## Model oral answer

Functional neurological disorder is a **positive, rule-in diagnosis**, not simply the conclusion reached when tests are normal. I would identify the neurological phenotype, look for positive clinical features showing internal inconsistency or incongruity with recognised neurological disease, assess appropriate neurological differentials and comorbidity, and then explain the diagnosis in a way that validates the symptoms and leads directly into treatment. Psychological trauma or stress is not required for diagnosis.

FND can present with several phenotypes, including **functional weakness or gait disorder, functional movement symptoms, sensory symptoms, speech or communication symptoms and functional seizures**. The diagnostic examination should therefore be phenotype-specific rather than based on one generic sign.

In functional weakness, examples of useful positive signs include the **Hoover sign or hip-abductor sign**. In functional tremor, **distractibility, entrainment and variability** can support the diagnosis. These signs should be interpreted in the correct clinical context; no single manoeuvre should be applied mechanically or treated as proof in every patient.

**Functional seizures** require careful clinical semiology. When uncertainty remains, video-EEG recording of typical events is useful where feasible. Coexisting epilepsy must remain in the differential, so a functional diagnosis should not be used to dismiss the possibility of another neurological disorder.

A major modern principle is that neither **trauma nor an identifiable psychological stressor is required**. Psychological factors may contribute in some patients and may become relevant to formulation or treatment, but they should not be forced into every case. Equally, normal imaging or other investigations are not themselves positive evidence of FND.

Treatment begins with a **clear positive diagnostic explanation**. The patient should be told that the symptoms are real and involuntary, that the diagnosis is based on recognisable functional signs, and that altered nervous-system function or control can occur despite preserved capacity for normal movement or function. Where possible, demonstrating positive signs can help show that the problem is potentially reversible rather than evidence of irreversible structural damage.

Management should then be tailored to the phenotype. Motor symptoms often benefit from **FND-informed physiotherapy and rehabilitation**, with retraining of automatic movement, redirection of excessive self-focused attention, demonstration of preserved normal movement and reduction of maladaptive avoidance or movement patterns. Occupational or speech therapy may be appropriate depending on the symptoms. Psychological treatment is used when indicated for the functional symptoms or for comorbid psychiatric problems; it is not a prerequisite for accepting the diagnosis.

Good care is multidisciplinary. **Neurology and psychiatry or psychology should share treatment ownership** rather than passing the patient between specialties. The goal is a coherent biopsychosocial, non-dualistic formulation that combines positive diagnosis, rehabilitation and treatment of relevant comorbidity.

## Must-know board facts

- FND is **involuntary** and is diagnosed using **positive clinical features**.
- Psychological stress or trauma is **not required** for diagnosis.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q36a. How do you explain the diagnosis to the patient?

Name the diagnosis directly and state that the symptoms are **real, involuntary and potentially disabling**. Explain why the diagnosis was made using positive functional signs where possible rather than saying “nothing is wrong” or relying on normal scans.

A useful explanation is that the nervous system’s **function or control is altered**, while some normal capacity remains preserved; positive signs can help demonstrate that improvement and retraining are possible. Avoid implying that the symptoms are “all psychological,” caused by trauma, imagined or under voluntary control unless a particular psychological factor is actually supported by the individual formulation.

Use a non-dualistic biopsychosocial explanation and connect the diagnosis immediately to treatment and rehabilitation. A “software versus hardware” analogy can be used if it helps the individual patient, but it is optional and can oversimplify.

### Q36b. How do you distinguish factitious disorder, malingering and FND?

In **FND**, the symptoms are not intentionally produced. Positive functional signs demonstrate altered function; they do not demonstrate deception.

In **factitious disorder**, there is intentional falsification or induction of symptoms with deception while presenting oneself or another person as ill, without an obvious external reward. The traditional shorthand of seeking the “sick role” can be useful, but a specific psychodynamic motive does not have to be proven.

In **malingering**, symptoms are intentionally produced or exaggerated for an identifiable external incentive, and malingering is not itself a psychiatric disorder.

The crucial safeguard is that **intent and motive cannot reliably be inferred from inconsistency, unusual symptoms, secondary gain or clinician suspicion alone**. Deliberate deception requires evidence; FND should never be equated with factitious disorder or malingering.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- “All tests are normal, therefore this is FND.”
- Equating FND with malingering.
- Forcing trauma or psychological stress into every case.
- Passing the patient between neurology and psychiatry without active treatment ownership.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification:** ICD-11 places the corresponding condition under dissociative neurological symptom disorder, while DSM-5-TR places FND under Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders. The clinical diagnosis remains a positive functional diagnosis rather than one based on classification placement or negative investigations.
