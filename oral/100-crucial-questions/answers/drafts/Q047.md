# Q47. How do you diagnose and manage Alzheimer disease?

## Recall spine

1. **Insidious progressive amnestic syndrome**
2. **Establish functional decline and exclude mimics**
3. **Use biomarkers when diagnostic certainty or treatment selection requires them**
4. **AChE inhibitor ± memantine for symptomatic treatment**
5. **Support function, safety and carers**
6. **Anti-amyloid therapy only in selected early biomarker-confirmed disease**
7. **ARIA and MRI monitoring are central safety issues**

## Model oral answer

Alzheimer disease is typically an **insidious, progressive neurocognitive syndrome**, often beginning with impaired new learning and episodic memory and later involving other cortical functions. Diagnosis remains clinical and functional, supported by collateral history, examination, imaging and biomarkers when needed. Management combines symptomatic cognitive treatment, safety and carer support, with anti-amyloid therapy now available only for carefully selected early disease.

The typical presentation is gradual decline in recent memory and acquisition of new information. As the disease progresses, language, praxis, recognition, visuospatial function and executive abilities may also deteriorate. Functional decline is essential to dementia-level diagnosis, so I would establish how cognition is affecting medication management, finances, domestic tasks and other activities of daily living rather than relying on cognitive testing alone.

The differential includes **dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, vascular cognitive impairment, depression and delirium**. Delirium is suggested by acute fluctuation and inattention rather than a steady progressive course. The aetiological assessment should therefore integrate the pattern of decline, neurological examination and structural imaging.

Classically, Alzheimer pathology is characterised by **amyloid plaques and tau pathology**. Biomarkers can increase diagnostic certainty when the phenotype is atypical or uncertain and are particularly important when anti-amyloid treatment is being considered. Biomarker positivity does not replace the clinical diagnosis or functional assessment.

For symptomatic treatment, **donepezil, rivastigmine or galantamine** are established options for mild to moderate Alzheimer disease. **Memantine** can be used in moderate disease when acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are contraindicated or not tolerated and in severe disease; in moderate or severe disease it can also be combined with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor according to current guidance. Treatment should also address daily function, routines, comorbidity, safety, driving or financial concerns where relevant, and carer education and support.

A major current development is the arrival of **lecanemab and donanemab**. In the EU they are authorised only for carefully selected patients with early symptomatic Alzheimer disease—mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer disease—with confirmed amyloid pathology, and the current EU indication excludes APOE ε4 homozygotes. These treatments **slow decline modestly**; they do not reverse established dementia or restore lost function.

Their use is specialist and safety-intensive. **ARIA-E and ARIA-H** are central adverse effects and MRI monitoring is required. The existence of an EU authorisation should not be translated into an assumption of routine access, reimbursement or availability in Greece.

The practical answer is therefore to recognise the characteristic progressive syndrome, establish functional loss and exclude competing causes, use biomarkers selectively, provide established symptomatic treatment and supportive care, and reserve anti-amyloid therapy for appropriately selected early biomarker-confirmed disease under specialist monitoring.

## Must-know board facts

- **Lecanemab and donanemab** have EU authorisation for selected early Alzheimer disease.
- Current EU use requires **confirmed amyloid pathology** and excludes **APOE ε4 homozygotes**.
- **ARIA** is the defining safety issue and requires MRI monitoring.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Saying there is no disease-modifying treatment at all.
- Presenting anti-amyloid therapy as routine for all Alzheimer disease or moderate/severe dementia.
- Equating EU authorisation with routine Greek availability or reimbursement.
- Treating a positive biomarker as the whole diagnosis.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Disease modification:** Older teaching that Alzheimer disease has no disease-modifying treatment is no longer literally current. Anti-amyloid therapy is now available for a **restricted early-disease population**, with modest disease-slowing benefit and substantial specialist safety requirements; it is not curative.
