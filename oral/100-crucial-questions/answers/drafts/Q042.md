# Q42. How do you recognise and manage stimulant-related disorders?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise the sympathomimetic toxidrome**
2. **Look immediately for cardiovascular, neurological and hyperthermic emergencies**
3. **Use benzodiazepines and supportive care acutely**
4. **Treat psychosis without missing medical toxicity**
5. **Recognise the withdrawal crash and assess suicide risk**
6. **Use contingency management for longer-term treatment**

## Model oral answer

Stimulant intoxication is a **sympathomimetic emergency until proven otherwise**. Cocaine and amphetamine-type stimulants can produce agitation, autonomic activation, paranoia or psychosis, hyperthermia, seizures and major cardiovascular or cerebrovascular complications, so the first task is simultaneous psychiatric and medical assessment rather than simply sedating an agitated patient.

Typical intoxication may include marked agitation, anxiety, tachycardic or hypertensive autonomic activation and suspiciousness or frank psychosis. I would actively look for red flags that change urgency: **chest pain or myocardial ischaemia, severe hypertension, arrhythmia, hyperthermia, seizure, focal neurological signs, altered consciousness, rhabdomyolysis or renal injury, and severe psychosis or suicidality**.

For acute agitation and the hyperadrenergic state, **benzodiazepines are the core first-line treatment**, combined with environmental containment and supportive medical care. Hyperthermia requires active cooling; hydration and other supportive measures are guided by the clinical state, while specific complications such as cardiac ischaemia, seizures or neurological events require targeted emergency management.

A useful current correction concerns **beta-blockers after cocaine or stimulant exposure**. The old rule that all beta-blockers are absolutely contraindicated is too rigid. Benzodiazepines and vasodilator strategies remain preferred during acute intoxication, but if beta blockade is clinically required, combined **alpha/beta agents such as labetalol or carvedilol are preferred** rather than teaching a blanket prohibition.

Psychotic symptoms should be assessed in parallel with the medical syndrome. The presence of paranoia or hallucinations does not remove the need to consider hyperthermia, myocardial infarction, stroke or seizures. The temporal relationship to stimulant use, persistence after intoxication resolves and the patient’s wider psychiatric history help determine whether the psychosis is substance-induced or reflects an independent disorder.

After heavy stimulant use, patients may enter a **withdrawal or crash phase** with fatigue, altered sleep, dysphoria or depression, anhedonia and craving. Suicide risk needs specific assessment during this period because marked depressive symptoms can become clinically important even when the acute intoxication has ended.

Longer-term treatment is principally behavioural. **Contingency management has the strongest current psychosocial treatment signal for stimulant-use disorder**, and CBT or other structured behavioural approaches can be added according to need. There is no single universally approved pharmacotherapy that should be presented as the standard medication treatment.

The practical sequence is therefore: recognise the sympathomimetic toxidrome, identify medical emergencies early, use benzodiazepines and supportive care, assess psychosis and suicidality without losing sight of medical toxicity, then address the withdrawal crash and engage the patient in contingency-management-based treatment.

## Must-know board facts

- The blanket rule **“never use any beta-blocker after cocaine” is outdated**; if beta blockade is required during intoxication, combined alpha/beta agents are preferred.
- **Contingency management** is the strongest current psychosocial treatment signal for stimulant-use disorder.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating psychosis or agitation while missing hyperthermia, myocardial ischaemia, stroke or seizure risk.
- Repeating the old absolute beta-blocker contraindication.
- Forgetting suicide risk during the stimulant crash.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Beta-blockers:** Older teaching often stated a blanket prohibition after cocaine use. Current practice is more conditional: benzodiazepines and vasodilators remain preferred acutely, but combined alpha/beta blockade may be used when clinically necessary.
