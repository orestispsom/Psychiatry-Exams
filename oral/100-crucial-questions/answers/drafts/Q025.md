# Q25. How do you treat bipolar depression?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm bipolar depression and assess mixed/manic risk**
2. **Quetiapine, lithium and lamotrigine as core options**
3. **Use selected alternatives according to context**
4. **Antidepressants only cautiously and adjunctively**
5. **No antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I**
6. **ECT for urgent or refractory illness**
7. **Separate international evidence from EU licensing**

## Model oral answer

Bipolar depression should not be treated as ordinary unipolar depression. I would first confirm the bipolar diagnosis and subtype, assess for current mixed or manic symptoms, suicide risk, psychosis or catatonia, review adherence and substance use, and consider previous treatment response and the patient’s longer-term maintenance needs.

For a European-oriented approach, the high-yield core options are **quetiapine, lithium and lamotrigine**. The choice depends on bipolar subtype, previous response, predominant polarity, current severity, tolerability and the maintenance strategy that is likely to follow. Lamotrigine is particularly relevant to depressive polarity and prevention of depressive relapse, but it is not an acute antimanic treatment.

An additional recognised option for moderate-to-severe bipolar depression is **olanzapine combined with fluoxetine**. This does not mean that every patient requires the same fixed sequence; the treatment should be matched to the individual clinical picture rather than reduced to a memorised universal hierarchy.

The main caution is the role of **antidepressants**. They are not the backbone of treatment for bipolar-I depression, and **antidepressant monotherapy should not be recommended in bipolar I**. When an antidepressant is used, it should be reserved for selected patients and used adjunctively with an established mood-stabilising treatment. I would be particularly cautious or avoid antidepressants in patients with previous antidepressant-induced mania, current mixed features or recent rapid cycling. Patients should be educated about warning signs of mood elevation, and the antidepressant should be stopped if mania emerges.

**ECT** remains an important option for refractory bipolar depression and should be considered earlier when a rapid response is necessary because of severe suicidality, psychosis, catatonia or medical compromise from poor intake. Psychological intervention, attention to sleep and circadian regularity, substance management and relapse planning are useful adjuncts, but they do not replace an appropriate acute biological treatment when the episode is severe.

There is also an important distinction between **international treatment evidence and European licensing**. International guidelines include cariprazine and lurasidone-based strategies among first-line options for bipolar-I depression. However, current European product information for **cariprazine and lurasidone remains schizophrenia-only**. They may therefore be recognised as treatments with international bipolar-depression evidence, but they should not be presented as routine EU-licensed bipolar-depression treatments.

The practical answer is therefore to confirm the polarity and mixed-state risk, choose an established bipolar-depression treatment such as quetiapine, lithium or lamotrigine according to the patient’s history and maintenance needs, use antidepressants only selectively and never as monotherapy in bipolar I, and escalate to ECT when severity or refractoriness requires it.

## Must-know board facts

- **Bipolar-I depression:** do not recommend antidepressant monotherapy.
- **Cariprazine and lurasidone:** have international evidence for bipolar depression but are **not currently EU-licensed for bipolar depression**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating bipolar depression exactly like major depressive disorder.
- Using antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I.
- Presenting cariprazine or lurasidone as EU-approved bipolar-depression treatments.
- Forgetting mixed features and switch risk.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Evidence versus licensing:** International bipolar-depression recommendations and European product licensing are not identical. Do not convert international evidence for cariprazine or lurasidone into a claim that they are routine EU-licensed treatments for bipolar depression.
