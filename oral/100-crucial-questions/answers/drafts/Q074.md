# Q74. What core pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic principles matter in psychopharmacology?

## Recall spine

1. **Absorption and bioavailability**
2. **Distribution, protein binding and volume of distribution**
3. **Metabolism: Phase I/II and CYP interactions**
4. **Renal elimination, half-life and steady state**
5. **Agonism, antagonism and partial agonism**
6. **Dose–response and therapeutic index**
7. **Patient and drug modifiers**

## Model oral answer

The practical purpose of pharmacokinetics is to understand **what the body does to the drug**, while pharmacodynamics describes **what the drug does to the body**. In psychopharmacology, the clinically useful framework is absorption, distribution, metabolism and elimination, then linking concentration to receptor action, dose–response and safety. These principles explain why the same dose can behave very differently between patients or after an interaction.

**Absorption** determines how much and how quickly a drug enters the circulation, while bioavailability describes the proportion that reaches the systemic circulation. Once absorbed, distribution depends partly on tissue characteristics, protein binding and the apparent volume of distribution. Changes in protein binding or distribution can alter the relationship between total drug concentration and pharmacologically active exposure.

Most psychotropics undergo hepatic metabolism. **Phase I reactions** commonly involve oxidation and CYP enzymes, while Phase II reactions involve conjugation. This is clinically important because enzyme inhibition can raise exposure to a susceptible drug, whereas enzyme induction can lower it. Inhibition usually appears more rapidly than induction or de-induction, so interaction timing matters as well as the direction of the effect.

A high-yield example is smoking. **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke—not nicotine—induce CYP1A2**, which can lower exposure to drugs such as clozapine and olanzapine. Stopping smoking can therefore increase exposure even if nicotine replacement is used. Other useful interaction anchors are fluoxetine and paroxetine as clinically important CYP2D6 inhibitors and carbamazepine as a classic enzyme inducer.

Elimination may be hepatic, renal or both. **Half-life** helps predict how quickly concentrations fall after stopping or changing a dose and how long accumulation takes. Steady state is reached after several elimination half-lives, commonly around **4–5 half-lives**. Renal or hepatic impairment, age and interacting medicines may therefore change exposure and require individual clinical adjustment rather than one universal dose rule.

Pharmacodynamically, drugs may act as **agonists, antagonists or partial agonists** at receptors. Dose–response relationships describe how increasing exposure changes effect, while the therapeutic index expresses the separation between effective and toxic exposure and therefore contributes to how closely a treatment must be monitored.

The key viva point is to connect these principles to prescribing: consider the patient’s age and organ function, smoking, interacting drugs, the drug’s half-life and clearance, and its pharmacodynamic safety margin before interpreting response or toxicity.

## Must-know board facts

- Steady state is commonly reached after about **4–5 elimination half-lives**.
- **Tobacco-smoke PAHs, not nicotine, induce CYP1A2** and can lower clozapine/olanzapine exposure.
- Fluoxetine and paroxetine are clinically important **CYP2D6 inhibitors**.
- Carbamazepine is a classic **enzyme inducer**.
- CYP inhibition usually develops faster than induction or de-induction.
- Routine commercial pharmacogenomic panels are **not a universal prerequisite** for prescribing.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Building an exhaustive CYP catalogue instead of explaining clinically relevant principles.
- Assuming nicotine itself produces the smoking-related CYP1A2 interaction.
- Applying one universal dose adjustment to every patient with renal or hepatic impairment.
