# Q22. What do you do when depression is treatment-resistant?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm at least 2 adequate antidepressant failures**
2. **Exclude pseudoresistance**
3. **Reassess diagnosis, severity and risk**
4. **Switch after no response; augment after partial response**
5. **Antipsychotic or lithium augmentation**
6. **ECT and rTMS**
7. **Esketamine as a specialist EU-authorised option**
8. **Manage difficult-to-treat depression longitudinally**

## Model oral answer

I would first confirm that this is genuine treatment-resistant depression rather than apparent resistance. A practical definition is persistent clinically significant depression despite **at least two adequate antidepressant treatments in the current episode**. Before escalating, I would systematically look for pseudoresistance and reconsider the overall formulation.

The first question is whether the diagnosis is correct, especially whether an unrecognised **bipolar disorder** is present. I would then check whether previous treatments were genuinely adequate in dose and duration, whether they were taken consistently, whether adverse effects prevented effective exposure, and whether the treatment was actually completed or delivered competently. Substance use, psychiatric comorbidity, relevant medical illness or medication, and major psychosocial adversity can all maintain symptoms and need to be addressed rather than simply adding another drug.

I would also reassess the current syndrome itself: which symptoms remain, how severe the impairment is, and whether there is active suicide risk, psychosis or catatonia that changes urgency and treatment choice.

The next decision is **switch versus augmentation**. If an antidepressant has produced essentially no benefit or is poorly tolerated, switching is usually more logical. If there has been meaningful partial benefit and the treatment is well tolerated, augmentation or addition is more attractive. Among established augmentation strategies, atypical-antipsychotic augmentation has strong evidence, with aripiprazole as a representative example, and **lithium augmentation** remains an important established option. There is no need to pretend that every guideline uses one identical ranking.

For severe, urgent or highly refractory illness, **ECT** remains the most important somatic treatment, particularly when depression is accompanied by psychosis, catatonia, severe suicidality or life-threatening refusal of food or fluids. **rTMS** is also an established non-invasive treatment for depression that has not responded adequately to standard treatment; it should not be described as experimental fringe therapy.

**Intranasal esketamine** is an EU-authorised specialist option for adults with TRD after at least two antidepressant failures, used with an SSRI or SNRI under supervised administration and monitoring. Its regulatory authorisation does not mean that it is uniformly recommended, available or reimbursed in every health system, and Greek access or reimbursement should not be assumed. IV racemic ketamine is a separate issue and is not an EU-authorised antidepressant indication.

Finally, I would avoid making the management of a chronically difficult illness into serial medication changes alone. The broader concept of **difficult-to-treat depression** is useful when there is persistent burden despite several reasonable treatments, because it focuses attention on partial benefit, chronicity, function, relapse prevention, comorbidity and the patient’s own goals rather than a simple resistant/not-resistant label.

## Must-know board facts

- **Conventional TRD anchor:** failure of at least **2 adequate antidepressant treatments in the current episode**.
- **rTMS:** an established treatment option for depression after inadequate response to standard treatment.
- **Esketamine:** EU authorised for TRD, but authorisation does not establish universal guideline recommendation, availability or reimbursement.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling inadequate treatment or non-adherence TRD.
- Missing bipolar disorder.
- Reflexively adding medication after zero response without reconsidering the strategy.
- Listing every emerging intervention instead of giving a treatment sequence.
- Treating EU licensing as proof of routine Greek availability or reimbursement.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Treatment-resistant depression:** Classic answers often stop at switching, lithium or antipsychotic augmentation and ECT. Current practice also recognises rTMS as established treatment and intranasal esketamine as an EU-authorised specialist option, while the broader DTD concept can be more useful for long-term management than a binary resistance label.
