# Q98. What are the psychiatrist’s duties of confidentiality in Greece?

## Recall spine

1. **Law 3418/2005, Article 13**
2. **Strict professional confidentiality**
3. **Consent or defensible legal exception for disclosure**
4. **Relatives have no automatic access**
5. **Need-to-know sharing within treatment**
6. **Necessity, proportionality and data minimisation**
7. **Document the reasoning**

## Model oral answer

In Greece, the starting point is **Law 3418/2005, Article 13 of the Code of Medical Ethics**: the psychiatrist has a strict duty of confidentiality for information learned in professional practice about the patient and close persons, whether the information came from the patient or from third parties. The duty is continuing; it does not simply end when treatment ends, the doctor stops practising, or the patient dies.

The psychiatrist must also protect confidentiality operationally. That includes supervising collaborators and assistants and ensuring that information is handled securely. Psychiatric information should therefore be accessed and shared according to clinical purpose, not curiosity or convenience.

The clearest basis for disclosure is **valid patient consent**. Without consent, disclosure needs another defensible legal basis. Article 13 allows exceptions where disclosure is required to fulfil a legal duty or where it is necessary to protect a justified substantial interest that cannot otherwise be protected, including circumstances falling within necessity or defence principles. Because professional secrecy also has criminal-law protection under **Penal Code Article 371**, disclosure should never be treated casually: the clinician should be able to explain the legal and clinical basis for it.

A common practical error concerns relatives. **Family members do not automatically have a right to confidential psychiatric information about a competent living adult.** Their concern may be clinically valuable and they may provide information to the team, but receiving information from relatives is different from having permission to disclose the patient’s information back to them.

Within the legitimate treating system, necessary information can be shared on a **need-to-know and purpose-limited basis** for the patient’s care. That does not mean that every professional or service automatically needs the full psychiatric record. The same principle applies when disclosure outside the immediate treating team is justified: disclose only what is necessary, to the appropriate recipient, for the protective or legal purpose.

Health information is also **special-category personal data**. GDPR and Law 4624/2019 govern lawful processing and security, but they do not replace the substantive medical duty of confidentiality. Data-protection compliance and medical confidentiality operate together.

So when considering disclosure without consent, I would ask: what is the risk or legal duty, can the aim be achieved without disclosure, who actually needs the information, and what is the minimum necessary information to disclose? I would document that reasoning carefully.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q98a. What if a patient makes a credible imminent threat of violence against an identifiable person?

First I would assess the threat clinically: **target, specificity, intent, planning, access to means, imminence, previous violence, intoxication or psychosis, escalation and protective factors**. I would simultaneously reduce opportunity where possible, intensify treatment and support, and document the risk formulation and actions taken.

Greek law should not be taught as containing a general **Tarasoff-style duty to warn**. Article 13 allows proportionate protective disclosure where the confidentiality exceptions and necessity reasoning are met. In addition, **Penal Code Article 232** creates a specific duty to report to the authorities when one reliably learns of a planned or ongoing felony while it can still be prevented, subject to the statutory conditions.

The exact circumstances requiring a **direct warning to the threatened person**, rather than reporting to authorities or another protective disclosure, are not established as one universal rule. The defensible answer is therefore risk-based and legally grounded: act proportionately according to seriousness and imminence, use the applicable Article 13/necessity and Article 232 framework, disclose only what is needed to the appropriate recipient, and document the reasoning.

### Q98b. How should psychiatrists handle social media?

Confidentiality applies **online exactly as offline**. Identifiable patient material should not be posted without an appropriate lawful basis and consent. Professional boundaries should also be protected: private interactions, dual-role dynamics and patient friend or follow requests should generally be handled through a clear professional frame.

Routine curiosity-driven online searching of patients is not good clinical practice. Separating professional and personal accounts can reduce boundary problems, but it does not remove confidentiality obligations. There is no need to invent a special Greek social-media statute to justify these ordinary confidentiality and boundary principles.

## Exam answer vs current practice

A generic common-law or UK confidentiality answer is insufficient for Greek practice. The oral answer should begin with **Law 3418/2005 Article 13**, then explain consent, lawful exceptions, proportional disclosure, Article 371 professional secrecy and—where relevant to threatened violence—Article 232 reporting obligations.
