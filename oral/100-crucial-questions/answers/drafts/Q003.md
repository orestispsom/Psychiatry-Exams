# Q03. When psychiatric symptoms appear for the first time, when should you suspect a medical, neurological, medication-related or substance-related cause?

## Recall spine

1. **Acute, atypical or unusual onset**
2. **Attention, consciousness and cognition**
3. **Neurological and systemic red flags**
4. **Medication, intoxication and withdrawal**
5. **Physical and neurological examination**
6. **Targeted investigations**
7. **Test the causal chronology**

## Model oral answer

A first psychiatric presentation should never be assumed to be primary psychiatric simply because the symptoms look psychiatric. Suspicion for a secondary cause rises when the onset, course or associated findings are atypical—particularly acute or subacute onset, fluctuation, altered attention or consciousness, cognitive change, neurological signs, seizures, autonomic instability, abnormal vital signs, systemic symptoms, unusual age of onset or rapid deterioration.

The most urgent exclusion is **delirium**, because it is a medical emergency. I would look for impaired attention or consciousness with an acute and fluctuating course, often accompanied by disorientation, cognitive fluctuation, sleep–wake disturbance, illusions or visual hallucinations. An agitated or psychotic-looking patient can still be delirious, so severe behavioural disturbance must not distract from the level of consciousness and attention.

I would then look for **neurological and systemic clues**: new focal or other neurological signs, seizures, endocrine or metabolic symptoms, infection, hypoxia, head injury or a rapidly changing cognitive state. The exact symptom profile of a secondary syndrome may resemble a primary psychiatric disorder, so the distinction often comes from chronology and associated physical findings rather than from the psychiatric phenomenology itself.

A careful **medication and substance history** is essential. I would review prescribed medication, recent changes, over-the-counter or alternative agents, intoxication, withdrawal and other substance exposure. Temporal association matters, but exposure alone is not proof of causation.

The examination should include a **physical and neurological assessment** whenever a secondary cause is plausible, and investigations should follow the clinical hypothesis. I would not imply that every psychiatric presentation needs the same exhaustive test panel. Examples of causes worth considering, depending on the presentation, include neurological disease or epilepsy, neurodegenerative illness, endocrine or metabolic disorders, infection or encephalitis, autoimmune or limbic processes, head injury, hypoxia, medication toxicity and substance-related states.

To decide whether a medical or substance factor is genuinely causal, I would use a simple **causal sequence**: is there a disease or exposure capable of producing the syndrome; is the temporal relationship plausible; does the psychiatric syndrome improve as the cause resolves; and is there a better primary psychiatric explanation? None of these should be considered in isolation.

The practical principle is therefore targeted suspicion rather than indiscriminate investigation. Acute fluctuation, impaired attention, neurological or systemic abnormalities, medication or substance timing and an atypical course should move secondary causes to the front of the differential and trigger the appropriate medical work-up.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Assuming a psychiatric-looking syndrome must be a primary psychiatric disorder.
- Missing delirium in an agitated or psychotic patient.
- Ignoring medication changes, intoxication or withdrawal.
- Ordering indiscriminate investigations without a clinical hypothesis.
