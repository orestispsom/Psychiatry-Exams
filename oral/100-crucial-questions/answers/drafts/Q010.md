# Q10. How do you recognise catatonia and how do you treat it?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise at least three catatonic signs**
2. **Think transdiagnostically**
3. **Search for medical, neurological, toxic and withdrawal causes**
4. **Treat complications in parallel**
5. **Lorazepam / lorazepam challenge**
6. **Escalate to ECT when needed**
7. **Use antipsychotics cautiously, especially in malignant presentations**

## Model oral answer

Catatonia is a **transdiagnostic psychomotor syndrome**, not a subtype of schizophrenia. I would recognise it from a cluster of characteristic motor, behavioural and speech abnormalities, actively search for psychiatric and medical causes, treat physical complications at the same time, and start benzodiazepine treatment promptly with early use of ECT when response is inadequate or the presentation is severe.

A practical current threshold is **three or more catatonic signs**. The syndrome can present with reduced activity, for example stupor and mutism; abnormal psychomotor phenomena such as posturing, catalepsy or waxy flexibility, negativism, mannerisms, stereotypy, grimacing and echophenomena; or an **excited/agitated form**. The latter matters because catatonia can be missed if the clinician expects only an immobile patient. A structured examination or rating instrument such as the **BFCRS** may help identify and follow signs, but it does not replace clinical diagnosis or aetiological assessment.

Catatonia occurs across **mood disorders, psychotic disorders, autism, neurological and medical disease, and substance or medication states**. I would therefore take collateral history, perform physical and neurological examination and order targeted investigations according to the presentation, looking specifically for medical, neurological, toxic and withdrawal causes.

At the same time, I would manage **supportive and medical complications**. Depending on the patient, these can include dehydration or poor nutrition, complications of immobility, pressure injury, aspiration, thromboembolism and other medical risks. Treatment of the syndrome and treatment of the underlying cause proceed in parallel.

**Benzodiazepines and ECT are first-line treatments.** Lorazepam is commonly used, and a lorazepam challenge can support the diagnosis and demonstrate treatment responsiveness. The verified challenge range is **1–2 mg IV or IM, or 2 mg orally**. A challenge is helpful rather than mandatory: failure to respond does not exclude catatonia and should not become a reason to delay treatment.

**ECT** is indicated when benzodiazepines are ineffective or inadequate, when the presentation is severe or medically risky, and particularly in malignant catatonia. Dopamine antagonists and antipsychotics require caution in catatonia, especially when malignant catatonia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a possibility.

If the patient has **fever, autonomic instability and rigidity**, the situation becomes a medical emergency. I would consider malignant catatonia and the major overlapping differential of NMS, stop dopamine antagonists when malignant catatonia is suspected, provide urgent medical and supportive care and treat promptly with lorazepam. If response is inadequate, ECT should be escalated rapidly rather than delayed; **48–72 hours** is the relevant urgency anchor in a malignant presentation.

The key is not to wait for perfect nosological certainty while the patient deteriorates. Recognition of the syndrome, exclusion and treatment of secondary causes, supportive care and timely benzodiazepine or ECT treatment should proceed together.

## Must-know board facts

- Practical current diagnostic threshold: **≥3 catatonic signs**.
- Lorazepam challenge: **1–2 mg IV/IM or 2 mg oral**.
- Malignant catatonia: stop dopamine antagonists, start urgent lorazepam and escalate rapidly to ECT if response is inadequate; **48–72 hours** is the current urgency anchor.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q10a. What changes if the patient also has fever, autonomic instability and rigidity?

Treat this as a **malignant catatonia/NMS-level emergency**. Arrange urgent medical assessment and supportive care, stop dopamine antagonists when malignant catatonia is suspected and start lorazepam urgently. If response is inadequate, escalate rapidly to ECT rather than waiting; 48–72 hours is the relevant urgency anchor.

NMS is an important overlapping differential. Recent dopamine-antagonist exposure or withdrawal of dopaminergic agents, rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability and CK or other medical complications should be assessed, while recognising substantial clinical overlap. Definitive treatment should not be delayed while waiting for perfect diagnostic certainty in a medically deteriorating patient.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Restricting catatonia to schizophrenia.
- Missing excited catatonia because the patient is not stuporous.
- Giving antipsychotics reflexively before considering malignant catatonia or NMS.
- Delaying ECT in a medically unstable malignant presentation.
