# Q97. How do you critically appraise a psychiatric study and decide whether it should change practice?

## Recall spine

1. **Question and design**
2. **Population and comparator**
3. **Internal validity**
4. **Effect size and precision**
5. **Clinical versus statistical significance**
6. **Harms and generalisability**
7. **Registration, reporting and total evidence**

## Model oral answer

I would appraise a psychiatric study by asking three broad questions: **Is the design capable of answering the clinical question, are the results internally valid, and are the findings important and applicable enough to change practice?** A statistically significant result is only one part of that judgement.

First, I would define the **clinical question**, usually in PICO terms: the population, intervention or exposure, comparator and outcomes. I would then ask whether the study design fits that question and whether the participants and comparator resemble the clinical decision I am trying to make.

Second, I would examine **internal validity**. In a randomised trial that includes the quality of randomisation and allocation concealment, blinding where feasible, attrition and missing data, and whether analysis preserved the randomised comparison, for example through an intention-to-treat approach. In observational studies I would focus more heavily on confounding, selection bias and other systematic differences that could explain the association.

Third, I would look at the **results themselves**: the effect size, confidence intervals and therefore precision, and the absolute as well as relative effect. Where meaningful, NNT or NNH can help translate the result into clinical terms. A small p value does not tell me whether the benefit is large enough to matter clinically.

I would then balance **benefits against harms, burden and feasibility**. That includes adverse effects, treatment burden and whether multiple outcomes or repeated analyses raise concern about chance findings. I would also assess external validity: whether the study population, setting and intervention are sufficiently similar to my patients and service for the result to generalise.

Selective reporting is another major issue. I would ask whether the trial or protocol was **prospectively registered**, whether outcomes and analyses were prespecified, and whether the published report appears to match those plans. Funding sources and conflicts of interest should also be considered because they can affect study design, analysis or interpretation.

Finally, I would place the study within the **total body of evidence**. One positive study should change practice only if the effect is clinically meaningful, reasonably precise, applicable and safe, and sufficiently consistent with the broader evidence. The stronger and more practice-changing the claim, the stronger that overall evidential foundation should be.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating **p < 0.05** as proof that an effect is clinically important.
- Ignoring absolute effect size and precision.
- Ignoring harms, external validity, preregistration or selective reporting.
- Changing practice on the basis of one isolated result without considering the total evidence.
