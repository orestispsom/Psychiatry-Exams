# Q49. How do you recognise and manage frontotemporal dementia?

## Recall spine

1. **Behavioural change may lead the syndrome**
2. **Disinhibition, apathy and loss of empathy**
3. **Stereotypy, compulsivity and hyperorality**
4. **Executive dysfunction with possible language variants**
5. **Use imaging to support the clinical diagnosis**
6. **Do not import routine Alzheimer drugs**
7. **Use symptom-targeted and supportive management**

## Model oral answer

Frontotemporal dementia, particularly behavioural-variant FTD, often presents with **early change in personality, behaviour and executive function rather than a predominantly amnestic syndrome**. The key is to recognise a progressive behavioural-executive pattern, distinguish it from primary psychiatric illness and Alzheimer disease, and manage symptoms and safety without automatically using generic “anti-dementia” medication.

Typical early features include **behavioural disinhibition**, socially inappropriate behaviour or loss of restraint; **apathy or inertia**; reduced empathy or sympathy; perseverative, stereotyped or compulsive behaviour; and **hyperorality or dietary change**. Executive dysfunction is common. Language-predominant variants can also occur, but in a general viva answer the central point is that FTD may begin as a behavioural or language syndrome rather than as classic episodic-memory failure.

Relative preservation of episodic memory and visuospatial function is a common early pattern, but it is **not absolute**. Memory impairment does not exclude bvFTD. This is important because an overly rigid “memory spared” rule can delay diagnosis in patients who otherwise have a compelling behavioural and executive syndrome.

The psychiatric differential is especially important. Mania may produce disinhibition and reduced social judgement, depression may present with apathy, and longstanding personality pathology may resemble behavioural change. Psychosis can also complicate the picture. The distinction depends on demonstrating a **progressive acquired change from previous personality and function**, supported by collateral history and the broader neurological/cognitive course. Alzheimer disease remains an important differential when memory or visuospatial decline is prominent.

Structural MRI may show **frontal or anterior temporal atrophy**. Functional imaging such as FDG-PET or perfusion SPECT can support the diagnosis when uncertainty remains. Normal imaging alone should not be used to rule out FTD if the clinical syndrome is otherwise convincing.

There is currently **no established disease-modifying or cognitive-enhancing drug** for FTD. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine should not be routinely offered simply because the patient has dementia. Management is symptom-targeted and supportive. SSRIs or trazodone may be used in selected patients for disinhibition, compulsive behaviour, irritability or hyperorality, but evidence is limited and treatment should be matched to the specific problem.

Non-pharmacological management is central: carer education, environmental and behavioural strategies, safety planning and multidisciplinary support. The overall aim is to reduce harmful behaviour, preserve function where possible and support carers who may be dealing with profound personality and behavioural change.

## Must-know board facts

- Early episodic-memory sparing is **common but not required**.
- **Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine are not routine FTD treatments**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Rejecting FTD because episodic memory is impaired.
- Diagnosing bipolar or personality disorder without considering progressive acquired behavioural change.
- Automatically carrying Alzheimer medication into FTD.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Treatment update:** Generic “anti-dementia drug” prescribing is inappropriate in FTD. Current practice is supportive and symptom-targeted; routine acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine should be avoided.
