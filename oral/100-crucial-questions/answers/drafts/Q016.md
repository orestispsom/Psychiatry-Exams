# Q16. How do you treat an acute episode of schizophrenia?

## Recall spine

1. **Reassess diagnosis, risk and setting**
2. **Choose one antipsychotic collaboratively**
3. **Start low and titrate**
4. **Run an adequate 4–6-week trial**
5. **Manage severe agitation separately**
6. **Add CBTp and family intervention**
7. **Monitor and reassess before changing strategy**

## Model oral answer

Management of an acute episode starts with **reassessment**, not simply prescribing a higher antipsychotic dose. I would confirm the diagnosis and current syndrome, assess severity and immediate risk, review substance use, medical causes, adherence and current medication, and decide whether the patient needs inpatient care, crisis treatment or can be managed at home according to clinical need and risk.

Antipsychotic treatment should then be chosen through **shared decision-making**. Previous response and the patient’s priorities matter, particularly their individual vulnerability to metabolic effects, extrapyramidal symptoms, cardiovascular or QT effects, prolactin or hormonal adverse effects, and subjective tolerability. The aim is to select one oral antipsychotic that the patient can realistically take and continue.

I would use it as an **explicit therapeutic trial**: begin at the lower end of the licensed range, titrate gradually, define the target symptoms, and monitor both adverse effects and adherence. If it is tolerated, the usual trial should be assessed at an optimum tolerated dose over about **4–6 weeks**. Early improvement at around two weeks has prognostic value, but failure to achieve a fixed 20% improvement by that point is not, by itself, a mandatory reason to switch treatment.

Routine **rapid neuroleptisation, loading and antipsychotic polypharmacy** should be avoided. Brief overlap while switching antipsychotics is a different situation from maintaining two agents without a clear rationale.

Severe agitation requiring **rapid tranquillisation** is a separate emergency-management problem and should not be confused with the ordinary pharmacological treatment of schizophrenia. High antipsychotic doses should not simply be used as sedation, and benzodiazepines are not routine augmentation for the schizophrenia syndrome itself.

Medication is only one part of acute treatment. In both first episodes and acute recurrences, antipsychotic treatment should be combined with **CBT for psychosis and family intervention**. Psychological work does not have to wait for a chronic or fully stabilised phase; it can begin during the acute episode according to the patient’s ability and clinical need.

Physical health and treatment-emergent adverse effects should be monitored from the start. If response is inadequate, I would first check whether the trial was genuinely adequate: dose, duration, adherence and exposure, diagnostic accuracy, ongoing substance use and other secondary factors all need review before switching or escalating. Repeated adequate failures then move the problem toward the treatment-resistant-schizophrenia pathway rather than repeated empirical changes.

## Must-know board facts

- **Adequate acute antipsychotic trial:** about **4–6 weeks at the optimum tolerated dose** if the drug is tolerated.
- **Early response:** improvement at about 2 weeks has prognostic value, but a fixed 20% non-response threshold is not a mandatory switch rule.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Routine antipsychotic polypharmacy.
- Using high antipsychotic doses mainly to sedate the patient.
- Treating severe agitation and ordinary antipsychotic treatment as the same problem.
- Delaying psychological and family intervention until the chronic phase.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Early non-response:** Older teaching sometimes converted the two-week/20% response finding into a rigid switching rule. It is better understood as a predictor of later response, while an otherwise appropriate and tolerated antipsychotic trial is generally assessed over about 4–6 weeks at an optimum tolerated dose.
