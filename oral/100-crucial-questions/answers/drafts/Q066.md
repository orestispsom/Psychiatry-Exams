# Q66. How do you assess excessive daytime sleepiness and recognise narcolepsy?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm true daytime sleepiness**
2. **Exclude insufficient sleep, OSA, drugs and circadian causes**
3. **Ask about cataplexy and REM phenomena**
4. **Distinguish type 1 from type 2 narcolepsy**
5. **Use overnight PSG followed by MSLT**
6. **Treat through specialist sleep care**

## Model oral answer

Excessive daytime sleepiness is a symptom, so the first task is to establish whether the patient is genuinely sleepy and identify common alternative causes before diagnosing narcolepsy. I would exclude insufficient sleep, obstructive sleep apnoea, sedating medication, circadian disorder, depression or medical illness, then look specifically for narcoleptic features. **Cataplexy is the most discriminating clinical clue for narcolepsy type 1.**

I would start with the sleep history: total sleep opportunity, usual sleep schedule, naps, shift work, snoring or witnessed apnoeas, sedative or other medication use, alcohol and other substances, and whether the complaint is sleepiness rather than fatigue or low motivation. Circadian delay and chronic insufficient sleep are common mimics and need to be separated from an intrinsic hypersomnolence disorder.

Narcolepsy is characterised by persistent excessive sleepiness with irresistible sleep episodes or sleep attacks. I would ask directly about **cataplexy**—brief episodes of loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion—and about sleep paralysis and hypnagogic or hypnopompic hallucinations. Those REM-related phenomena support the diagnosis but are neither required nor specific enough to diagnose narcolepsy by themselves.

Current classification distinguishes two main forms. **Narcolepsy type 1** is associated with cataplexy and/or hypocretin or orexin deficiency within the appropriate diagnostic framework. **Type 2** lacks cataplexy and confirmed hypocretin deficiency. I would avoid turning the viva answer into a numerical hypocretin-threshold recital unless specifically asked.

Objective assessment in an appropriate patient uses **overnight polysomnography followed by a multiple sleep latency test**. This pathway helps confirm pathological sleepiness and characteristic sleep-onset REM phenomena while also identifying competing sleep disorders. The result must still be interpreted in the context of adequate prior sleep and the broader clinical picture.

Management is specialist and combines behavioural measures, regular and scheduled sleep strategies, and pharmacotherapy directed at daytime sleepiness and cataplexy as appropriate. There is no need to list every available agent in a general viva answer; the important point is accurate diagnosis, exclusion of common causes and specialist treatment of the disabling symptoms.

The practical sequence is therefore: verify excessive sleepiness, exclude common secondary causes, look for cataplexy and other REM phenomena, investigate with PSG/MSLT when appropriate, and manage confirmed narcolepsy through specialist sleep care.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q66a. What is REM sleep behaviour disorder and why does it matter psychiatrically?

REM sleep behaviour disorder is **dream-enactment behaviour caused by loss of normal REM muscle atonia**. Diagnosis is supported or confirmed by polysomnographic demonstration of REM sleep without atonia.

Management begins with **injury prevention**, review of provoking medication and assessment for other sleep disorders. Immediate-release melatonin or clonazepam remain conditionally supported symptomatic options in suitable patients.

The major long-term significance is its strong association with **alpha-synucleinopathies**, including Parkinson disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. That association is prognostically important, but there is **no established neuroprotective treatment** that should be promised to prevent future neurodegeneration.

## Must-know board facts

- **Cataplexy strongly points to narcolepsy type 1**.
- RBD = **dream enactment + REM sleep without atonia**.
- RBD has a strong association with **alpha-synucleinopathies**, but no established neuroprotective therapy.
