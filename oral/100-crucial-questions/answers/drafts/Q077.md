# Q77. What extrapyramidal adverse effects occur with antipsychotics, and how do you manage them?

## Recall spine

1. **Acute dystonia — urgent antimuscarinic**
2. **Parkinsonism — reduce burden or switch**
3. **Akathisia — recognise subjective restlessness**
4. **Tardive dyskinesia — minimise dopamine blockade**
5. **Avoid unnecessary chronic anticholinergics**
6. **VMAT2 inhibition for significant TD**

## Model oral answer

The four high-yield extrapyramidal syndromes are **acute dystonia, drug-induced parkinsonism, akathisia and tardive dyskinesia**. Management starts by identifying the syndrome correctly, reviewing the need and dose of the dopamine-blocking drug, and then using syndrome-specific treatment. The main examination trap is to treat every movement complaint with an anticholinergic.

**Acute dystonia** is an early-onset sustained muscle-contraction syndrome after dopamine blockade, often involving the neck, jaw, eyes or other muscle groups. It requires urgent antimuscarinic treatment. **Laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency**, so airway safety takes priority over routine symptom management.

**Drug-induced parkinsonism** produces a parkinsonian motor syndrome during dopamine blockade. The preferred approach is to reassess whether the antipsychotic dose can be reduced and whether switching to a lower-risk strategy is possible without destabilising the psychiatric illness. A short course of an anticholinergic may be useful when clinically necessary, but automatic long-term prophylactic anticholinergic treatment should be avoided because it adds its own adverse-effect burden.

**Akathisia** is different. The key feature is a subjective inner restlessness or urge to move together with observable motor restlessness, often emerging after a drug is started or increased. It can be mistaken for anxiety, mania or worsening psychotic agitation and may lead to exactly the wrong response—further antipsychotic escalation. Dose reduction or switching should be considered where feasible, and **propranolol is a major pharmacological option** when not contraindicated. Pure akathisia is not generally managed with anticholinergics.

**Tardive dyskinesia** is a later persistent hyperkinetic movement disorder associated with dopamine-receptor-blocking exposure. Management includes confirming the diagnosis, stopping unnecessary dopamine-blocking exposure, using the lowest necessary ongoing burden and stopping unnecessary anticholinergics. A lower-risk antipsychotic strategy may be appropriate, and **VMAT2 inhibition** can be considered for clinically significant TD.

A relevant current update is **deutetrabenazine**, which received EU-wide authorisation in January 2026 for moderate-to-severe tardive dyskinesia in adults. That authorisation should not be translated automatically into a claim of routine Greek availability or reimbursement.

The safest overall approach is therefore phenomenology first: dystonia is an urgent muscle-contraction syndrome, parkinsonism calls for reduction or switching where possible, akathisia requires recognition of the inner restlessness rather than reflexive dose escalation, and TD requires minimising dopamine-blocker exposure and considering specific VMAT2 treatment.

## Must-know board facts

- **Laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency.**
- Pure akathisia is not generically treated with anticholinergics; **propranolol is a major option** when appropriate.
- **Deutetrabenazine** has EU-wide authorisation for moderate–severe adult TD; routine Greek access/reimbursement has not been established.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q77a. How do you distinguish akathisia from agitation?

Akathisia requires **subjective inner restlessness or an urge to move together with observable motor restlessness**, usually with a temporal relationship to starting or increasing a causative drug. I would ask the patient directly whether movement relieves an internal sense of restlessness and examine for pacing, shifting weight or inability to remain still.

Psychotic agitation, mania and anxiety can all produce increased activity, but they are distinguished by the accompanying mental state and by the absence of the characteristic medication-linked inner motor restlessness. This distinction matters because increasing the antipsychotic for mislabelled akathisia may worsen the syndrome. Dose reduction or switching is preferred where feasible, and propranolol is a major pharmacological option if suitable; anticholinergics are not generic treatment for pure akathisia.
