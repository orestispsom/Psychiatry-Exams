# Q64. How do you diagnose and treat bulimia nervosa?

## Recall spine

1. **Recurrent binge eating with loss of control**
2. **Recurrent compensatory behaviour**
3. **Weight and shape overvaluation**
4. **Assess purging-related medical risk**
5. **Use CBT-based eating-disorder treatment**
6. **Fluoxetine 60 mg/day only as an adjunct**

## Model oral answer

Bulimia nervosa is characterised by **recurrent binge eating with loss of control, recurrent compensatory behaviours, and excessive influence of weight or shape on self-evaluation**. The key diagnostic distinction from anorexia nervosa is that if the person is significantly underweight within the anorexia-nervosa architecture, the diagnosis remains anorexia nervosa rather than bulimia nervosa. Management combines medical-risk assessment with CBT-based eating-disorder treatment, with medication only as an adjunct where appropriate.

I would first establish the binge-eating pattern: whether episodes involve a subjective or objective sense of excessive intake together with **loss of control**, and how they relate to restriction, emotional states and compensatory behaviours. I would then ask about vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, fasting and excessive exercise, and assess the degree to which weight and shape dominate self-evaluation.

I would not use a rigid BMI 17.5 boundary to separate bulimia from anorexia. The diagnostic question is whether the overall syndrome is one of anorexia nervosa with significantly low weight and the corresponding restrictive/weight-gain-prevention architecture, or bulimia nervosa without that anorexia framework.

Physical assessment should focus especially on the consequences of purging. Repeated vomiting or other compensatory behaviours can cause **hypokalaemia and other electrolyte abnormalities, dehydration and arrhythmia or QT-related risk**. I would ask about dental and gastrointestinal complications and obtain electrolytes and an ECG when clinically indicated. Menstrual or endocrine issues may also be relevant depending on the individual presentation.

Psychiatric assessment should include depression, anxiety, self-harm or suicide risk, substance use and other comorbidity. The disorder often has a strong shame component, so direct, non-judgemental questioning is important.

The treatment backbone is **CBT-based eating-disorder treatment**. A stepped approach may begin with bulimia-focused guided self-help and progress to individual CBT-ED when needed. Treatment addresses regular eating, the binge–restriction–compensation cycle, shape and weight overvaluation, and relapse prevention rather than focusing only on stopping vomiting.

Medication can be useful, but should not replace psychotherapy. In adults, **fluoxetine 60 mg/day** is a valid board-level treatment fact as an **adjunct to psychotherapy** to reduce bingeing and purging. It should not be presented as sole treatment or as evidence that pharmacotherapy is the central intervention.

The practical approach is therefore to define the binge–compensation syndrome accurately, assess medical consequences of purging, distinguish it from anorexia nervosa without a rigid BMI rule, and use CBT-based treatment with fluoxetine only as an adjunct where appropriate.

## Must-know board fact

- **Fluoxetine 60 mg/day** is an evidence-based **adjunctive** treatment in adult bulimia nervosa; it is not a substitute for psychotherapy.
