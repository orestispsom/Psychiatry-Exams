# Q72. How do you assess and manage a paraphilic disorder and associated risk?

## Recall spine

1. **Atypical interest is not automatically a disorder**
2. **Clarify consent, harm, distress and direct risk**
3. **Take sexual and forensic history**
4. **Assess potential victims, access and escalation**
5. **Assess comorbidity, disinhibition and protective factors**
6. **Use specialist psychotherapy and risk management**
7. **Reserve medication for selected indications**

## Model oral answer

An atypical sexual interest or paraphilia is **not automatically a psychiatric disorder**. The assessment should first determine whether the pattern involves non-consenting individuals, clinically significant distress that is not simply a consequence of stigma or social rejection, or substantial direct risk of injury or death depending on the condition. From there, the task is a careful sexual, psychiatric and forensic risk formulation rather than moral judgement about consensual sexuality.

I would clarify the nature, frequency and persistence of the fantasies, urges and behaviours, whether they have been acted on, and the degree of voluntary control. Consent status is central. I would ask whether any behaviour involves or targets non-consenting people and whether there are identifiable potential victims or situations that increase access and opportunity.

Risk assessment should be specific and behavioural. I would explore escalation over time, prior sexual and non-sexual offending, planning or rehearsal, access to potential victims, substance use or other disinhibiting factors, current mental disorder, impulse control, protective relationships, treatment engagement and willingness to use risk-management strategies. Safeguarding and public-protection actions depend on the actual level and nature of risk and on the relevant jurisdiction; the diagnosis itself does not dictate one automatic response.

Comorbidity matters because depression, anxiety, obsessive or compulsive features, substance use and other psychiatric disorders may affect both distress and risk. I would distinguish distress intrinsic to the sexual pattern from distress caused solely by shame, stigma or fear of social rejection, because current classification deliberately avoids pathologising consensual atypical sexual interests on that basis alone.

Treatment is specialist and usually combines **psychotherapy, relapse-prevention work and explicit risk-management strategies**. Interventions should target triggers, cognitive and behavioural patterns, self-regulation, access to risk situations and protective factors.

Medication may be considered selectively. **SSRIs** can have a role in some lower- or moderate-risk presentations with compulsive or intrusive sexual preoccupation or relevant comorbidity. **Antiandrogenic treatment or GnRH analogues** are specialist interventions for selected severe or high-risk cases and require an appropriate consent and legal framework together with endocrine, metabolic, bone and cardiovascular monitoring. They should never be presented as routine treatment for consensual atypical sexuality.

The key viva principle is therefore to separate interest from disorder, organise assessment around consent and harm, formulate risk specifically, and use proportionate specialist treatment rather than pathologising sexual diversity.

## Must-know board fact

- **Paraphilia is not synonymous with paraphilic disorder.**

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older category-driven teaching could pathologise atypical sexual interests too readily. **Current ICD-11 explicitly avoids classifying consensual atypical interests as disorders without qualifying harm, non-consent, clinically relevant distress or direct risk.**
