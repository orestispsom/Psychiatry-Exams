# Q20. What are the clinical features, diagnosis and important differentials of major depressive disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Syndrome and impairment**
2. **Affective, cognitive, biological and psychomotor features**
3. **Suicide risk and psychotic severity**
4. **Current diagnostic anchor**
5. **Screen for bipolarity**
6. **Exclude substance/medication and medical causes**
7. **Make a longitudinal diagnosis**

## Model oral answer

Depression is a syndrome, not simply low mood. The core presentation is persistent depressed mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure, accompanied by a clinically significant change in functioning and a broader pattern of cognitive, biological and psychomotor symptoms. Diagnosis should be longitudinal and should include active assessment for suicide risk, psychosis, bipolarity and secondary causes.

Clinically, I would organise the syndrome into four domains. **Affective symptoms** include depressed mood, anhedonia, reduced emotional reactivity and, in some patients, prominent anxiety or irritability. **Cognitive symptoms** include pessimism and hopelessness, excessive guilt or worthlessness, impaired concentration and decision-making, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide. Suicidality needs a separate direct assessment of intent, planning and immediate risk rather than being treated as just another symptom on a checklist.

The **biological or somatic features** include sleep disturbance, appetite or weight change, loss of energy or fatigue, reduced libido and sometimes diurnal variation. **Psychomotor change** may take the form of retardation or agitation; in very severe depression, retardation can progress to stupor.

Severity also matters because depression may become **psychotic**. Delusions or hallucinations can be mood-congruent or mood-incongruent, and nihilistic or Cotard-type ideas are a classic severe-depression phenomenon. Psychotic depression changes the urgency and the treatment approach. Melancholic and atypical patterns can also be clinically useful descriptions, but they do not replace the underlying depressive-episode diagnosis.

For current ICD-11 diagnosis, a depressive episode requires at least **5 of 10 symptoms for at least 2 weeks**, with either depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure present, together with clinically significant impairment and appropriate exclusions. Terminology should also be kept clear: **major depressive disorder** is a DSM term, whereas ICD-11 describes single-episode depressive disorder or recurrent depressive disorder according to longitudinal course.

Before concluding that the illness is unipolar, I would actively look for any lifetime history of mania or hypomania, mixed manic symptoms, collateral evidence and treatment-emergent episodes. Severe, recurrent or even psychotic depression alone does not establish bipolar disorder.

The differential includes bipolar depression, adjustment or bereavement-related presentations, substance- or medication-induced depression, endocrine, metabolic, neurological and other medical causes, and depressive symptoms occurring within other psychiatric disorders. The diagnosis should therefore integrate the history and mental-state examination with physical and neurological assessment and targeted investigations where the presentation suggests a secondary cause, rather than relying on symptom count alone.

## Must-know board facts

- **Current ICD-11 depressive episode:** at least **5 of 10 symptoms**, for at least **2 weeks**, with depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure among them.
- **Terminology:** “major depressive disorder” is DSM terminology; ICD-11 uses single-episode or recurrent depressive disorder according to course.
- **Cotard-type nihilistic delusions:** a classic phenomenon of severe psychotic depression.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q20a. How do you distinguish PMDD from premenstrual worsening of another disorder?

The key distinction is whether there is a genuine **cyclical on/off pattern**. In PMDD or a core premenstrual disorder, symptoms emerge in the luteal or premenstrual phase, improve within a few days after menstruation starts, and are minimal or absent during the post-menstrual follicular interval, with clear functional impairment.

In **premenstrual exacerbation**, an underlying psychiatric or medical disorder is present throughout the cycle but becomes worse premenstrually. Persistent significant symptoms during the follicular phase therefore favour PME or comorbidity rather than pure PMDD.

When diagnostic certainty matters, retrospective recall is not enough. Prospective daily symptom ratings across **at least two consecutive symptomatic cycles** are the key confirmation method; the DRSP is one commonly used tool.

**Follow-up recall spine:** cyclical on/off → follicular symptom-free interval → persistent baseline disorder suggests PME → prospective ratings for 2 cycles.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing depression from sadness alone.
- Forgetting lifetime mania or hypomania before calling recurrent depression unipolar.
- Treating melancholic or atypical features as separate disorders.
- Missing psychosis, suicidality or medical/substance causes.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification update:** Older teaching may use the ICD-10 “2 of 3 core symptoms” hierarchy. Current ICD-11 uses a different depressive-episode framework; do not present the older hierarchy as current diagnostic truth.
