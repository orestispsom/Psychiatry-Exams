# Q39. How do you manage alcohol use disorder after withdrawal?

## Recall spine

1. **Define the treatment goal**
2. **Build structured psychosocial relapse prevention**
3. **Use acamprosate or naltrexone when appropriate**
4. **Reserve disulfiram and nalmefene for selected situations**
5. **Monitor recovery, adherence and physical health**
6. **Continue treatment long enough to consolidate change**

## Model oral answer

Detoxification is only the beginning of treatment for alcohol use disorder. After withdrawal, I would agree a clear goal—usually abstinence or reduction—then combine structured psychosocial relapse-prevention work with pharmacotherapy when appropriate, matched to medical status, opioid use, adherence and previous response.

The psychosocial plan should be explicit rather than generic advice to “stay sober.” Useful approaches include **motivational work, CBT and other behavioural strategies**, together with social-network or family involvement when appropriate. Mutual-aid groups can also be useful for some patients. The focus should be on identifying triggers, strengthening coping strategies, planning for high-risk situations and improving the social conditions that support recovery.

The medication strategy depends on the goal and the individual patient. **Acamprosate and oral naltrexone are the principal relapse-prevention medicines after withdrawal.** The choice should consider liver and renal status, current or foreseeable opioid use, adherence, previous benefit and patient preference. Medication should normally sit within a psychosocial treatment plan rather than replace it.

**Disulfiram** is more selective. It may be considered when abstinence is the agreed goal and first-choice options are unsuitable, not tolerated, ineffective or when the patient specifically prefers it. Its effectiveness depends heavily on adherence, so supervision can be useful, and the safety implications of drinking while taking it need to be part of the treatment discussion. It should not be presented as the routine first-line medication for everyone with alcohol dependence.

**Nalmefene** is different again because it is reduction-oriented rather than primarily an abstinence medication. It remains an option for a defined subgroup of people who are drinking at a harmful level but do not require immediate detoxification. It therefore should not simply be inserted into the same post-detoxification sequence as acamprosate or naltrexone without considering the treatment goal and clinical context.

Duration also needs to be practical rather than rigid. About **6 months** is a useful treatment anchor for acamprosate or naltrexone, but this is not an automatic stopping date. Continued treatment should be individualised according to benefit, relapse risk, adherence, adverse effects and the patient’s progress.

Long-term follow-up should include more than drinking quantity. I would monitor **craving, lapses or relapse, psychiatric comorbidity, physical complications, social and occupational recovery, adherence and the patient’s own goals**. If relapse occurs, the response should be to reassess what is maintaining the disorder and strengthen treatment rather than treating it as proof that treatment has failed.

The core principle is that alcohol withdrawal management removes acute physiological risk, whereas recovery from alcohol use disorder requires a sustained relapse-prevention plan combining psychosocial treatment, appropriately selected medication and longitudinal follow-up.

## Must-know board facts

- **Withdrawal treatment alone is not treatment of alcohol use disorder.**
- Pharmacotherapy for relapse prevention should usually be combined with structured psychosocial care.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Listing relapse-prevention drugs without matching them to treatment goal and contraindications.
- Treating disulfiram as routine first-line medication.
- Treating a lapse as automatic treatment failure rather than reassessing maintaining factors.
