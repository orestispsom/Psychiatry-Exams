# Q82. What do you need to know about valproate in psychiatry?

## Recall spine

1. **Bipolar indication where appropriate**
2. **Weight and metabolic effects**
3. **Tremor and haematological toxicity**
4. **Hepatic and pancreatic toxicity**
5. **Baseline and ongoing monitoring**
6. **Pregnancy Prevention Programme**
7. **Current male reproductive precautions**

## Model oral answer

Valproate can be used in bipolar disorder in appropriate patients, but prescribing is dominated by its **metabolic, haematological, hepatic, pancreatic and reproductive risks**. I would use it only after considering alternatives, obtain the required baseline and ongoing monitoring, and apply the current reproductive restrictions to both women and men. The reproductive framework must be stated accurately rather than reduced to either an outdated permissive approach or an unconditional ban in every patient who could conceive.

Important adverse effects include **weight gain and broader metabolic burden**, tremor and haematological abnormalities including thrombocytopenia. More serious toxicities include **hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis**. These risks mean that treatment requires appropriate baseline assessment and continued monitoring according to the product information and relevant guideline rather than casual long-term prescribing without review.

The most important modern change is reproductive safety. For **bipolar disorder, valproate is contraindicated during pregnancy**. In women and girls able to become pregnant, it should not be described as an unconditional absolute prohibition in every circumstance: use is contraindicated **unless the stringent Pregnancy Prevention Programme conditions are met** and alternative treatments are unsuitable, ineffective or not tolerated under specialist management. That formulation reflects the severity of the reproductive risk while preserving the qualified regulatory framework.

Current European precautions also extend to **men taking valproate**. Men should receive counselling about the potential paternal neurodevelopmental risk. Contraception considerations apply during treatment and for **3 months after stopping valproate**, and sperm donation should be avoided during that same interval.

These reproductive issues should be integrated into ordinary prescribing rather than raised only after pregnancy occurs. The prescriber should establish whether pregnancy is relevant, discuss alternatives and reproductive plans, and document that the patient understands the applicable restrictions and precautions.

I would also review the reason valproate was chosen, its current benefit, adverse effects and the ongoing need for treatment. Monitoring and reproductive counselling are not substitutes for checking whether the drug remains clinically justified.

The concise viva answer is therefore: valproate is an effective mood-stabilising option in selected bipolar patients but requires attention to metabolic, haematological, hepatic and pancreatic toxicity, appropriate laboratory monitoring, and stringent modern reproductive regulation. Pregnancy is contraindicated for bipolar treatment; use in women able to conceive is tightly PPP-qualified, and men now also require specific reproductive counselling and precautions.

## Must-know board facts

- For bipolar disorder, valproate is **contraindicated during pregnancy**.
- In women/girls able to become pregnant, use is contraindicated unless **Pregnancy Prevention Programme** conditions are met and alternatives are unsuitable, ineffective or not tolerated under specialist care.
- Men require counselling and contraception considerations during treatment and for **3 months after stopping**; no sperm donation during that interval.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Describing valproate as an unconditional absolute ban in every woman of childbearing potential.
- Forgetting the current **male reproductive precautions**.
- Quoting old teratogenicity percentages instead of giving the current prescribing framework.
