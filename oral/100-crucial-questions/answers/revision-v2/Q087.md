# Q87. How do you distinguish and manage neuroleptic malignant syndrome and serotonin syndrome?

## Recall spine

1. **Identify the culprit exposure**
2. **NMS: rigidity and dopamine mechanism**
3. **Serotonin syndrome: clonus and hyperreflexia**
4. **Hyperthermia, autonomic and mental-state change**
5. **CK and medical complications**
6. **Stop causative drugs**
7. **Aggressive supportive/critical care**

## Model oral answer

Both neuroleptic malignant syndrome and serotonin syndrome are potentially life-threatening drug reactions with **mental-state change, autonomic instability and hyperthermia**, but the drug history and neuromuscular examination usually separate them. NMS is associated with dopamine blockade or dopaminergic withdrawal and is characterised by marked generalised rigidity; serotonin syndrome follows serotonergic excess and is distinguished particularly by **clonus and hyperreflexia**. In both, treatment starts by stopping the causative drug and providing aggressive supportive medical care.

For **NMS**, I would look for a recent dopamine-blocking exposure or relevant dopaminergic withdrawal together with hyperthermia, autonomic instability, altered mental state and prominent generalised rigidity. Creatine kinase is often raised and helps define the systemic muscle injury, but the diagnosis is clinical and should not depend on one laboratory result. NMS commonly evolves over roughly **1–3 days**, but timing is supportive rather than diagnostic.

**Serotonin syndrome** generally evolves much more rapidly after serotonergic exposure or dose change, often within **hours**. The most useful bedside discriminators are neuromuscular hyperactivity—particularly **clonus and hyperreflexia**—rather than simply fever and confusion. Those signs are especially discriminating and central to Hunter-type diagnostic reasoning, but I would avoid claiming that one sign is universally the single most sensitive and specific feature.

Management of either syndrome begins with **immediate withdrawal of the offending agents**. The patient then needs close medical assessment, fluids, cooling and management of complications, with ICU escalation when severe. The foundational treatment is supportive and critical care; specific drugs are adjuncts rather than mandatory antidotes.

In NMS, **bromocriptine, amantadine or dantrolene** may be considered in selected or severe cases, and ECT may have a role in severe/refractory presentations. In serotonin syndrome, **cyproheptadine**, a 5-HT2A antagonist, may be used in appropriate cases, although high-quality efficacy evidence is limited.

The differential should remain clinical and dynamic because both syndromes can deteriorate rapidly and may resemble other medical emergencies. The key examination sequence is therefore: identify the causative drug mechanism, examine the neuromuscular pattern, stop the culprit, provide aggressive supportive care, and use syndrome-specific adjuncts only when clinically appropriate.

## Must-know board facts

- **NMS:** dopamine blockade/dopaminergic withdrawal + marked rigidity; often evolves over **1–3 days**.
- **Serotonin syndrome:** usually rapid after serotonergic exposure/change, often within **hours**.
- **Clonus and hyperreflexia** are especially discriminating serotonin-toxicity signs.
- **Cyproheptadine:** 5-HT2A antagonist used as an adjunct in selected serotonin toxicity.
- **Bromocriptine/amantadine/dantrolene** are selected NMS options, not mandatory antidotes.
- Immediate drug cessation and **supportive/critical care** are foundational in both syndromes.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing serotonin syndrome from fever and confusion alone without looking for **clonus/hyperreflexia**.
- Treating rigid timing rules or pupil/GI findings as more important than the neuromuscular pattern.
- Calling cyproheptadine, dantrolene or bromocriptine a mandatory antidote.
- Focusing on a specific drug while neglecting fluids, cooling and critical-care management.
