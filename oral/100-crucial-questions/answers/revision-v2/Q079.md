# Q79. How do you choose an antidepressant?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm diagnosis and severity**
2. **Previous response and tolerability**
3. **Comorbidity and physical illness**
4. **Sleep, appetite and pain profile**
5. **Sexual, cardiac and overdose risk**
6. **Interactions and reproductive context**
7. **Patient preference**

## Model oral answer

Antidepressant choice should be **individualised to the depressive illness, the patient’s previous treatment history, comorbidity, adverse-effect vulnerability, interaction burden and preference**. SSRIs are a major first-line class because they combine efficacy with generally favourable tolerability and overdose safety, but SNRIs and mirtazapine are also standard options in appropriate patients. TCAs and MAOIs have important roles but require more selective use.

I would first confirm that I am treating a depressive disorder and assess its severity, associated risks and relevant comorbidity. Previous response is highly informative: if a patient has previously had a clear, well-tolerated response to a particular antidepressant, that may be more useful than choosing from an abstract class hierarchy.

The symptom profile can then help refine the choice. Sleep disturbance, appetite change and pain may make the differing clinical profiles of available antidepressants relevant. At the same time, I would actively ask which adverse effects would be especially problematic for this patient, including **sexual dysfunction, sedation, cardiovascular effects and weight-related concerns**.

Physical illness and overdose risk matter. SSRIs are generally attractive when overdose safety is an important consideration. **TCAs** are limited by their anticholinergic, cardiac and overdose toxicity, so they are less suitable when those risks are clinically significant. **MAOIs** remain specialist options rather than routine first-line treatment because of their interaction and switching complexity.

Mechanisms can help explain clinical differences without becoming a receptor catalogue. **SSRIs inhibit SERT**; **SNRIs inhibit SERT and NET**, with the relative noradrenergic contribution varying by agent and dose rather than appearing at one sharp threshold. **Mirtazapine** combines presynaptic alpha-2 antagonism with 5-HT2/5-HT3 and H1 antagonism, which helps explain its sedating and appetite-related profile. **Trazodone** combines 5-HT2A antagonism with serotonin-reuptake inhibition, with H1 and alpha-1 effects contributing at common doses.

Drug interactions should be considered before prescribing rather than after an adverse event. I would review all prescribed and non-prescribed medication, smoking where relevant, and the possibility of pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions. Pregnancy or reproductive considerations may also materially change the risk–benefit balance and should be handled in a drug-specific way rather than by a blanket rule.

Licensing context matters. **Bupropion is a routine antidepressant option in some jurisdictions but not uniformly across Europe**, so it should not be inserted automatically into a European treatment hierarchy. Similarly, no mechanistic fact should substitute for the actual product indication and safety profile.

The final decision should incorporate the patient’s preferences and previous subjective experience. A drug that is theoretically appropriate but unacceptable to the patient because of a feared or previously experienced adverse effect is unlikely to be a sustainable treatment.

The concise viva structure is therefore: diagnosis and severity, previous response, psychiatric and physical comorbidity, symptom profile, adverse-effect and overdose vulnerability, interactions and reproductive context, then shared choice. This is more clinically useful than assuming one antidepressant is universally best.

## Must-know board facts

- **SSRIs:** serotonin-transporter (**SERT**) inhibition.
- **SNRIs:** **SERT + NET** inhibition; noradrenergic contribution varies by drug and dose, without one sharp universal threshold.
- **Mirtazapine:** presynaptic **alpha-2 antagonism** plus **5-HT2/5-HT3 and H1 antagonism**.
- **Trazodone:** **5-HT2A antagonism + serotonin-reuptake inhibition**, with H1/alpha-1 effects contributing clinically.
- Bupropion should not be presented as a routine European antidepressant option without **jurisdiction/licensing context**.
- Antidepressant selection remains driven by prior response, comorbidity, sleep/appetite/pain, sexual effects, cardiac/overdose risk, interactions and patient preference.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Choosing an antidepressant from a rigid hierarchy without considering the individual patient.
- Ignoring overdose, cardiac or interaction risk.
- Teaching a sharp venlafaxine dose threshold at which NET activity suddenly appears.
- Presenting a mechanism map as though it determines efficacy ranking.
- Importing bupropion automatically into a European prescribing algorithm.
- Presenting routine commercial pharmacogenomic testing as a prerequisite for antidepressant prescribing.
