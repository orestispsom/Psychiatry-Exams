# Q84. What are the main principles for benzodiazepine and hypnotic prescribing?

## Recall spine

1. **GABA-A positive allosteric modulation**
2. **Short-term clinical indications**
3. **Sedation, falls and cognitive impairment**
4. **Tolerance and dependence**
5. **Opioid/alcohol respiratory risk**
6. **Withdrawal, including seizures and delirium**
7. **Individualised taper**

## Model oral answer

Benzodiazepines can be useful for selected short-term indications, but safe prescribing depends on anticipating **sedation, falls, cognitive effects, tolerance, dependence, respiratory interactions and withdrawal**. They are GABA-A positive allosteric modulators, and the aim is usually the lowest necessary exposure for the shortest clinically appropriate period rather than routine indefinite treatment. Z-drugs should be approached with similar dependence and sedation principles.

Benzodiazepines bind to benzodiazepine-sensitive **GABA-A receptor sites as positive allosteric modulators**, increasing the effect of endogenous GABA. Their immediate adverse-effect burden includes **sedation, psychomotor impairment, falls and cognitive impairment**. These risks are especially important with frailty, other sedating medication or circumstances in which impaired alertness could have serious consequences. Paradoxical disinhibition can also occur.

With repeated use, **tolerance and physical dependence** can develop. Dependence does not mean that every patient has a substance use disorder, but it does mean abrupt discontinuation may be unsafe. Withdrawal can include marked anxiety, autonomic symptoms and insomnia and may progress to **seizures or delirium** in severe cases.

A major interaction is with **opioids and alcohol**. Combining CNS depressants can markedly increase respiratory-depression risk, so co-prescribing and substance use must be actively reviewed rather than assuming that a familiar benzodiazepine remains safe in a changed medication context.

Duration should be framed carefully. The familiar **“2–4 weeks”** rule is a useful short-term prescribing and licensing heuristic for many anxiety or insomnia indications; it is **not a universal statutory ceiling**. Longer use needs an explicit indication, regular review and a dependence/withdrawal plan rather than continuation by inertia.

**Z-drugs are not dependence-free substitutes.** They also act at benzodiazepine-sensitive GABA-A receptors, with subtype preference, and can still cause tolerance, dependence, psychomotor impairment and complex sleep behaviours. Their pharmacological differences do not exempt them from hypnotic-safety principles.

If a benzodiazepine has been used long enough for dependence to be likely, discontinuation should be **gradual and individualised**. There is no single taper that suits every patient. The pace should reflect the drug, dose, duration of exposure, withdrawal symptoms, comorbidity and the patient’s ability to tolerate reduction.

The core viva answer is therefore to use benzodiazepines selectively, remember the risks of sedation and falls, avoid dangerous CNS-depressant combinations, anticipate tolerance and dependence, and taper individually when stopping. Z-drugs should not be treated as a fundamentally risk-free alternative.

## Must-know board facts

- Benzodiazepines are **positive allosteric modulators at GABA-A benzodiazepine sites**.
- Withdrawal can include **seizures and delirium**.
- “**2–4 weeks**” is a useful prescribing heuristic, not a universal statutory ceiling.
- **Z-drugs also act at benzodiazepine-sensitive GABA-A receptors** and still carry dependence and impairment risk.
- Benzodiazepine + opioid/alcohol = dangerous **additive respiratory depression**.
- Tapers should be **individualised**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating 2–4 weeks as a legal universal maximum.
- Assuming Z-drugs avoid tolerance, dependence or impairment.
- Abruptly stopping a physiologically dependent patient.
- Missing opioid/alcohol co-use when assessing benzodiazepine safety.
