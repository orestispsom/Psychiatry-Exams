# Q69. How do you manage borderline personality disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Assess risk and establish the therapeutic frame**
2. **Use a collaborative formulation**
3. **Make structured psychotherapy the primary treatment**
4. **Use a crisis plan and clear boundaries**
5. **Treat genuine comorbidity**
6. **Avoid medication for core BPD and chronic polypharmacy**

## Model oral answer

Borderline personality disorder is managed primarily with a **structured therapeutic relationship and evidence-based psychotherapy**, not with medication directed at the personality disorder itself. I would begin with risk assessment, a collaborative formulation, clear boundaries and a crisis plan, then offer a coherent specialist psychotherapy while treating comorbid disorders separately and reviewing unnecessary medication.

The clinical pattern typically includes instability in relationships, self-image and affect, impulsivity, recurrent self-harm or suicidality, sensitivity to abandonment, anger, and sometimes transient stress-related paranoia or dissociation. These features need to be understood longitudinally rather than interpreted as a series of unrelated crises.

Risk assessment is therefore central. I would assess current and past self-harm, suicidal intent, impulsive risk-taking, substance use, interpersonal violence or vulnerability and the circumstances that precipitate crises. At the same time, I would avoid allowing every contact to become an unstructured emergency response. A **clear therapeutic frame**, shared formulation and agreed crisis plan help maintain consistency and reduce reactive care.

The main treatment is **structured specialist psychotherapy**. **DBT, MBT, schema therapy and transference-focused psychotherapy** are established specialist approaches; no single model has to be presented as mandatory for every patient. DBT explicitly combines **acceptance and change** with behavioural skills targeting emotion regulation, distress tolerance and self-harm. MBT focuses on improving the capacity to understand one’s own and others’ mental states, particularly under interpersonal stress. The broader principle is a sustained, organised treatment that addresses emotional dysregulation, interpersonal patterns, self-harm and the patient’s goals within a consistent therapeutic relationship.

Medication should not be presented as treatment for **core BPD, affective instability or recurrent self-harm**. Chronic antipsychotic treatment and long-term polypharmacy for the personality disorder itself should be avoided. If there is a clearly diagnosed comorbid depressive, anxiety, bipolar, psychotic or other disorder, that condition should be treated on its own merits, and existing medication should be reviewed for ineffective or unnecessary prescriptions.

Very short-term crisis medication may occasionally be considered for a specific target, but only with attention to overdose and misuse risk, preferably one drug, and a clear stop or review plan. It should remain peripheral to the treatment strategy rather than becoming the default response to distress.

Long-term course should be discussed without nihilism. Symptomatic remission is common over time, but **functional recovery may lag behind symptom improvement**, and suicide risk remains clinically important. I would avoid quoting fixed remission or lifetime-suicide percentages as though they were universal prognosis figures.

Overall, good management combines a stable therapeutic frame, active risk work, structured psychotherapy, crisis planning, treatment of genuine comorbidity and deliberate avoidance of medication-centred care for the core personality disorder.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q69a. How do you distinguish BPD affective instability from bipolar disorder?

In BPD, affective shifts are often **rapid and reactive to interpersonal or environmental events** within a chronic pattern of identity and relationship dysregulation. Bipolar disorder requires **distinct sustained episodes** representing a clear departure from baseline.

I would look for episodicity, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, increased goal-directed activity and a recognisable inter-episode baseline. Duration helps but is not sufficient on its own; the distinction should integrate biological activation, baseline change, interpersonal reactivity and the longitudinal personality pattern. The two disorders can also coexist.

## Must-know board facts

- **Structured psychotherapy is primary treatment**; medication does not treat the core BPD syndrome.
- Evidence-based specialist therapies include **DBT, MBT, schema therapy and transference-focused psychotherapy**.
- **DBT:** dialectical acceptance + change with behavioural skills for emotion regulation/distress tolerance/self-harm.
- **MBT:** strengthens mentalising, especially under interpersonal stress.
- Long-term symptomatic remission is common, but functional recovery can lag; avoid fixed universal remission/suicide percentages.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating recurrent crises with escalating long-term polypharmacy.
- Prescribing an antipsychotic or mood stabiliser as though it were disease-modifying BPD treatment.
- Diagnosing bipolar disorder solely because mood shifts are intense.
- Diagnosing BPD versus bipolar disorder solely from a duration rule.

## Exam answer vs current practice

A medication- or polypharmacy-centred approach is outdated. **Structured psychotherapy is primary treatment, and medication is not treatment for the core BPD syndrome.**
