# Q27. How do you recognise and manage postpartum psychosis?

## Recall spine

1. **Psychiatric emergency, usually within 2 weeks postpartum**
2. **Fluctuating affective, psychotic and disorganised syndrome**
3. **Strong bipolar-spectrum relationship**
4. **Assess maternal and infant safety plus medical causes**
5. **Urgent inpatient treatment; MBU where available**
6. **Antipsychotic treatment ± lithium; early ECT when needed**
7. **Breastfeeding requires drug-specific assessment**
8. **Plan prophylaxis for future pregnancies**

## Model oral answer

Postpartum psychosis is a **psychiatric emergency**. It usually has an abrupt onset in the immediate postpartum period, commonly within the **first 2 weeks after childbirth**, and can deteriorate rapidly. The syndrome is often affective as well as psychotic, so I would think strongly about a bipolar-spectrum illness rather than conceptualising it simply as schizophrenia occurring after delivery.

The presentation may be highly fluctuating and can include mania or mixed symptoms, severe depression, marked insomnia, agitation, delusions or hallucinations, confusion, disorganisation and disturbed behaviour. Because the clinical picture can change quickly, collateral information from family or carers is especially important.

The first priority is **safety**. I would assess suicide risk, thoughts or behaviour that could endanger the infant, the mother’s ability to provide safe care, severe agitation, psychosis, catatonia and any confusion or delirium. At the same time, relevant obstetric, medical and substance-related causes of acute confusion or psychosis must be considered rather than assuming that every postpartum presentation is a primary psychiatric disorder.

Urgent specialist assessment is required, and **inpatient treatment is generally necessary**. Where available, a specialist **mother-and-baby unit** is preferred because maternal treatment, infant safety and the mother–infant relationship can be managed together. Obstetric and perinatal services should be involved as appropriate.

For acute treatment, an **antipsychotic** is usually required. **Lithium** should be considered or added where clinically appropriate, particularly because of the strong bipolar-spectrum relationship and its role in relapse prevention. Short-term benzodiazepines may be used when severe agitation or insomnia requires additional control, but they are not the central treatment.

**ECT should be considered early** when a rapid response is needed, especially with severe or refractory illness, catatonia, extreme agitation, or suicidal or infanticidal risk. It should not be presented as an option reserved only after prolonged failure of all other treatments.

Breastfeeding decisions require an individual **drug-specific risk–benefit assessment** that considers the mother’s relapse risk, infant exposure and the infant’s medical vulnerability. Blanket claims that psychiatric drugs are simply “safe” or “unsafe” in breastfeeding are inappropriate. Current guidance advises against breastfeeding while taking **lithium, clozapine or carbamazepine**.

Long-term management must include **relapse prevention**. Lithium has the strongest evidence for prevention after postpartum psychosis and is an important prophylactic option in women with a previous episode. A history of postpartum psychosis or bipolar I disorder confers a **high postpartum recurrence risk**, but the exact percentage varies substantially with history and treatment and should not be frozen into one board number. Future pregnancies therefore require specialist preconception planning, an explicit antenatal and postpartum relapse-prevention plan, and consideration of prophylaxis immediately after delivery in high-risk women.

## Must-know board facts

- Postpartum psychosis commonly begins abruptly within the **first 2 weeks postpartum**.
- It is strongly associated with **bipolar-spectrum illness**.
- Broad epidemiological anchor: approximately **1–2 per 1,000 births**; this is an approximate follow-up fact, not a diagnostic threshold.
- Prior postpartum psychosis or bipolar I disorder confers **high postpartum recurrence risk**; do not quote one fixed recurrence percentage.
- **Mother-and-baby unit admission** is preferred where available.
- **ECT can be an early treatment** when rapid response is required.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q27a. How does postpartum depression differ and how is it managed?

Postpartum depression is a **depressive syndrome after childbirth**, without the defining manic, psychotic or disorganised emergency picture of postpartum psychosis. It should also be distinguished from **maternity blues**, which are mild, short-lived and self-limiting; persistent clinically significant depression, functional impairment, suicidality or inability to care requires assessment for postpartum depression.

Assessment should cover severity, suicide risk, infant safety, functioning, bonding and care context, available support, bipolar history and any psychotic symptoms. Treatment broadly follows evidence-based depression care but must be adapted to previous response, breastfeeding and the maternal–infant context.

For mild or moderate presentations, facilitated self-help or structured psychotherapy may be appropriate. For moderate or severe depression, high-intensity psychotherapy, antidepressant treatment or combination treatment can be selected according to preference, previous response and risk, with specialist perinatal input where needed. Severe, psychotic, catatonic or life-threatening depression may require inpatient care and ECT.

Breastfeeding decisions should again be made **drug by drug**, not through broad class-level assumptions.

A current European development is **zuranolone**, which received EU marketing authorisation in **September 2025** for postpartum depression in adults following childbirth. It is a **14-day oral course** and may be used alone or added to another antidepressant. This is worth knowing as a current option, but EU authorisation does not establish that it is routinely available or reimbursed in Greece, and it should not displace established psychotherapy- and antidepressant-based pathways in the general answer.

**Follow-up recall spine:** depression vs blues vs psychosis → severity/safety/bipolar screen → psychotherapy/antidepressant/combination → breastfeeding/perinatal adaptation → zuranolone EU update.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling postpartum psychosis “postpartum schizophrenia.”
- Missing infant-safety assessment.
- Managing it as routine outpatient depression.
- Ignoring medical or obstetric causes of confusion and psychosis.
- Making blanket breastfeeding-safety claims.
- Quoting fixed postpartum recurrence percentages as though they apply uniformly.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Postpartum depression update:** Zuranolone is no longer a US-only development; it has EU authorisation since September 2025. That regulatory status should not be converted into a claim of confirmed Greek availability or reimbursement.
