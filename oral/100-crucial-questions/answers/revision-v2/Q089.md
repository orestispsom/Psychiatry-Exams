# Q89. What do you need to know about electroconvulsive therapy?

## Recall spine

1. **Severe depression, psychosis and catatonia**
2. **Use when rapid reliable response is needed**
3. **General anaesthesia and muscle relaxation**
4. **Unilateral versus bilateral strategy**
5. **Cognitive and short-term adverse effects**
6. **Medical/anaesthetic risk assessment**
7. **Continuation or maintenance ECT**

## Model oral answer

ECT is a **highly effective biological treatment** for severe depression, particularly psychotic depression, and for catatonia, and it is especially important when a rapid, high-probability response is required. It can also be used in selected severe or refractory mania. It is delivered under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation, with electrode placement and stimulus strategy chosen to balance efficacy against cognitive burden.

The strongest clinical indication is not simply “treatment resistance.” ECT may be appropriate when illness is severe, psychotic or catatonic, or when the clinical situation makes speed and probability of response especially important. Previous response and the consequences of waiting for slower treatments are therefore relevant to the decision.

The procedure is performed in a controlled medical setting under **general anaesthesia with muscle relaxation**. Electrode placement may be unilateral or bilateral. High-dose right-unilateral and ultrabrief strategies generally reduce cognitive burden, while bilateral or bitemporal treatment often offers faster or higher acute efficacy at the cost of greater cognitive effects. Technique is individualised rather than one approach being universally superior.

Common short-term adverse effects include **headache and transient confusion**. Cognitive adverse effects are central to counselling and include anterograde and retrograde memory impairment, including possible effects on autobiographical memory. These risks must be discussed proportionately alongside the severity of the illness being treated and the expected benefits.

ECT should not be taught through a simplistic list of absolute medical contraindications. Medical and neurological conditions generally alter **anaesthetic and procedural risk** and therefore require appropriate medical assessment, optimisation and risk management. Likewise, medication review is individualised: benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants can raise seizure threshold and lithium may increase delirium risk in some circumstances, but they are not universally “must-hold” medicines without considering the ECT/anaesthetic plan.

An important modern correction is that **adequate ECT is not defined by a minimum seizure duration such as 25–30 seconds**. Seizure quality, EEG morphology and most importantly clinical response matter more than one stopwatch threshold.

Response to an acute course does not eliminate relapse risk. **Continuation or maintenance ECT** can be considered when relapse risk is high and the individual clinical history supports it, integrated with the broader maintenance plan.

The concise viva answer is therefore: ECT is a powerful treatment for severe depression, psychotic depression and catatonia, particularly when rapid response matters; it is delivered under anaesthesia with muscle relaxation; technique balances efficacy and cognitive burden; memory effects require explicit counselling; medical illness prompts risk assessment rather than an absolute-contraindication list; and continuation treatment may be appropriate in patients at high relapse risk.

## Must-know board facts

- ECT is especially important for **severe/psychotic depression and catatonia**, particularly when rapid high-probability response is required.
- Right-unilateral high-dose/ultrabrief strategies generally reduce cognitive burden; bilateral/bitemporal treatment may achieve faster/higher acute efficacy but with greater cognitive effects.
- **Do not define an adequate ECT seizure by a minimum 25–30-second duration.**
- Medication withholding/reduction around ECT is **individualised**, not a universal benzodiazepine/anticonvulsant/lithium stop rule.
- Medical comorbidity usually changes procedural/anaesthetic risk rather than functioning as a simple absolute-contraindication list.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q89a. What about consent, incapacity and involuntary treatment in Greece?

A capacitous adult should ordinarily give **prior informed consent** to ECT. Involuntary psychiatric admission **does not itself authorise ECT**, and it does not establish global incapacity; decision-making ability must be considered for the specific treatment decision.

Greek law contains general substitute-consent and emergency-treatment provisions and a psychiatry-specific provision concerning treatment against a patient’s will in serious circumstances. However, the **ECT-specific authorisation procedure when a patient lacks capacity or urgent non-consensual ECT is contemplated remains unresolved** in the verified source set. The safe current answer is therefore to state the verified consent principles and not invent whether a court, prosecutor, judicial supporter, independent second opinion or another special safeguard is required. UK Mental Health Act procedures should not be imported into Greek practice.

### Q89b. How does rTMS compare with ECT?

**rTMS is an established non-invasive treatment for depression** and is an option in treatment-resistant depression. It is generally delivered without general anaesthesia. Seizure risk is low but real.

It should not simply be described as an “inferior ECT.” The treatments differ in procedure, burden and clinical role. ECT remains preferable when a **rapid, high-probability response** is required in severe, psychotic or catatonic illness, whereas rTMS offers a non-invasive depression treatment option without general anaesthesia.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating ECT as only a last resort after every medication has failed.
- Requiring a seizure duration of 25–30 seconds to call treatment adequate.
- Using a rigid absolute-contraindication list without anaesthetic risk formulation.
- Automatically holding every benzodiazepine, anticonvulsant or lithium dose without an individual ECT plan.
- Assuming involuntary admission itself authorises ECT in Greece.
- Importing UK ECT consent procedures into Greek law.
