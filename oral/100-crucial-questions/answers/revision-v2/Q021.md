# Q21. How do you treat a major depressive episode?

## Recall spine

1. **Reassess diagnosis, severity and immediate risk**
2. **Choose setting and treatment collaboratively**
3. **Psychotherapy, antidepressant or combination**
4. **Psychotic depression and ECT**
5. **Review response at about 4 weeks**
6. **Switch versus augment according to response**
7. **Continue treatment and prevent relapse**

## Model oral answer

Treatment starts with confirming what I am treating and how urgent the situation is. I would reassess suicide risk, psychosis, catatonia or severe psychomotor retardation, self-neglect, nutrition and hydration, substance use, relevant medical causes, previous treatment response, adherence and available support. The treatment setting depends on safety and the patient’s ability to remain safely managed, not on the diagnosis alone.

The next step is **shared treatment selection**. Severity is important, but current practice is not a rigid ladder in which mild depression always means psychotherapy and severe depression automatically means combination treatment. Previous response, comorbidity, adverse-effect priorities, patient preference and treatment availability all influence the choice.

In **less-severe depression**, evidence-based psychological or behavioural interventions are often preferred initially, and an antidepressant is not automatically required unless the patient prefers medication or there is another clinical reason to use it. In **more-severe depression**, structured psychotherapy and antidepressant medication are both legitimate first-line approaches, and combination treatment becomes especially attractive when illness is more severe or chronic, safety risk is higher, or there has been incomplete response to one modality. CBT is a high-yield example of an evidence-based structured psychological treatment; psychotherapy should not be presented as merely an adjunct to medication.

When medication is chosen, the antidepressant should be individualised according to previous benefit, tolerability, overdose toxicity, interactions, comorbidity and patient preference rather than selected from a fixed universal hierarchy.

**Psychotic depression** requires specialist management. The high-yield pharmacological approach is an antidepressant combined with an antipsychotic; if the patient declines an antipsychotic, antidepressant monotherapy can still be considered, but combination treatment is generally the stronger answer. **ECT** should be considered when a rapid response is needed because the depression is life-threatening, for example with refusal of food or fluids, or when severe psychotic or catatonic features make rapid treatment important. It is also appropriate after treatment failure or when there has been a strong previous response or clear patient preference; it should not be described only as a remote last resort.

If there is no or only limited improvement, I would review the treatment at about **4 weeks at a recognised therapeutic dose**. Before changing strategy, I would check adherence, tolerability, diagnosis, psychosocial and medical contributors, and whether the treatment was actually adequate. Four weeks is a reassessment point, not a definition of treatment-resistant depression.

After an inadequate trial, a **switch** is more logical when there has been essentially no benefit or tolerability is poor. **Augmentation or addition** is more attractive when there has been meaningful partial response to a well-tolerated antidepressant. Psychological treatment can also be added or changed.

Once remission is achieved, effective treatment should be **continued rather than stopped abruptly**. A traditional continuation anchor is about **6 months after remission**, but this should not be turned into a universal stopping rule. Longer relapse-prevention treatment is increasingly justified as prior episodes, severity, residual symptoms, comorbidity or other relapse risks accumulate. Discontinuation should be gradual and accompanied by relapse planning and follow-up.

## Must-know board facts

- **Response review:** about **4 weeks at a therapeutic dose** is a useful checkpoint for inadequate antidepressant response; it does not by itself define TRD.
- **Continuation:** treatment continues after remission. Around **6 months** is a useful traditional/minimum anchor, while longer-term maintenance is individualised according to relapse risk rather than a fixed episode-count rule.
- **Recurrence:** risk rises with previous episodes and residual symptoms; do not memorise the old 50/70/90 recurrence table as current truth.
- **CBT mechanism:** identifies and tests maladaptive appraisals and behavioural patterns that maintain depression.
- **Behavioural activation:** reverses withdrawal and avoidance by planned re-engagement with reinforcing, goal-directed activity.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Starting treatment without checking bipolarity, psychosis or suicide risk.
- Waiting passively despite no response or important tolerability problems.
- Calling 4-week non-response “treatment resistance.”
- Treating ECT as an absolute last resort in life-threatening, psychotic or catatonic depression.
- Stopping antidepressant treatment immediately after remission.
- Quoting fixed recurrence percentages or a universal maintenance duration.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Treatment selection:** Older teaching may use a rigid severity-based stepped ladder. Current practice is more individualised: severity matters, but psychotherapy and medication can both be first-line in appropriate patients, with risk, prior response, preference and access shaping the choice.
