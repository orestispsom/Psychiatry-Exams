# Q39. How do you manage alcohol use disorder after withdrawal?

## Recall spine

1. **Define the treatment goal**
2. **Build structured psychosocial relapse prevention**
3. **Use acamprosate or naltrexone when appropriate**
4. **Reserve disulfiram and nalmefene for selected situations**
5. **Monitor recovery, adherence and physical health**
6. **Continue treatment long enough to consolidate change**

## Model oral answer

Detoxification is only the beginning of treatment for alcohol use disorder. After withdrawal, I would agree a clear goal—usually abstinence or reduction—then combine structured psychosocial relapse-prevention work with pharmacotherapy when appropriate, matched to medical status, opioid use, adherence and previous response.

The psychosocial plan should be explicit rather than generic advice to “stay sober.” Useful approaches include **motivational work, CBT and other behavioural strategies**, together with social-network or family involvement when appropriate. Mutual-aid groups can also be useful for some patients. The focus should be on identifying triggers, strengthening coping strategies, planning for high-risk situations and improving the social conditions that support recovery.

The medication strategy depends on the goal and the individual patient. **Acamprosate and oral naltrexone are the principal relapse-prevention medicines after withdrawal.** Acamprosate modulates **glutamatergic and GABAergic signalling**, although its precise mechanism is not fully settled. Naltrexone is an **opioid-receptor antagonist, mainly at the μ receptor clinically**, and reduces opioid-mediated reward signalling. The choice should consider liver and renal status, current or foreseeable opioid use, adherence, previous benefit and patient preference.

Naltrexone must **not be initiated in current opioid use or dependence**, because opioid antagonism can precipitate withdrawal, and it will also block opioid analgesia. Hepatic contraindications and monitoring should follow current product information rather than describing naltrexone simply as a universally “hepatotoxic” drug.

**Disulfiram** is more selective. It inhibits **aldehyde dehydrogenase**, so alcohol consumption causes acetaldehyde accumulation and an aversive reaction. It may be considered when abstinence is the agreed goal and first-choice options are unsuitable, not tolerated, ineffective or when the patient specifically prefers it. Its effectiveness depends heavily on adherence, so supervision can be useful, and relevant contraindications and the safety implications of drinking while taking it need to be addressed.

**Nalmefene** is different again because it is reduction-oriented rather than primarily an abstinence medication. It is a licensing- and jurisdiction-specific option for selected adults with high drinking-risk levels who do not require immediate detoxification. It should not simply be inserted into a universal post-withdrawal treatment ladder.

Duration also needs to be practical rather than rigid. About **6 months** is a useful treatment anchor for acamprosate or naltrexone in the existing treatment framework, but this is not an automatic stopping date. Continued treatment should be individualised according to benefit, relapse risk, adherence, adverse effects and the patient’s progress.

Long-term follow-up should include more than drinking quantity. I would monitor **craving, lapses or relapse, psychiatric comorbidity, physical complications, social and occupational recovery, adherence and the patient’s own goals**. If relapse occurs, the response should be to reassess what is maintaining the disorder and strengthen treatment rather than treating it as proof that treatment has failed.

The core principle is that alcohol withdrawal management removes acute physiological risk, whereas recovery from alcohol use disorder requires a sustained relapse-prevention plan combining psychosocial treatment, appropriately selected medication and longitudinal follow-up.

## Must-know board facts

- **Withdrawal treatment alone is not treatment of alcohol use disorder.**
- **Acamprosate:** modulates glutamatergic/GABAergic signalling; the precise mechanism is not fully settled, so do not reduce it to a single-receptor NMDA-antagonist rule.
- **Naltrexone:** opioid-receptor antagonist, mainly **μ** clinically. Do not initiate in current opioid use/dependence because it can precipitate withdrawal and it blocks opioid analgesia.
- **Disulfiram:** inhibits **aldehyde dehydrogenase**, causing acetaldehyde accumulation with alcohol; this is an aversive-treatment mechanism, not treatment of withdrawal.
- **Nalmefene:** selected reduction-oriented option whose place depends on licensing and jurisdiction; it is not a universal post-detoxification step.
- Pharmacotherapy for relapse prevention should usually be combined with structured psychosocial care.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q39a. How do you choose between acamprosate, naltrexone and disulfiram?

I would start with the **treatment goal and contraindication profile**. Acamprosate is an abstinence-maintenance option and is particularly useful when opioid antagonism is undesirable. Naltrexone is useful when reducing reward-driven drinking or heavy drinking is a priority, but current opioid use or dependence is a major exclusion because of precipitated withdrawal and blockade of opioid analgesia. Disulfiram is a selected abstinence-oriented aversive treatment whose effectiveness depends heavily on adherence and often benefits from supervision. The choice should therefore follow goal, physical health, opioid exposure, adherence likelihood, previous response and patient preference rather than a simple efficacy ranking.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Listing relapse-prevention drugs without matching them to treatment goal and contraindications.
- Calling acamprosate simply an NMDA antagonist as though its mechanism were settled.
- Calling naltrexone generically “hepatotoxic” while forgetting the immediate opioid-related safety issue.
- Starting naltrexone in someone currently opioid-dependent.
- Treating disulfiram as routine first-line medication.
- Presenting nalmefene as a universal treatment step.
- Treating a lapse as automatic treatment failure rather than reassessing maintaining factors.
