# Q34. What are the main dissociative disorders and how do you assess them?

## Recall spine

1. **Depersonalisation and derealisation**
2. **Dissociative amnesia and fugue qualifier**
3. **Identity disruption**
4. **Remember dissociative neurological symptoms in ICD-11**
5. **Assess phenomenology, course and collateral**
6. **Exclude neurological, substance and other psychiatric explanations**

## Model oral answer

Dissociation refers to disruptions in the normal integration of experience, including the sense of self, surroundings, autobiographical memory, identity and, in current ICD-11, some neurological-type symptoms. The assessment should be **phenomenological and differential-diagnostic**: describe exactly what is disrupted, establish the course and context, and actively consider neurological, substance-related, sleep and other psychiatric explanations. Normal investigations alone do not establish a dissociative diagnosis.

**Depersonalisation** is a sense of detachment or unreality concerning the self—for example feeling estranged from one’s thoughts, body or actions. **Derealisation** is a sense that the external world or surroundings are unreal or detached. An important distinction from psychosis is that **reality testing is generally preserved**: the patient experiences the unreality but does not ordinarily lose the understanding that this is a subjective alteration in experience.

**Dissociative amnesia** involves inability to recall important autobiographical information in a way that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting, after relevant alternative explanations have been considered. **Dissociative fugue** is not a separate disorder in ICD-11; it is used as a qualifier of dissociative amnesia.

**Dissociative identity disorder** belongs in the adult differential when there is clinically significant disruption or discontinuity of identity associated with memory or agency phenomena. Trauma and childhood maltreatment are strongly associated with DID in clinical literature, but trauma is not a necessary diagnostic criterion and the association should not be converted into a simple proven causal rule. The diagnosis should not be made from a single unusual experience or from suggestion; the task is to establish the longitudinal pattern and impairment.

Current ICD-11 also includes **dissociative neurological symptom disorder** within the dissociative grouping. This differs from DSM-5-TR, where functional neurological disorder is placed under Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders. The classification difference should not obscure the clinical principle that neurological-type symptoms require an appropriate neurological assessment and a positive diagnostic formulation rather than a conclusion based only on normal tests.

Assessment should cover the exact phenomenology, onset and course, precipitating and maintaining context where relevant, and functional consequences. Collateral information can be important, particularly when memory or identity continuity is in question. I would perform a mental-state examination, review substances and medications, and consider trauma history when clinically relevant without assuming that trauma must be identified before a dissociative disorder can be diagnosed.

The differential includes **epilepsy or syncope, delirium, sleep disorders, PTSD and other psychiatric illness**, as well as substance or medication effects. Neurological assessment and targeted investigation should follow the presentation. The overall aim is therefore to define the dissociative phenomenon positively, preserve the distinction from psychosis, and avoid diagnosing dissociation merely because standard investigations are unrevealing.

## Must-know board facts

- **Depersonalisation/derealisation:** reality testing is generally preserved.
- **Dissociative fugue:** an ICD-11 qualifier of dissociative amnesia, not a separate disorder.
- **DID and trauma:** trauma/maltreatment are strongly associated in clinical literature, but trauma is not a necessary diagnostic criterion and should not be taught as a simple deterministic cause.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q34a. What organic disorders are particularly important when the complaint is amnesia or episodic altered experience?

Two high-yield neurological differentials are **transient global amnesia** and **temporal-lobe epilepsy**. TGA is an acute neurological amnestic syndrome whose episode duration is characteristically **less than 24 hours**; that is a neurological diagnostic anchor, not a general rule for dissociative amnesia. Temporal-lobe epilepsy may produce stereotyped experiential, affective or altered-awareness phenomena. The practical point is that episodic amnesia or altered experience requires a neurological differential rather than a dissociative label based on negative routine tests.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Confusing depersonalisation with psychosis.
- Diagnosing dissociation because scans or EEG are normal.
- Assuming trauma must be identified before diagnosis.
- Treating trauma association in DID as a necessary causal criterion.
- Missing transient global amnesia or temporal-lobe epilepsy in an amnestic/episodic presentation.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification:** ICD-11 includes dissociative neurological symptom disorder in the dissociative grouping; DSM-5-TR places FND under Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders.
