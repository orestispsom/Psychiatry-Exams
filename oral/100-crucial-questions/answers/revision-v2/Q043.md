# Q43. How do you manage benzodiazepine dependence and withdrawal?

## Recall spine

1. **Do not stop abruptly**
2. **Assess withdrawal and complication risk**
3. **Use a slow, individualised taper**
4. **Pause or slow when symptoms become problematic**
5. **Consider longer-acting substitution selectively**
6. **Treat underlying anxiety or insomnia in parallel**
7. **Remember flumazenil can be dangerous in dependence**

## Model oral answer

Benzodiazepine dependence should usually be managed with a **collaborative, gradual and individualised taper**, because abrupt cessation can cause severe withdrawal including seizures and delirium. I would first assess the current dose and duration of use, previous withdrawal experiences, comorbidity and factors that increase medical or psychiatric risk, then agree a taper that is adjusted according to symptoms rather than forcing the patient through a fixed schedule.

The first principle is **not to stop suddenly**, even when the current dose appears modest, if physiological dependence is likely. Withdrawal severity is influenced by the individual pattern of use and vulnerability, so I would ask about previous difficult tapers, seizures or delirium, concurrent alcohol or other sedative use, major medical illness and psychiatric instability. Complicated withdrawal or substantial medical or psychiatric risk may justify inpatient or residential management.

A useful current starting approach is a reduction of about **5–10% every 2–4 weeks**, generally avoiding reductions greater than **25% every 2 weeks**. These are ASAM-style starting principles rather than a prescription for every patient. Long-term users, patients taking higher doses or people who are particularly sensitive to withdrawal may require much slower reductions.

The taper should be **symptom-responsive**. If withdrawal becomes clinically problematic, I would pause the taper or make subsequent reductions smaller and slower rather than assuming that distress means the patient has failed treatment. The aim is safe progressive reduction while maintaining engagement and function.

Some patients may benefit from switching to **diazepam or another longer-acting benzodiazepine** before or during tapering, but this is optional rather than mandatory. The decision depends on the current drug, dosing pattern, patient preference and medical factors. In hepatic impairment, a long-acting conversion may be less attractive; lorazepam or oxazepam, or remaining with a shorter-acting agent, may be more appropriate. Equivalence tables can guide conversion but should not be treated as exact biological truth.

Management should also address **why the benzodiazepine is being used**. Anxiety, insomnia and other psychiatric symptoms need active evidence-based treatment so that tapering is not experienced simply as removal of the patient’s only coping strategy. CBT, CBT for insomnia and other relevant behavioural support can improve the overall deprescribing plan.

A related toxicology point is **flumazenil**. Although it antagonises the benzodiazepine receptor site, it can precipitate severe withdrawal and seizures in a chronically benzodiazepine-dependent patient and can be hazardous in mixed pro-convulsant overdoses. Its use therefore requires a selected specialist indication rather than routine reversal of sedation in dependent patients.

## Must-know board facts

- **ASAM 2025 starting taper principle:** about **5–10% reduction every 2–4 weeks**, generally not exceeding **25% every 2 weeks**; slow or pause as required.
- Modern tapering is **symptom-responsive**, not rigid schedule-following.
- **Diazepam substitution is optional**, not a requirement for every taper.
- Benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause **seizures and delirium**.
- **Flumazenil** can precipitate severe withdrawal/seizures in chronic benzodiazepine dependence or mixed pro-convulsant overdose; it is not a routine reversal strategy in that setting.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Abruptly stopping because the current dose appears low.
- Treating a taper percentage as a mandatory fixed schedule.
- Treating equivalence tables as precise biological conversions.
- Assuming every patient must convert to diazepam.
- Continuing a fixed taper despite clinically significant withdrawal.
- Giving flumazenil casually to a chronically dependent patient.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Tapering schedules:** Classic Ashton-style or fixed fractional schedules are useful historical templates, but they should not be taught as universal current standards. Current tapering is individualised and adjusted according to withdrawal symptoms and clinical risk.
