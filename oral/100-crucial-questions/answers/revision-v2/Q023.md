# Q23. How do you diagnose bipolar disorder and distinguish it from important alternatives?

## Recall spine

1. **Establish an episodic change from baseline**
2. **Define the manic/hypomanic syndrome**
3. **Separate mania from hypomania by severity and consequence**
4. **Classify bipolar I versus bipolar II**
5. **Look for mixed features**
6. **Differentiate unipolar depression, ADHD, BPD and psychosis**
7. **Exclude substance/medication and medical causes**
8. **Interpret antidepressant-emergent syndromes carefully**

## Model oral answer

Bipolar disorder is a **longitudinal, episodic diagnosis**. I would not diagnose it from vague “mood swings”; I would establish whether there have been distinct periods of altered mood and increased energy or activity representing a clear departure from the person’s baseline, and then determine whether those episodes meet the clinical threshold for mania or hypomania.

A manic-spectrum episode typically involves elevated, expansive or irritable mood together with increased energy or activity. High-yield associated features include reduced need for sleep, increased or pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, disinhibition or risk-taking and grandiosity. Psychotic symptoms may occur in mania.

The key distinction between **mania and hypomania is severity and functional consequence**, not simply counting symptoms. Mania causes marked impairment, may require hospitalisation and may include psychosis. Hypomania is still a clear episodic change from baseline, but without the same degree of marked impairment or psychosis.

The current classification architecture is straightforward at the highest level. **Bipolar I disorder requires at least one manic episode. Bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one depressive episode, with no history of mania.** Mixed features are also important: clinically significant manic symptoms may occur during depression, and depressive symptoms may occur during mania or hypomania, without requiring the old idea of a fully simultaneous mixed episode. Their presence makes diagnosis more complex and increases caution with antidepressants.

The differential is mainly resolved through chronology and baseline change. In **unipolar depression**, I would actively search for any lifetime hypomania or mania, mixed symptoms, family history, collateral evidence and treatment-emergent activation; no particular depressive phenotype proves bipolarity on its own. **ADHD** is usually trait-like and persistent from development, whereas hypomania or mania is episodic. A clear shift in mood and energy, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity or psychosis favours bipolar disorder, although ADHD and bipolar disorder can coexist.

With **borderline personality affective instability**, mood shifts are often rapid and reactive to interpersonal or environmental events and occur within a broader pervasive pattern of identity, relationship and impulsivity difficulties. Bipolar disorder requires sustained episodes representing a distinct change from baseline; the two conditions can nevertheless coexist.

I would also consider substances, medications and medical causes, including stimulants, corticosteroids, antidepressants, intoxication or withdrawal, and thyroid, neurological or other medical disorders according to the presentation. A schizophrenia-spectrum illness becomes more likely when persistent psychosis is not organised around mood episodes, again making longitudinal chronology essential.

Finally, antidepressant-emergent mania or hypomania should not be interpreted mechanically. A full syndrome that **persists beyond discontinuation and beyond the direct physiological effects of the antidepressant** may count toward a primary bipolar diagnosis. Brief activation confined to the direct drug effect does not automatically establish bipolar disorder.

## Must-know board facts

- **Bipolar I:** at least one manic episode.
- **Bipolar II:** hypomania plus a depressive episode, with no history of mania.
- **DSM duration anchors:** mania at least **7 days**, or any duration if hospitalisation is required; hypomania at least **4 consecutive days**. These are DSM anchors, not universal ICD-11 wording; ICD-11 uses different duration framing.
- **DSM rapid-cycling specifier:** at least **4 mood episodes in 12 months**.
- Bipolar disorder is **highly familial and polygenic**, but exact heritability or first-degree-relative risk percentages should not be used as individual predictions.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing bipolar disorder from ordinary mood variability.
- Calling chronic ADHD activation hypomania.
- Calling reactive borderline affective shifts bipolar episodes.
- Treating DSM duration wording as universal ICD-11 wording.
- Assuming any antidepressant activation proves bipolar disorder.
- Quoting fixed heritability or family-risk percentages as though they determine an individual patient’s risk.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification update:** Current ICD-11 explicitly distinguishes bipolar I and bipolar II. Older ICD-10-era formulations are therefore outdated.

**Antidepressant-emergent episodes:** A full manic or hypomanic syndrome that persists beyond direct antidepressant effects can count toward a primary bipolar diagnosis; it is too simplistic to classify every antidepressant-associated episode as substance-induced.
