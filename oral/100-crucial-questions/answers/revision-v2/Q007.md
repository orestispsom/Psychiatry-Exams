# Q07. How do you manage severe agitation in the emergency setting?

## Recall spine

1. **Immediate safety and medical stability**
2. **Search for the cause**
3. **Verbal and environmental de-escalation**
4. **Least-restrictive containment**
5. **Rapid tranquillisation only when required**
6. **Close physiological monitoring**
7. **Post-incident review**

## Model oral answer

Severe agitation is a **symptom and an emergency-management problem, not a diagnosis**. I would first secure immediate safety while simultaneously assessing for a medical or toxic cause, use verbal and environmental de-escalation whenever feasible, and move to restrictive measures or rapid tranquillisation only when the clinical situation requires it.

The first step is to assess **immediate danger, staffing and environment**, whether the patient or others are at risk, and whether there is medical instability requiring urgent treatment. At the same time I would consider delirium, intoxication or withdrawal, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, head injury, seizure or post-ictal state, infection, metabolic disturbance, medication toxicity, mania, psychosis and other behavioural causes. Sedating an agitated patient must not replace diagnosing the cause.

If it can be done safely, **de-escalation is first-line**. I would use calm verbal and non-verbal communication, avoid provocation, reduce stimulation, maintain appropriate personal space and move to a quieter environment. The approach should preserve dignity and maximise the patient’s sense of control.

Any **restrictive intervention** should be a last-resort, least-restrictive measure used for the shortest necessary time, with physical safety and dignity maintained. The exact legal and procedural requirements for restraint or seclusion should follow the applicable local framework rather than being improvised.

If urgent sedation is required and oral medication is not possible or appropriate, **rapid tranquillisation** means urgent parenteral medication for emergency containment. It is not routine treatment of schizophrenia or mania. Current adult options include **IM lorazepam alone** or **IM haloperidol combined with IM promethazine**. When information is insufficient, the patient is antipsychotic-naïve, there is cardiovascular or QT concern, or no ECG is available, lorazepam is favoured. Exact doses should follow the current local protocol and product information.

The initial medication should be given as a **single dose**, and I would not repeat it until the clinical effect has been reviewed. After rapid tranquillisation, monitoring is part of the treatment: adverse effects, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, hydration and level of consciousness should be checked at least hourly. **Fifteen-minute observations** are appropriate in higher-risk circumstances such as significant sedation or sleep, alcohol or illicit-drug use, physical-health problems, injury during restriction or concerns around maximum dosing.

Once the immediate crisis has settled, I would perform a **post-incident review**: what precipitated the agitation, which de-escalation strategies were attempted, whether there were injuries or medication adverse effects, and what should change in the patient’s future risk-management plan.

## Must-know board facts

- Rapid tranquillisation is a **separate emergency intervention**, not routine antipsychotic treatment.
- Current adult options: **IM lorazepam** or **IM haloperidol + IM promethazine**. Exact dosing should follow current local protocol and product information.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Sedating before considering delirium or another medical cause.
- Repeating medication before assessing the effect of the first dose.
- Giving unverified exact doses as though they were universal.
- Using restraint as punishment or routine behavioural control.
