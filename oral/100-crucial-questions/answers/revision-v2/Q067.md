# Q67. What are the main sleep stages and their EEG characteristics?

## Recall spine

1. **N1 — theta / transition**
2. **N2 — spindles + K-complexes**
3. **N3 — high-amplitude slow-wave sleep**
4. **REM — low-voltage mixed frequency + atonia**
5. **NREM–REM cycling across the night**
6. **Slow-wave sleep earlier; REM later**

## Model oral answer

Normal sleep is organised into **NREM stages N1, N2 and N3, alternating with REM sleep** in repeated cycles through the night. The high-yield board task is to recognise each stage from its EEG and physiological pattern rather than memorising one rigid percentage distribution.

**N1** is light transitional sleep. The waking alpha rhythm attenuates and the EEG becomes predominantly **theta-frequency activity**; vertex sharp waves may appear. It is the lightest NREM stage and represents the transition from wakefulness into established sleep.

**N2** is identified by **sleep spindles and K-complexes** on a background of lower-voltage mixed activity. Spindles are generated by thalamocortical circuitry and are one of the defining electrophysiological markers of this stage. N2 usually occupies the largest share of adult sleep.

**N3** is slow-wave sleep, characterised by high-amplitude **slow delta activity**. It is deepest early NREM sleep, tends to be concentrated more heavily in the first part of the night, and is associated with the highest arousal threshold. Exact frequency/amplitude scoring conventions are technical sleep-laboratory criteria rather than necessary clinical diagnostic rules for a general psychiatry viva.

**REM sleep** has a relatively **low-voltage mixed-frequency, desynchronised EEG**, rapid eye movements and marked skeletal-muscle atonia apart from expected exceptions such as the diaphragm and brief twitches. Dreaming can occur in other stages, but vivid dream recall is classically associated with REM. REM periods become longer and more prominent toward the latter part of the night.

Across the night, NREM and REM recur cyclically. Classic teaching often quotes roughly **90–110-minute cycles**, with several cycles per night, but cycle length and stage proportions vary with age, the individual, the night and the scoring context. The reliable qualitative pattern is that **slow-wave sleep is weighted toward the first third of the night and REM toward the final third**.

The practical viva answer is therefore: N1 theta, N2 spindles and K-complexes, N3 high-amplitude slow waves, REM low-voltage mixed-frequency EEG with rapid eye movements and atonia, all organised in repeated NREM–REM cycles.

## Must-know board facts

- **N1:** predominantly theta activity; vertex sharp waves may occur.
- **N2:** **sleep spindles + K-complexes**.
- **N3:** high-amplitude **slow delta activity**.
- **REM:** low-voltage mixed-frequency/desynchronised EEG + rapid eye movements + skeletal-muscle atonia.
- Slow-wave sleep is concentrated earlier in the night; REM becomes more prominent later.

## Historical / classic exam physiology

**HISTORICAL_EXAM_FACT:** Classic adult sleep teaching often gives approximate proportions of **N1 ~5%, N2 ~45–50%, N3 ~15–20%, REM ~20–25%**, with cycles around **90–110 minutes** and roughly **4–6 cycles per night**. These are useful orientation figures, not fixed physiological or diagnostic truths; age and night-to-night variation matter.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q67a. What sleep-architecture changes are classically associated with depression?

Classic group-level findings are **shortened REM latency, increased REM density and reduced slow-wave sleep**. These are useful biological-psychiatry and traditional examination associations, but they are **not diagnostic tests for depression**, and an exact REM-latency cut-off should not be treated as a universal clinical rule.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Confusing N2 spindles/K-complexes with N3 slow waves.
- Forgetting REM muscle atonia.
- Treating classic sleep-stage percentages as invariant normal values.
- Using shortened REM latency as a diagnostic test for depression.
