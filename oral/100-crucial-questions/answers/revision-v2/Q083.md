# Q83. What do you need to know about lamotrigine in bipolar disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Bipolar depression and maintenance**
2. **Not an acute antimanic treatment**
3. **Rash, SJS and TEN**
4. **Slow titration and restart rules**
5. **Valproate and enzyme-inducer interactions**
6. **Hormonal contraception interaction**
7. **Pregnancy and postpartum level changes**

## Model oral answer

Lamotrigine is mainly useful in **bipolar depression and maintenance**, not as an acute antimanic treatment. Safe prescribing centres on slow titration because of serious rash risk, recognising the major interaction with valproate, and anticipating changes in exposure with enzyme inducers, estrogen-containing contraception and pregnancy. I would not improvise a titration schedule unless the specific regimen had been verified.

The major safety issue is **rash**, including the rare but serious syndromes Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis. Lamotrigine therefore has to be introduced slowly, and a concerning rash requires urgent clinical assessment rather than reassurance based only on its appearance or timing.

The most important pharmacokinetic interaction is with **valproate**. Valproate inhibits lamotrigine glucuronidation, increasing lamotrigine exposure. This means lamotrigine must be introduced more slowly and at a lower regimen when the two are combined. The exact titration follows the SmPC rather than a universal percentage dose rule. Conversely, enzyme-inducing drugs can lower lamotrigine exposure and may reduce effectiveness.

A crucial restart rule is also easy to miss. If lamotrigine has been interrupted for long enough that the interval exceeds roughly **five lamotrigine half-lives**, it should be restarted using the **initial escalation schedule** rather than resumed at the previous full dose. The calendar interval is not fixed because lamotrigine half-life changes substantially with interacting drugs such as valproate or enzyme inducers.

Hormonal contraception is another important interaction. **Estrogen-containing combined hormonal contraception can approximately double lamotrigine clearance.** Starting or stopping such contraception can therefore materially alter lamotrigine exposure, so the medication plan needs review when contraceptive status changes rather than assuming a stable dose will behave identically.

Pregnancy also changes lamotrigine pharmacokinetics. Concentrations may **fall during pregnancy** and then **rise rapidly postpartum**. This makes clinical and concentration monitoring particularly relevant around pregnancy when lamotrigine is continued, with dose decisions guided by the individual patient rather than a universal rule.

In routine follow-up I would assess mood response, adherence, rash or other adverse effects, interacting medication and reproductive context. If the patient becomes pregnant, starts or stops estrogen-containing contraception, or valproate is added or withdrawn, I would treat that as a clinically significant change rather than simply continuing the prior regimen unchanged.

The high-yield viva summary is therefore: lamotrigine belongs mainly to the bipolar-depression and maintenance side of treatment, not acute mania; serious rash risk makes slow titration essential; valproate raises its exposure, enzyme inducers lower it, estrogen-containing contraception can markedly increase clearance, and interruption beyond about five half-lives requires re-titration.

## Must-know board facts

- Lamotrigine is important for **bipolar depression/maintenance**, not acute mania.
- **Valproate inhibits lamotrigine glucuronidation**; follow the exact SmPC titration rather than applying a simple percentage rule.
- After interruption exceeding approximately **5 lamotrigine half-lives**, restart the initial escalation schedule.
- Do **not** translate that into a fixed “3–5 day” rule: interacting drugs change the half-life.
- Estrogen-containing combined hormonal contraception can approximately **double lamotrigine clearance**.
- Lamotrigine concentrations may **fall in pregnancy and rise rapidly postpartum**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Presenting lamotrigine as an acute antimanic drug.
- Ignoring the valproate interaction.
- Restarting the previous full dose after a clinically significant interruption.
- Memorising “3–5 days” instead of the **five-half-life** principle.
- Inventing an exact titration schedule that has not been supplied.
