# Q55. What psychiatric and behavioural consequences can follow traumatic brain injury?

## Recall spine

1. **Acute delirium and post-traumatic amnesia**
2. **Persistent cognitive and executive deficits**
3. **Depression, irritability, aggression and lability**
4. **Apathy, disinhibition and personality-behaviour change**
5. **Suicide and substance-use risk**
6. **Rehabilitation, environment and family**
7. **Cautious symptom-targeted pharmacotherapy**

## Model oral answer

Traumatic brain injury can produce an acute confusional state and post-traumatic amnesia, followed by a broad range of persistent cognitive, emotional and behavioural syndromes. The psychiatric assessment should link symptoms to the injury, neurological recovery, frontal-executive function and the patient’s pre-injury vulnerabilities, while management remains rehabilitation-oriented rather than relying on a single medication algorithm.

Early after injury, **delirium and post-traumatic amnesia, or PTA**, are central. PTA remains clinically important for judging injury severity, prognosis and rehabilitation planning, but exact older duration bins such as “mild <24 hours, moderate 1–7 days, severe >7 days” should not be presented as one universal contemporary classification. Modern severity assessment integrates PTA with other information such as **GCS, loss of consciousness and imaging**, and thresholds vary across systems.

Longer-term problems include **cognitive and executive dysfunction**, depression, irritability and aggression, emotional lability, apathy or inertia, disinhibition and acquired personality or behavioural change. Substance misuse may complicate recovery, and suicide risk should be assessed explicitly.

Frontal-executive dysfunction can be clinically useful in understanding the pattern. **Orbitofrontal dysfunction** may be associated with impulsivity and disinhibition, **dorsolateral prefrontal dysfunction** with executive problems, and **anterior cingulate/medial frontal dysfunction** with apathy and poor initiation. These are high-yield network associations rather than rigid lesion-to-symptom rules.

Assessment should therefore include the original injury and course of PTA, current neurological and medical status, cognition and executive function, mood, aggression or impulsivity, substance use, suicide risk, medication effects, family or carer burden and the person’s functional goals. Acquired behavioural change should not be mislabeled as a pre-existing personality disorder without considering the effects of brain injury.

During **PTA or acute agitation**, management should emphasise a low-stimulation environment, orientation, sleep regulation and rehabilitation principles. Longer-term care is multimodal: neurorehabilitation, occupational and cognitive interventions, behavioural approaches, family education and practical support should be combined according to the deficits and goals.

Pharmacotherapy, when needed, should be **symptom-targeted**. The evidence for specific drugs in post-TBI agitation is heterogeneous, so there is no single universal algorithm to recite. Psychotropics should be used cautiously because sedation, anticholinergic burden, cognitive adverse effects and effects on seizure threshold may be more consequential in this population.

Apathy deserves particular care because reduced initiation after TBI may reflect frontal-subcortical dysfunction rather than depression. Similarly, aggression, impulsivity or lability should be understood within the neurological and environmental context rather than treated as isolated psychiatric symptoms.

## Must-know board facts

- **PTA is an important severity/prognostic marker**, but do not use one universal duration-bin table or call it the sole best predictor.
- Contemporary TBI severity assessment integrates **PTA with GCS, loss of consciousness and imaging** rather than one metric alone.
- **Orbitofrontal → disinhibition/impulsivity; DLPFC → executive dysfunction; anterior cingulate/medial frontal → apathy/poor initiation** are useful neuropsychiatric associations, not deterministic lesion rules.
- Acute agitation during PTA is managed primarily with **environmental and rehabilitative strategies**.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q55a. How do you distinguish post-TBI apathy from depression?

Apathy is mainly a reduction in **goal-directed initiation and motivation**, often with little subjective sadness, guilt or hopelessness. Depression is supported by a broader affective-cognitive syndrome including persistent low mood, negative self-evaluation, guilt, hopelessness and suicidality. They can coexist, and anhedonia alone does not reliably distinguish them.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing a primary personality disorder without considering acquired post-TBI behavioural change.
- Equating all low motivation after TBI with depression.
- Reciting rigid PTA duration bins as universal modern classification.
- Calling PTA the single definitive predictor of outcome.
- Treating frontal associations as exact one-lesion/one-symptom maps.
- Reciting a rigid universal drug algorithm for agitation.
