# Q46. How do you assess a patient with suspected major neurocognitive disorder / dementia?

## Recall spine

1. **History plus collateral**
2. **Define onset, trajectory and cognitive pattern**
3. **Establish functional loss with ADLs/IADLs**
4. **Exclude delirium, depression and secondary causes**
5. **Examine and use targeted investigations**
6. **Structural imaging for cause/subtype**
7. **Use specialist biomarkers only when they change management**

## Model oral answer

A dementia assessment should establish **whether there is acquired cognitive decline, whether it has caused loss of independence, what the trajectory suggests about aetiology, and whether there are reversible or contributing causes**. The diagnosis cannot be made from a cognitive score alone. I would combine patient history, collateral history, functional assessment, mental and neurological examination, targeted investigations and structural imaging.

I would first clarify the **onset and course**: insidious versus abrupt, progressive versus fluctuating, and whether there were stepwise or post-stroke changes. Collateral is often essential because insight may be limited and the patient may underestimate functional decline. I would organise cognition across attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor or visuospatial function, and social cognition, while also asking about behavioural or neuropsychiatric symptoms.

The critical diagnostic step is to establish **functional impact**. I would assess basic activities of daily living but pay particular attention to instrumental activities such as medication management, finances, cooking, transport and other complex tasks. **Interference with independent everyday functioning** is the high-yield distinction between dementia/major neurocognitive disorder and milder cognitive impairment.

I would actively look for **alternative or contributing explanations**. Delirium must be excluded when there is acute fluctuation or inattention, and a major depressive syndrome may mimic or worsen cognitive complaints. Medication burden, especially anticholinergic effects, alcohol or other substances, and medical or neurological illness should be reviewed. Physical and neurological examination may reveal focal signs, parkinsonism, gait disorder or other clues to subtype or secondary cause.

A validated brief cognitive test is useful, but a **normal brief score does not exclude dementia**, particularly when the history and function are concerning. MoCA, MMSE, ACE-III and similar instruments are screening or descriptive tools whose performance varies with age, education, language, culture and setting. I would not diagnose or exclude a major neurocognitive disorder from a single cut-off such as MoCA <26 or MMSE <24. Formal neuropsychological testing is helpful when the diagnosis or subtype remains uncertain, when deficits are subtle, or when the cognitive profile needs clearer definition.

Investigations should be targeted rather than reduced to a rigid universal panel. I would obtain blood or urine tests for plausible reversible or contributing causes according to clinical context and usually arrange **structural brain imaging** unless the diagnosis and subtype are already clear. MRI is preferable when vascular burden or subtype detail matters, while CT is acceptable when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated.

Specialist biomarkers now have a larger role in selected cases, particularly when Alzheimer disease remains uncertain or when disease-modifying anti-amyloid treatment is being considered. CSF amyloid/tau and validated **plasma biomarkers** can increase aetiological confidence in the appropriate syndrome, but they complement rather than replace comprehensive clinical assessment and structural imaging.

Finally, the assessment should end with practical risk and care planning: driving, medication and financial safety, capacity, safeguarding and support for the patient and carers according to the individual situation and jurisdiction.

## Must-know board facts

- **Functional loss matters more than one test score:** interference with independent everyday functioning distinguishes dementia/major NCD from milder cognitive impairment.
- **MoCA, MMSE and ACE-III cut-offs are not diagnostic thresholds**; operating characteristics vary with population and context.
- A **normal brief cognitive test does not exclude dementia**.
- Do not teach one fixed “reversible dementia blood panel”; investigations are targeted to plausible reversible/contributing causes.
- Plasma Alzheimer biomarkers are entering specialist practice but do **not** replace clinical assessment or structural imaging universally.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing dementia from a screening score alone.
- Using MoCA <26 or MMSE <24 as though they were diagnostic criteria.
- Omitting collateral history or functional decline.
- Ordering one fixed investigation panel regardless of context.
- Ordering biomarkers before doing a proper clinical assessment.
- Treating “major neurocognitive disorder” as the only current terminology.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Terminology:** “Major neurocognitive disorder” is DSM terminology. **Dementia remains current clinical terminology**, and ICD-11 retains dementia diagnoses organised by aetiology.
