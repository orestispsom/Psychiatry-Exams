# Q85. How do you use medication for adult ADHD?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm diagnosis and indication**
2. **Baseline cardiovascular assessment**
3. **Methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine first-line**
4. **Switch between stimulant options if needed**
5. **Atomoxetine after both are unsuitable/ineffective**
6. **Monitor BP, pulse and weight**
7. **Misuse and diversion risk**

## Model oral answer

When medication is indicated for adult ADHD, the current high-level sequence is **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate as first-line pharmacological options**, followed by a trial of the alternative stimulant if an adequate trial is ineffective or not suitable. **Atomoxetine** is the principal non-stimulant option after intolerance or nonresponse to both stimulants. Treatment should follow a confirmed developmental ADHD diagnosis and be accompanied by cardiovascular, weight and misuse/diversion assessment and monitoring.

Before starting medication I would review the patient’s medical and psychiatric history, current medicines and substance use. Baseline assessment should include a **cardiovascular history, pulse and blood pressure, weight**, and assessment of substance misuse or diversion risk. A routine ECG is **not required in every patient**; it is used when the history, examination or other clinical factors make it indicated.

Choice between methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine should be individualised. A useful NICE sequencing anchor is to give **separate adequate trials of each stimulant, commonly around 6 weeks at an adequate dose**, before concluding that both are ineffective. If both are ineffective or not tolerated, atomoxetine is the principal non-stimulant option in the current sequence.

Mechanistically, **methylphenidate inhibits DAT and NET**, whereas **lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug converted to dexamfetamine**, which increases catecholamine release/reverse transport. **Atomoxetine selectively inhibits NET**. These distinctions can help explain the stimulant versus non-stimulant categories, but do not replace clinical selection and monitoring.

Follow-up should establish that medication is improving **clinically meaningful functioning**, not merely changing a questionnaire score. I would monitor **blood pressure and pulse before and after each dose change and every 6 months**, together with weight and adverse effects, while reviewing adherence and whether the formulation fits the patient’s daily needs.

Misuse and diversion risk should be assessed explicitly but without assuming that every adult requesting treatment is misusing medication. Long-acting or prodrug formulations can be useful when diversion/misuse risk is a concern, but they do not have zero abuse potential.

Psychiatric comorbidity also matters. If **psychosis or mania is active**, ADHD medication should be stopped and the acute episode treated; the ADHD plan can then be reconsidered after resolution. A past psychosis or substance-use disorder is not an automatic permanent stimulant exclusion, but it requires specialist risk assessment and appropriate monitoring.

The concise viva answer is therefore: confirm adult ADHD properly, perform cardiovascular and misuse/diversion assessment, use methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine first, switch between them if needed, move to atomoxetine after both are unsuitable or ineffective, and monitor cardiovascular parameters, weight, response and misuse risk over time.

## Must-know board facts

- NICE adult sequence: **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first-line**, trial the alternative if needed, then **atomoxetine** after intolerance/nonresponse to both.
- Separate adequate stimulant trials are commonly around **6 weeks** each in the NICE sequence.
- **Methylphenidate:** DAT/NET reuptake inhibition.
- **Lisdexamfetamine:** prodrug of dexamfetamine; increases catecholamine release/reverse transport.
- **Atomoxetine:** selective NET inhibition.
- **BP/pulse:** before and after each dose change, then **every 6 months**.
- Acute psychosis/mania: stop ADHD medication and **reassess after resolution**; past psychosis/SUD is not an automatic permanent contraindication.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q85a. What if the patient has a previous psychosis or a substance use disorder?

Neither a history of psychosis nor a substance use disorder is automatically an **absolute stimulant contraindication**. The key is the current clinical state and the risk formulation.

If psychosis or mania is active, I would stabilise it first and then reassess the ADHD treatment plan. With a substance use disorder, I would assess current use, misuse and diversion risk, favour a **long-acting or prodrug formulation** when appropriate, consider non-stimulant treatment where it fits the case, and monitor closely. The diagnosis of ADHD and the substance-use problem should both be treated on their merits rather than using the history alone as an automatic exclusion.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Starting medication from a positive screening questionnaire without a confirmed ADHD diagnosis.
- Treating routine ECG as mandatory for every adult.
- Declaring stimulant failure after only one stimulant class.
- Calling long-acting/prodrug formulations abuse-proof.
- Treating past psychosis or substance use as an automatic lifelong stimulant contraindication.
