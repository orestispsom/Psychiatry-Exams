# Q60. How do you assess autism in an adult?

## Recall spine

1. **Lifelong developmental pattern**
2. **Social communication and interaction**
3. **Restricted, repetitive and sensory features**
4. **Reconstruct developmental history**
5. **Assess function and adaptation**
6. **Differentiate and identify comorbidity**
7. **Consider masking without making it diagnostic**

## Model oral answer

Adult autism assessment is a **developmental assessment**, not the diagnosis of a new adult-onset syndrome. I would establish a lifelong pattern combining social-communication and social-interaction differences with restricted or repetitive patterns, insistence on sameness, restricted interests and/or sensory differences, and show how that pattern affects current functioning.

I would begin with the person’s current presentation while keeping the developmental frame in view. In the social domain I would explore reciprocal interaction, understanding and navigating social conventions, relationships and communication style. In the second domain I would ask about routines, difficulty with change, restricted or highly focused interests, repetitive behaviours and sensory sensitivities or sensory-seeking patterns. Both domains matter; social difficulty alone is not sufficient.

I would then reconstruct **childhood and adolescent development** as far as possible. This includes early peer relationships, communication style, play and interests, responses to change, sensory experiences, schooling and adaptation. Informants and records can be helpful, but they are not always available, so their absence should be acknowledged rather than treated as an automatic exclusion.

Current functioning is equally important. I would assess education and employment, relationships, independent living, daily organisation and the effect of sensory or environmental demands. Some adults function well in highly structured settings but experience marked difficulty when social or executive demands increase, so apparent achievement should not substitute for assessing the effort and supports required.

The differential and comorbidity assessment should include **social anxiety, ADHD, OCD, schizoid or schizotypal traits, psychosis and intellectual disability**, as well as mood and anxiety disorders and other co-occurring problems. The aim is not to force a single explanation for every difficulty. Autism can coexist with these conditions, but the autistic pattern should remain developmentally coherent.

Structured instruments and questionnaires can support the assessment, but **no AQ, ADOS, ADI-R, CAT-Q or other single tool independently establishes or excludes autism**. Results must be interpreted in the context of developmental history, direct clinical assessment and functional information.

A further issue in adults is **masking or camouflaging**. Some people consciously suppress or compensate for visible autistic features, which can make a short structured encounter look less obviously autistic. Camouflaging can contribute to under-recognition, including in some women and other adults, but occurs across genders and is **not itself a diagnostic criterion**. “Autistic burnout” is also a useful lived-experience and research construct, not a formal diagnostic requirement.

The overall assessment therefore integrates developmental continuity, both core domains, real-world functioning, collateral where available, differential diagnosis and the possibility that compensatory strategies may have obscured the presentation.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q60a. What is masking or camouflaging?

Camouflaging refers to **suppressing, concealing or compensating for observable autistic features**. Examples can include rehearsed conversational scripts, imitation of peers, deliberate learning of social rules, forced eye contact or suppression of stimming.

It can reduce visible signs during a brief structured assessment and contribute to delayed recognition. However, **masking is not itself a diagnosis or diagnostic criterion**, and it should not become a stereotype such as assuming that autism in women is defined by masking. It is one piece of the clinical formulation that must be supported by the wider developmental pattern.

## Must-know board facts

- Autism is a **lifelong neurodevelopmental condition** with developmental onset.
- Both **social-communication/social-interaction** and **restricted/repetitive/sensory** domains matter.
- Structured tools support assessment but **do not independently diagnose or exclude autism**.
- Masking can obscure presentation but **does not replace diagnostic evidence**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing autism from CAT-Q, ADOS, ADI-R or another instrument alone.
- Treating masking as proof of autism.
- Assuming camouflaging is exclusive to women.
- Treating “autistic burnout” as a formal diagnostic criterion.
- Ignoring developmental history because the patient presents in adulthood.
