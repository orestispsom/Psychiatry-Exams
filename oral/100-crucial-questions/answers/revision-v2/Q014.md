# Q14. What do we know about the neurobiology of schizophrenia?

## Recall spine

1. **Polygenic vulnerability and neurodevelopment**
2. **Environmental/developmental risk**
3. **Circuit and connectivity abnormalities**
4. **Striatal dopamine dysregulation**
5. **Glutamate/NMDA hypotheses**
6. **Other interacting systems**
7. **No individual diagnostic biomarker**

## Model oral answer

Schizophrenia is best understood as a **biologically heterogeneous, highly polygenic disorder with neurodevelopmental and circuit-level abnormalities**, not as a disease explained by one gene, one neurotransmitter or one brain region. The strongest current model is multilevel: genetic liability interacts with developmental and environmental influences, producing alterations in brain systems that contribute to psychosis, cognition and negative symptoms. None of these findings currently provides a diagnostic biomarker for an individual patient.

Genetically, schizophrenia is **highly polygenic**. Liability is distributed across many common variants of individually small effect, together with rarer variants and copy-number variants that can carry larger effects in selected patients. A useful example is **22q11.2 deletion**, but neither this nor any other single variant is necessary or sufficient for ordinary schizophrenia. The clinical message is therefore vulnerability rather than genetic destiny.

A **neurodevelopmental framework** remains useful because abnormalities in developmental and synaptic processes may precede the first psychotic episode. Cognitive, social or motor differences can occur before overt psychosis. Environmental and developmental associations include cannabis exposure, migration or minority stress, urbanicity, childhood adversity and obstetric or other developmental exposures. These are probabilistic risk associations, not deterministic causes.

At the neurochemical level, the most robust finding remains **dopamine dysregulation**, particularly increased presynaptic striatal dopamine synthesis and release in psychosis. This is relevant especially to positive symptoms and aberrant salience. It is more accurate than saying schizophrenia is caused by “too much dopamine everywhere,” because dopaminergic abnormalities are pathway- and state-dependent and do not explain the full syndrome.

**Glutamatergic and NMDA-receptor hypofunction models** are also important. One influential hypothesis proposes NMDA hypofunction on cortical interneurons with downstream circuit disinhibition and effects on striatal dopamine. That is a useful mechanistic model, not an established single causal chain. Likewise, GABAergic, serotonergic and immune/inflammatory systems may contribute but should not be presented as independent proven causes.

Structural and functional neuroimaging show reproducible **group-level differences**, including ventricular enlargement and changes in cortical grey matter, hippocampal and thalamic systems, and broader dysconnectivity. These findings overlap substantially with healthy controls and other psychiatric disorders, so routine MRI or functional imaging cannot diagnose schizophrenia in an individual patient.

The clinically useful conclusion is therefore that schizophrenia has a strong biological basis but marked heterogeneity: polygenic vulnerability, neurodevelopment, dopamine and glutamate models, and distributed circuit abnormalities all matter, while no single mechanism or biomarker is sufficient.

## Must-know board facts

- Schizophrenia is **highly polygenic**: many common small-effect variants plus rarer variants/CNVs contribute.
- **22q11.2 deletion** is a high-yield rare-CNV example, but no fixed penetrance percentage should be treated as a diagnostic rule.
- The strongest neurochemical finding is **presynaptic striatal dopamine dysregulation**, especially relevant to positive psychosis.
- NMDA hypofunction is an important **mechanistic hypothesis**, not a proven single causal pathway.
- Ventricular, cortical, hippocampal and other imaging differences are **group-level research findings**, not individual diagnostic tests.

## Examiner follow-up

### How would you connect the glutamate and dopamine hypotheses?

A useful model is that cortical **NMDA-receptor hypofunction**, particularly involving inhibitory interneuron circuitry, may produce cortical disinhibition and alter downstream mesostriatal dopamine signalling. That can help explain why a glutamatergic disturbance could coexist with the robust striatal dopamine findings. I would present this as a **hypothesis linking systems**, not as an established linear causal chain in every patient.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Saying schizophrenia is simply “too much dopamine everywhere.”
- Quoting moving GWAS-locus counts or one heritability percentage as timeless board facts.
- Presenting environmental associations as deterministic causes.
- Treating one MRI/PET finding as diagnostic.
- Presenting the glutamate–dopamine hierarchy as settled fact.
