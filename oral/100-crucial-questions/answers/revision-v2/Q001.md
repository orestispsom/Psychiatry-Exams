# Q01. How do you approach a new psychiatric patient and organise the initial assessment?

## Recall spine

1. **Safety and setting**
2. **Presenting problem and longitudinal history**
3. **Collateral and reliability**
4. **Mental-state examination**
5. **Physical/neurological examination and targeted investigations**
6. **Explicit risk assessment**
7. **Formulation, differential and plan**

## Model oral answer

A new psychiatric assessment has three linked purposes: to identify the syndrome and differential diagnosis, to understand why this problem has arisen in this person at this time, and to establish a therapeutic relationship that allows assessment and treatment to proceed. It should be structured but hypothesis-driven, with safety considered from the beginning and a synthesis at the end rather than a checklist of disconnected facts.

I would first decide whether there is any **immediate emergency** that changes the setting or pace of assessment: risk to self or others, severe agitation, inability to care for basic needs, intoxication or withdrawal, delirium, or medical instability. If urgent containment or medical treatment is needed, that takes priority while the psychiatric assessment continues in parallel.

The history begins with the **presenting complaint and history of the current problem**: onset, course, severity, fluctuation, precipitating events and functional impact. I would then cover previous psychiatric history, medical and neurological illness, medication including over-the-counter or other agents, substance use, family history, and the relevant developmental, personal, social, occupational and relationship history. Forensic history is included when clinically relevant. Throughout, I am testing hypotheses rather than simply collecting symptoms; the absence of an expected feature can be as informative as its presence.

**Collateral information** is often essential. I would seek it particularly when insight, cognition, reliability or risk is uncertain, because the patient’s account may not capture behavioural change, deterioration in functioning or recent dangerous behaviour.

The **mental-state examination** gives the cross-sectional picture. I would organise it as appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition and insight. Findings must be interpreted phenomenologically and in context rather than converted directly into diagnoses.

Psychiatric assessment also includes the body. A **physical and neurological examination** is integral, especially in a first presentation, an atypical syndrome or when a secondary cause is suspected. Investigations should then be targeted to the history and examination rather than ordered as a universal psychiatric battery.

I would always make **risk explicit**: suicide and self-harm, violence or aggression, vulnerability, self-neglect and any other immediate safety issue relevant to the presentation. Risk assessment should feed directly into the management plan.

Finally, I would synthesise the information into a **problem list, differential diagnosis and formulation**. The formulation brings together predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating biological, psychological and social factors, alongside protective factors and current functioning. From that I would derive the immediate treatment plan, further assessment needed, follow-up and a provisional view of prognosis. The process remains iterative: as collateral, examination findings and the longitudinal course emerge, the formulation and diagnosis should be revised.

## Must-know board facts

- A practical MSE scaffold is **appearance/behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form/content, perception, cognition and insight**. It is an organisational framework, not a statutory or diagnostic requirement for one exact domain count.
- A useful formulation scaffold is the **4Ps**—predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors—considered across biological, psychological and social domains.
- **No single delusional feature is pathognomonic** of a mental disorder. Delusions must be assessed phenomenologically and in cultural and clinical context.
- **HISTORICAL_EXAM_FACT:** Jaspers distinguished *Verstehen*—empathic understanding of subjective meaning—from *Erklären*—causal explanation. This remains useful conceptual viva language, not a modern diagnostic criterion.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q01a. A patient reports hearing a voice while fully awake. How do you distinguish a true hallucination from an illusion, an organic/substance-related phenomenon or a sensory-deprivation phenomenon?

A **hallucination** is a perception-like experience occurring without an external stimulus; an **illusion** is a misperception or misinterpretation of a real stimulus. I would characterise modality, clarity, location and relation to external space, timing, triggers, level of consciousness, insight and associated symptoms.

Organic or delirious causes are suggested by impaired attention or consciousness, disorientation, cognitive fluctuation, acute onset, sleep–wake disturbance, visual illusions or misinterpretations and a medical precipitant. For substances or medication, I would establish the temporal relationship to intoxication, withdrawal or exposure without assuming causation from exposure alone. Sensory impairment can also produce hallucinations, so vision, hearing and the context of sensory deprivation matter. A single hallucination never establishes schizophrenia; it has to be interpreted within the whole syndrome.

### Q01b. What do you mean by formal thought disorder, and how do the main abnormalities differ?

Formal thought disorder concerns the **organisation and flow of thinking as expressed in speech**, not the content of thought. Relevant abnormalities include accelerated or flight-like associations, loosening of associations or derailment, tangentiality, circumstantiality, thought blocking, perseveration, incoherence or word salad, and neologistic or idiosyncratic language where present.

Thought form must remain separate from thought content: delusions and obsessions are content phenomena. I would also avoid overdiagnosing formal thought disorder in speech that is simply verbose, culturally unfamiliar or unusual but still coherent in context.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating the assessment as a fixed checklist without synthesis.
- Omitting collateral information or physical examination.
- Diagnosing from a single symptom.
- Confusing thought form with thought content.
- Treating a classic psychopathology framework as though it were a current diagnostic rule.
