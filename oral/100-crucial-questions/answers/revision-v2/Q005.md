# Q05. A patient has survived a suicide attempt. What can you do to reduce the risk of recurrence?

## Recall spine

1. **Early psychosocial assessment**
2. **Treat underlying psychiatric and substance problems**
3. **Collaborative safety plan**
4. **Make access to lethal means safer**
5. **Continuity, ownership and early follow-up**
6. **Targeted psychological intervention**
7. **Reassess risk dynamically**

## Model oral answer

After a suicide attempt, recurrence prevention begins with a careful **psychosocial assessment and an active continuity plan**, not simply with discharge once the immediate medical consequences are treated. I would clarify the function and context of the attempt, current suicidal intent, ongoing psychiatric and substance-related drivers, supports and vulnerabilities, then build a collaborative plan that reduces immediate danger and ensures rapid follow-up.

The psychosocial assessment should take place at the **earliest appropriate opportunity**. I would reconstruct what led to the act, the degree of planning and intent, what the person expected would happen, current mental state, whether suicidal thoughts remain, and what has changed since the attempt. I would also identify strengths, supportive relationships and practical problems that may influence the next few days.

Any **underlying psychiatric or substance-use disorder** needs active treatment. Medication should be used when indicated for the underlying disorder rather than presented as a generic anti-self-harm treatment in itself.

A core intervention is a **collaborative safety plan**. This should identify warning signs and triggers, coping strategies the person can use independently, people or places that provide support, family or friends who can help, crisis or out-of-hours contacts, and concrete steps for what to do if suicidal urges escalate. The plan has to be usable by the patient, not merely documented by the clinician.

I would address **access to lethal means** specifically and collaboratively. Means safety should follow the person’s own method, access and foreseeable scenarios rather than being generic advice.

Continuity is especially important after an attempt. Before discharge or transfer there should be a clear management plan, a named person or service responsible for follow-up and communication between the relevant teams. If there are ongoing safety concerns, **aftercare within 48 hours** is a useful current anchor. A structured, person-centred **CBT-informed intervention tailored to self-harm** is also supported; a typical recommendation is around **4–10 sessions**, adjusted to need.

Risk remains dynamic. I would reassess changes in intent, stressors, substance use, access to means, engagement and protective factors rather than rely on a risk category or score as a discharge gate. The objective is to make the next crisis less likely, recognise it earlier if it develops and ensure the person knows exactly how to access help.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q05a. What are the major suicide risk factors, and how does risk differ between psychiatric disorders?

High-yield factors include **previous attempt or self-harm, current intent or plan, hopelessness, severe or worsening psychiatric symptoms, substance misuse or intoxication, social isolation, acute interpersonal, financial or legal crises, chronic or painful physical illness, recent discharge or care transition, and access to lethal means**.

I would separate background or static vulnerabilities from dynamic future factors and protective factors. Mood disorders, psychotic disorders, substance-use disorders and personality pathology are all associated with increased suicide risk, but I would not give a rigid diagnostic ranking or quote old lifetime diagnosis-specific percentages. Diagnosis informs the formulation; it does not predict the individual outcome.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Using a “no-suicide contract” or generic reassurance instead of a concrete safety plan.
- Discharging without clear follow-up ownership.
- Giving generic means-restriction advice rather than addressing the person’s likely method and access.
- Quoting diagnosis-specific lifetime suicide percentages as though they predict the individual.
