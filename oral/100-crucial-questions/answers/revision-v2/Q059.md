# Q59. How do you diagnose adult ADHD and distinguish it from common mimics?

## Recall spine

1. **Developmental disorder, not adult symptom counting**
2. **Some childhood symptoms before 12**
3. **Cross-situational pattern with current impairment**
4. **Reconstruct development and seek collateral where possible**
5. **Exclude mimics and identify comorbidity**
6. **Use scales as support, not diagnosis**
7. **Treat after diagnostic formulation and safety assessment**

## Model oral answer

Adult ADHD is a **neurodevelopmental disorder**, so diagnosis requires more than current complaints of distractibility or poor organisation. I would establish a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with executive dysfunction, confirm that some symptoms were present **before age 12**, demonstrate impairment across important areas of current life, and actively assess alternative explanations and comorbidity.

I would first characterise the present syndrome: distractibility, disorganisation, forgetfulness, poor task completion, impulsivity, restlessness and difficulties with planning or sustained effort, according to the individual presentation. The key is functional impact. I would ask about work or education, household management, finances and practical tasks, relationships and social functioning, and any consequences such as repeated underperformance or instability.

The developmental history is central. Current classification requires **some ADHD symptoms before age 12**, but it does not require that the person was formally diagnosed in childhood or that school reports are available. I would reconstruct childhood and adolescent functioning carefully, using the patient’s account and collateral or records when available. If collateral cannot be obtained, that creates uncertainty but does not automatically exclude the diagnosis.

Symptoms should also form a **cross-situational pattern** rather than appearing only in one context. I would therefore ask how difficulties manifest across work, home, relationships and other sustained responsibilities. Rating scales can structure symptom review and provide useful collateral information, but they do not establish ADHD on their own.

The differential is broad because concentration and executive problems are nonspecific. Important alternatives or comorbidities include **anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, sleep disorders, substance use, autism, personality pathology and TBI or other cognitive causes**. I would also review caffeine and other substances, current medication, sleep pattern and whether symptoms fluctuate with mood episodes or other states. Comorbidity does not itself rule out ADHD; the aim is to identify which symptoms are developmentally persistent and which are better explained by another condition.

One high-yield discriminator from bipolar disorder is **trait-like developmental persistence versus episodic change from baseline**. ADHD symptoms can vary with structure, demands and context, but the syndrome is developmental and persistent; mania or hypomania is episodic and includes a broader biological and behavioural shift.

Once the diagnosis is established, treatment should be individualised. At a high level, current adult pharmacological sequencing uses **lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate as first-line options**, with **atomoxetine** as the principal non-stimulant option after intolerance or inadequate response to both stimulants. Before medication, I would review cardiovascular history, pulse and blood pressure, weight, psychiatric and medical comorbidity, drug interactions and misuse or diversion risk. A routine ECG is not required for every patient.

The overall diagnostic principle is therefore developmental continuity, cross-situational impairment and careful differential diagnosis rather than a positive adult questionnaire alone.

## Must-know board facts

- **DSM-5-TR adult symptom threshold:** at least **5 symptoms** in either the inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive domain, with the wider diagnostic requirements also met. This is a DSM-specific numerical scaffold, not a stand-alone diagnosis.
- Current developmental anchor: **some ADHD symptoms before age 12**.
- A childhood diagnosis, surviving school records or parental collateral are **not mandatory**; a credible developmental history is still required.
- Diagnosis is developmental, cross-situational and impairment-based; **rating scales alone are insufficient**.
- **ADHD versus bipolar disorder:** developmental/trait-like pattern versus episodic shift from baseline.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q59a. What if there is no clear evidence of childhood impairment?

I would distinguish **absence of evidence from evidence of absence**. Some adults had symptoms that were missed because high ability, strong external structure or family scaffolding compensated for them, and childhood paperwork may simply no longer exist.

I would reconstruct childhood functioning as carefully as possible and look for persistent early symptoms rather than insisting on a prior diagnosis. However, **true de novo adult-onset ADHD is not an established DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 diagnosis**. If symptoms appear genuinely new in adulthood, I would reassess for an overlooked developmental history and alternative explanations such as mood, anxiety, sleep, substance or cognitive disorders.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Requiring a formal childhood diagnosis or surviving school records.
- Diagnosing ADHD from an adult rating scale alone.
- Calling any concentration problem ADHD.
- Confusing chronic developmental symptoms with episodic bipolar activation.
- Treating every anxiety, tic or substance-use history as an absolute stimulant contraindication.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older ICD-10 teaching used an onset-before-7 framework. Current DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 use **symptoms before age 12** as the developmental anchor.
