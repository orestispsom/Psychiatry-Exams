# Q99. How does Greek law approach criminal responsibility, and how does that differ from clinical psychiatric assessment?

## Recall spine

1. **Greek Penal Code Articles 34 and 36**
2. **Assess mental state at the time of the offence**
3. **Understand wrongfulness or act accordingly**
4. **Diagnosis alone is insufficient**
5. **Reconstruct retrospectively from multiple sources**
6. **Consider substances and malingering**
7. **Separate responsibility from capacity, risk and fitness**

## Model oral answer

In Greece, criminal responsibility in the presence of mental disorder is primarily framed by **Penal Code Articles 34 and 36**. The key question is retrospective and offence-specific: because of a qualifying mental or intellectual disorder, or a disturbance of consciousness, was the person able at the time of the offence to understand the wrongfulness of the act and to act according to that understanding? A psychiatric diagnosis by itself does not answer that legal question.

**Article 34** concerns abolished capacity. The relevant impairment may involve either the capacity to understand the wrongfulness of the act or the capacity to act in accordance with that understanding. The assessment therefore has to connect the mental state to the specific offence and to the person’s functional capacities at that time, rather than simply listing diagnoses or symptoms.

**Article 36** concerns substantially reduced rather than abolished capacity and is associated with reduced punishment. The distinction is therefore not between “psychiatric illness” and “no psychiatric illness,” but between the degree to which the qualifying disturbance affected the legally relevant capacities at the time of the act.

Forensic psychiatric assessment is necessarily **retrospective**. I would reconstruct the mental state at the relevant time using the interview, collateral information, medical and psychiatric records, witness accounts where available, and the person’s behaviour before, during and after the offence. I would examine the temporal relationship between symptoms and the act, whether the behaviour was organised or disorganised, and whether there is evidence that the person appreciated consequences or attempted to conceal, plan or respond to the offence. Those observations are evidence to be interpreted in context, not automatic legal tests.

Substance use and possible **malingering** also need to be considered where relevant, again without assuming either from the diagnosis or the allegation alone. The final opinion must distinguish observed or reconstructed psychiatric phenomena from the ultimate legal determination.

Criminal responsibility must also be separated from several other psychiatric questions. It is not the same as the patient’s **current diagnosis**, current decision-making capacity, current dangerousness or risk, or ability to participate in legal proceedings. A person may have a serious current disorder and still have been criminally responsible at the time of an earlier offence, or the reverse.

A further important distinction is what happens after a finding relevant to Article 34. **An Article 34 finding does not automatically mean forensic hospitalisation under Article 69.** Any subsequent safety measure is a separate judicial decision with its own statutory criteria. Diagnosis, criminal irresponsibility and the need for a later security measure must therefore not be collapsed into one step.

The concise viva answer is therefore: use Articles 34 and 36, reconstruct the mental state at the time of the offence, determine how any qualifying disturbance affected understanding of wrongfulness and behavioural control, and never infer responsibility or later compulsory measures directly from diagnosis.

## Must-know board facts

- **Article 34:** qualifying disorder/disturbance may abolish either the capacity to understand wrongfulness or the capacity to act according to that understanding.
- **Article 36:** substantially reduced, rather than absent, capacity is linked to reduced punishment.
- Assessment is **retrospective and offence-specific**.
- Diagnosis alone does **not** determine criminal responsibility.
- An Article 34 finding does **not automatically trigger Article 69 forensic hospitalisation**; subsequent safety measures require a separate judicial decision under their own criteria.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Giving M’Naghten or another UK formulation as Greek law.
- Equating psychosis or another diagnosis with criminal irresponsibility.
- Assessing only the patient’s current mental state rather than reconstructing the state at the time of the offence.
- Treating Article 34 and Article 69 as an automatic single sequence.
- Confusing criminal responsibility with current capacity, risk or fitness to participate in proceedings.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Greek criminal responsibility should be answered with **Penal Code Articles 34 and 36**. M’Naghten or UK formulations should not be presented as Greek law, and a finding under Article 34 should not be described as automatically producing forensic hospitalisation.
