# Q53. When should autoimmune encephalitis be suspected in a psychiatric presentation, and what do you do?

## Recall spine

1. **Rapid or subacute progression**
2. **Seizures, catatonia and cognitive decline**
3. **Movement, language and consciousness change**
4. **Autonomic instability or focal neurological signs**
5. **EEG + MRI + CSF + serum/CSF antibodies**
6. **Early immunotherapy and tumour assessment**

## Model oral answer

Autoimmune encephalitis should be suspected when a new psychiatric syndrome evolves **rapidly over days to weeks** and is accompanied by neurological or encephalopathic features. In that setting, the presentation should not be assumed to be primary psychosis or mania until an encephalitic process has been actively considered. The practical response is urgent neurological assessment, EEG, MRI, CSF studies including neuronal antibodies, serum testing, and early immunotherapy once infection has been reasonably excluded.

The most important clues are **red flags in the course and examination**. New seizures, catatonia, rapidly progressive cognitive decline, reduced or fluctuating consciousness, abnormal movements or orofacial dyskinesias, autonomic instability, language dysfunction and focal neurological signs all raise concern. A marked behavioural or psychiatric change that is disproportionate to the expected course of a primary psychiatric disorder should also lower the threshold for investigation.

**Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis** is the prototypical example because psychiatric symptoms may dominate early. Other neuronal antibodies may be relevant according to phenotype, including **LGI1 or CASPR2**, but the viva answer should not become an antibody catalogue. In LGI1 encephalitis, **faciobrachial dystonic seizures are highly characteristic** and hyponatraemia is common, but neither finding should be called pathognomonic or obligatory.

The diagnostic pathway should include a careful neurological examination and **EEG, MRI brain and CSF assessment**. Neuronal-antibody testing should usually use **both serum and CSF** because performance differs by antibody. CSF is especially important for NMDAR antibodies, whereas some antibodies such as LGI1 may be detected more sensitively in serum. Serum-only testing can therefore mislead in some syndromes, but the correct message is not that one specimen is universally superior for every antibody.

EEG may provide evidence of encephalopathy or seizure activity, while MRI can support the diagnosis or help identify alternative pathology. **Extreme delta brush** is a characteristic EEG pattern associated with anti-NMDAR encephalitis, but it is uncommon and not required for diagnosis. A normal or non-specific result on one investigation should not end the work-up when the clinical red flags remain strong.

The assessment should also include a search for an associated tumour when appropriate to the clinical syndrome or antibody profile.

Treatment should not be delayed unnecessarily while waiting for every result. Once infection has been **reasonably excluded**, first-line immunotherapy consists of **high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG and/or plasma exchange**. If an associated tumour is present, treating or removing it is part of the therapeutic strategy. Escalation to second-line immunotherapy belongs in specialist neurological care.

Antipsychotics may sometimes be required for severe agitation or dangerous behavioural disturbance, but they are only symptomatic treatment. They do not treat the encephalitis and must not delay the neurological work-up or immunotherapy.

## Must-know board facts

- Rapid psychiatric deterioration plus **seizures, catatonia, cognitive decline, movement disorder, altered consciousness or autonomic instability** should trigger an autoimmune-encephalitis pathway.
- Test **both serum and CSF** when appropriate. CSF is particularly important for NMDAR antibodies; some antibodies such as LGI1 may be more sensitive in serum.
- **Extreme delta brush** is characteristic of anti-NMDAR encephalitis but is uncommon and not required.
- **LGI1:** faciobrachial dystonic seizures are highly characteristic and hyponatraemia is common; neither is an absolute diagnostic criterion.
- First-line immunotherapy includes **corticosteroids, IVIG and/or plasma exchange**, plus tumour treatment where relevant.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q53a. Why is serum-only antibody testing dangerous?

Because the diagnostic performance of neuronal antibodies depends on the antibody and specimen. In **anti-NMDAR encephalitis**, CSF testing is particularly important and an isolated serum result can mislead. Conversely, some antibodies such as **LGI1** may be more sensitively detected in serum. The safe approach is syndrome-led interpretation with the appropriate serum/CSF combination, not a universal rule that one compartment is always superior.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing schizophrenia or mania from the psychiatric phenotype despite neurological red flags.
- Waiting for a complete “classic syndrome.”
- Relying on serum-only testing indiscriminately.
- Calling extreme delta brush required or common enough to exclude the diagnosis when absent.
- Calling faciobrachial dystonic seizures pathognomonic.
- Turning the answer into an exhaustive antibody list.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Current approach:** A modern answer should go beyond naming anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis. The clinically useful framework is red-flag recognition followed by EEG, MRI, appropriate CSF plus serum antibody assessment and early immunotherapy once infection is reasonably excluded.
