# Q37. How do you assess and formulate a substance use disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Map every substance and pattern of use**
2. **Identify intoxication, withdrawal and dependence features**
3. **Define psychiatric, physical and social harms**
4. **Assess immediate and longer-term risk**
5. **Clarify previous treatment, motivation and goals**
6. **Formulate comorbidity, maintaining factors and harm reduction**

## Model oral answer

A substance-use assessment should establish **what is being used, the pattern and syndrome of use, the harms and risks, and what is maintaining it**, then convert that information into a treatment formulation. I would not focus only on quantity; I would assess intoxication, withdrawal, loss of control, comorbidity, functional consequences, immediate safety and the patient’s own goals.

I would begin by mapping **all substances**, including alcohol, illicit drugs, prescribed medication, over-the-counter agents and relevant polysubstance use. For each, I would ask about quantity and frequency, route, duration, recent changes, context of use and whether substances are combined. The temporal pattern matters because episodic binges, continuous use and use linked to particular situations can carry different risks.

I would then establish the **substance-related syndrome**. This includes episodes of intoxication and withdrawal, craving, impaired control, increasing priority given to substance use, tolerance or withdrawal where present, and continued use despite harm. Current classification is broader than the older shorthand of simply deciding between harmful use and dependence, so the assessment should also recognise harmful episodes or patterns, withdrawal states and substance-induced psychiatric syndromes when relevant.

The next step is to define the **consequences**. Psychiatric effects may include worsening mood, anxiety, psychosis or other symptoms, while physical complications depend on the substance and route. I would ask about occupational and relationship impairment, financial or legal consequences, neglect of responsibilities and the effect on housing or social stability.

Risk assessment is integral. I would specifically ask about **overdose, withdrawal seizures, self-harm or suicide, violence, neglect or safeguarding concerns, and driving or occupational risk**. If the history is unreliable or risk is unclear, collateral information can be important. Physical examination and investigations should be targeted to the substances used, complications suspected and treatment being considered rather than applied as an indiscriminate battery.

I would also review **previous treatment attempts and periods of abstinence or reduced use**: what helped, what precipitated relapse, and whether the patient has experienced severe withdrawal or other complications before. Motivation should not be treated as a fixed personality trait. I would clarify the patient’s current goals, readiness for change and whether the immediate priority is abstinence, reduction, safer use or engagement with treatment.

Finally, I would formulate the case by integrating the substance-use syndrome with psychiatric and medical comorbidity, social context, triggers, reinforcing factors, previous successful changes and current risks. The plan should address both **treatment and harm reduction**, with realistic goals and follow-up rather than assuming that diagnosis alone determines the next step.

## Must-know board facts

- **DSM-5-TR substance use disorder severity:** **2–3 criteria = mild, 4–5 = moderate, ≥6 = severe**, out of 11 criteria. This is a DSM-specific severity scaffold; it should not be presented as the ICD-11 architecture.
- Dependence remains clinically recognisable through impaired control, craving, prioritisation of use, tolerance or withdrawal, and persistence despite harm.
- Current ICD-11 substance-related classification is broader than the older ICD-10 harmful-use/dependence shorthand.
- Urine toxicology is an **exposure aid**, not a diagnosis or precise chronology tool. Do not memorise one fixed detection-window table across assays, substances and patterns of use.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q37a. How do you approach tobacco cessation in schizophrenia, including important psychotropic interactions?

Smoking cessation should be **actively offered** to people with schizophrenia, but medication interactions must be anticipated. The important pharmacokinetic point is that **polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke—not nicotine—induce CYP1A2**.

This is especially important with **clozapine**, and also affects olanzapine. Abrupt smoking reduction or cessation removes CYP1A2 induction and can raise drug exposure, sometimes within days. A smoke-free admission can therefore increase clozapine concentrations even when nicotine replacement is provided.

**Nicotine replacement and nicotine vaping do not preserve smoke-induced CYP1A2 induction.** I would therefore arrange specialist medication review, monitor for toxicity and use therapeutic drug monitoring where appropriate rather than applying one universal percentage dose reduction. If smoking is restarted, clozapine or olanzapine exposure can fall again, with possible loss of efficacy, so the medication plan needs review in both directions.

For cessation treatment, **varenicline and nicotine replacement are legitimate options** in schizophrenia; bupropion may also be used after individual assessment of contraindications. The older blanket psychiatric-safety alarm around varenicline should not be repeated as a universal contraindication.

**Follow-up recall spine:** offer cessation → smoke PAHs induce CYP1A2 → clozapine/olanzapine → stopping raises exposure → NRT is not smoking → monitor/TDM → restarting smoking lowers exposure.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Focusing only on amount used and missing consequences, risk and comorbidity.
- Ignoring prescribed sedatives, opioids or polysubstance use.
- Treating motivation as fixed rather than something to assess and work with.
- Treating a toxicology result or detection window as proof of diagnosis or exact timing.
- Saying nicotine induces CYP1A2.
- Assuming nicotine replacement prevents the clozapine interaction.
- Memorising a fixed clozapine percentage dose reduction after smoking cessation.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Substance-use classification:** Older examination shorthand centred on ICD-10 harmful use and dependence syndrome. Current ICD-11 uses a broader substance-specific architecture that also distinguishes harmful episodes or patterns, intoxication, withdrawal and substance-induced disorders.

**Smoking cessation in schizophrenia:** Varenicline should not be rejected through an old blanket psychiatric-safety warning. The key practical safety issue is anticipating smoke-related CYP1A2 changes, particularly with clozapine.
