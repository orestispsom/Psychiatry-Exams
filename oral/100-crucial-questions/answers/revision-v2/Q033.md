# Q33. How do you distinguish acute stress disorder from adjustment disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Identify the classification system**
2. **DSM ASD follows qualifying trauma**
3. **DSM ASD lasts 3 days to 1 month**
4. **ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction is not a mental disorder**
5. **Adjustment disorder follows a broader psychosocial stressor**
6. **Look for preoccupation, failure to adapt and impairment**

## Model oral answer

The distinction begins with the **classification system**, because DSM and ICD do not use the same architecture. DSM-5-TR retains Acute Stress Disorder as a mental disorder after qualifying trauma. ICD-11 does not have an equivalent acute stress disorder diagnosis; its **Acute Stress Reaction is explicitly not classified as a mental disorder**. Adjustment disorder is different again because it can follow a much wider range of psychosocial stressors.

In **DSM Acute Stress Disorder**, there is first exposure to qualifying trauma. The syndrome then includes at least **9 symptoms** drawn from intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance and arousal, with clinically significant distress or impairment and appropriate exclusions. The key duration anchor is **3 days to 1 month after the trauma**. If clinically significant trauma symptoms persist beyond one month, the patient should be reassessed for PTSD; the diagnosis does not simply “convert automatically.”

That DSM construct should not be imported into ICD-11. **ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction** describes a transient normal-range response after exceptional stress. It usually begins quickly and tends to settle over days or roughly a week as the immediate threat or consequences resolve. The high-yield point is that ICD-11 does not classify this response as a psychiatric disorder.

**Adjustment disorder** follows an identifiable psychosocial stressor that does not have to meet a trauma threshold. Its characteristic pattern is **preoccupation with the stressor or its consequences together with failure to adapt**, causing clinically significant impairment. The syndrome is therefore organised around maladaptation to a stressor rather than the trauma-specific acute syndrome defined in DSM ASD.

Timing must again be tied to the classification system. **DSM** expects adjustment-disorder symptoms to begin within **3 months** of the stressor and usually resolve within **6 months** after the stressor or its consequences have ended. **ICD-11** generally expects onset within about **1 month** and also usually expects resolution within about 6 months once the stressor or consequences end, unless the stressor persists.

The practical viva distinction is therefore: DSM ASD is a **trauma-specific mental disorder within the 3-day to 1-month post-trauma window**; ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction is a **non-disorder response to exceptional stress**; and adjustment disorder can follow broader psychosocial stressors and is characterised particularly by preoccupation and impaired adaptation.

## Must-know board facts

- **DSM Acute Stress Disorder:** duration **3 days to 1 month**.
- Persistence beyond 1 month means **reassess for PTSD**; conversion is not automatic.
- **ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction:** **not a mental disorder**.
- **DSM adjustment disorder:** onset within **3 months**; usually resolves within **6 months** after the stressor or its consequences end.
- **ICD-11 adjustment disorder:** onset is generally within about **1 month**; timing must not be blended with the DSM rule.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Blending DSM Acute Stress Disorder with ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction.
- Calling ICD-11 Acute Stress Reaction a psychiatric disorder.
- Saying ASD automatically becomes PTSD at one month.
- Quoting DSM adjustment-disorder timing as though it were the ICD-11 rule.
- Diagnosing adjustment disorder after any stressful event without maladaptation and impairment.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification distinction:** Acute Stress Disorder is a DSM diagnosis. Do not present it as the current ICD-11 category, and state which system supplies any timing rule you quote.
