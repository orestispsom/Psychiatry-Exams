# Q08. How do you assess whether a patient can give valid informed consent?

## Recall spine

1. **Adequate information**
2. **Ability to consent to this decision**
3. **Voluntariness**
4. **Specific consent and withdrawal**
5. **Substitute consent if incapable**
6. **Emergency exception**
7. **Psychiatric autonomy: admission is not blanket treatment consent**

## Model oral answer

The default in Greek medical law is that a medical act requires the patient’s **prior valid consent**. In practice, I would ask three broad questions: has the patient received enough understandable information, can this patient meaningfully consent to this specific decision, and is the decision voluntary and specific rather than the result of coercion, error or deception?

First, **information** must be full, clear and comprehensible enough to support an informed decision. That includes the patient’s health condition, the proposed intervention, its expected result, foreseeable consequences and risks, alternatives and the expected course of recovery. Consent is meaningful only if the explanation is adapted to what the patient can actually understand.

Second, I would assess **ικανότητα για συναίνεση**, or ability to consent, functionally and for the particular decision. Greek law does not codify the UK Mental Capacity Act four-part test, so I would not present “understand, retain, use or weigh, communicate” as Greek statutory criteria. Clinically, however, I need to establish whether the patient can sufficiently understand the problem, the proposed intervention, the relevant risks and consequences and the available alternatives so that the consent is meaningful.

This is decision-specific rather than diagnosis-specific. A psychiatric diagnosis, psychosis, involuntary admission or disagreement with my recommendation does **not** by itself establish incapacity. The question is whether the person can consent to this particular act at this time.

Third, the consent must be **voluntary and specific**. It should refer to the proposed medical act and must not arise from mistake, fraud or threat. Consent can also be withdrawn.

If an adult cannot validly consent, substitute consent follows the Greek legal framework. Where a **judicial supporter** has been appointed for this function, that person provides consent; where none exists, the Code refers more broadly to **οικείοι**. I would not invent a priority hierarchy that the framework does not specify. Even when substitute consent is required, the patient should still receive information as far as feasible and be involved, with their views and assent sought to the greatest possible extent.

For **minors**, consent comes from those exercising parental responsibility or custody, while the minor’s own views should be considered according to maturity and understanding.

There is also an **emergency exception**. Necessary treatment may proceed when appropriate consent cannot be obtained and there is an immediate, absolute and urgent need; attempted suicide is specifically covered within this exception.

In psychiatry the overarching principle is that mental disorder does not erase dignity, preferences or autonomy. Involuntary admission and consent to a specific treatment are separate legal questions. Being detained involuntarily does not automatically authorise every medical intervention.

## Must-know board facts

- Greek law does **not** contain the UK statutory four-part capacity test.
- Psychiatric diagnosis or involuntary admission does not equal global incapacity.
- If an adult lacks capacity: consent is given by an appointed **judicial supporter** where applicable; otherwise the Code refers to **οικείοι**, without an invented priority hierarchy.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Importing UK capacity law and presenting it as Greek statute.
- Equating psychosis with incapacity.
- Treating involuntary admission as blanket consent to treatment.
