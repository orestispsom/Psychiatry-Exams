# Q38. How do you recognise and manage alcohol withdrawal?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise the withdrawal syndrome**
2. **Identify predictors of severe withdrawal**
3. **Choose community versus inpatient setting safely**
4. **Use an appropriate benzodiazepine regimen**
5. **Give thiamine and medical support**
6. **Monitor for seizures, delirium and autonomic instability**

## Model oral answer

Alcohol withdrawal ranges from an uncomplicated autonomic and anxiety syndrome to **seizures and delirium tremens**, so management begins by estimating severity and choosing a safe setting. I would assess current symptoms, previous severe withdrawal, comorbidity and social support, then use a benzodiazepine-based withdrawal regimen with thiamine and medical monitoring, escalating promptly if severe complications emerge.

Typical uncomplicated withdrawal includes **tremor, sweating and other autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, nausea and perceptual disturbance**. The two major complications I would actively anticipate are **withdrawal seizures** and **delirium tremens**. Delirium, marked autonomic instability, seizures or a patient who cannot be assessed reliably should immediately move the management away from a routine low-risk pathway.

The timing can be useful as rough classic orientation, but not as a diagnostic clock. Autonomic symptoms often emerge within roughly the first **6–12 hours**, perceptual symptoms may occur around **12–24 hours**, seizures commonly occur in the **24–48-hour** range, and delirium tremens classically emerges later, often around **48–72 hours**. These windows overlap substantially and vary between patients, so severity, history and current physiology matter more than matching an exact hour range.

The history of previous withdrawal is especially important. Previous **delirium tremens or withdrawal seizures**, significant physical or psychiatric comorbidity, frailty, poor social support and concurrent withdrawal from sedative drugs should all lower the threshold for inpatient or residential management. The setting must match the risk rather than simply the amount of alcohol reported.

**Benzodiazepine reducing regimens are standard treatment.** Long-acting benzodiazepines are often useful because they provide smoother withdrawal coverage, while **lorazepam or oxazepam are often preferred when hepatic oxidative metabolism is impaired**. That is a clinical preference rather than a mandatory rule. A fixed-dose regimen can be appropriate in community withdrawal management, whereas symptom-triggered treatment requires trained staff and frequent reliable assessment and is mainly suited to inpatient or residential settings.

A scale such as **CIWA-Ar** may assist structured monitoring, but it is an adjunct to clinical judgement rather than a diagnostic test or a stand-alone medication algorithm. Fixed cut-offs should not be applied mechanically, especially when the patient is delirious, medically unstable or unable to communicate consistently.

Medical care runs in parallel. I would give **thiamine**, assess hydration and nutrition, examine for physical complications and look for features suggesting Wernicke encephalopathy. Seizures, delirium, severe autonomic instability or suspected Wernicke require urgent escalation and hospital-level care rather than simply increasing a community regimen.

The practical approach is therefore to recognise the syndrome, identify who is at risk of severe withdrawal, select the correct treatment setting, use a monitored benzodiazepine regimen, give thiamine and supportive care, and escalate early when complications develop.

## Must-know board facts

- Classic withdrawal timing is only **approximate**: autonomic symptoms ~6–12 h, perceptual symptoms ~12–24 h, seizures ~24–48 h, delirium tremens often ~48–72 h; there is substantial overlap and individual variation.
- **Long-acting benzodiazepines** are often useful; **lorazepam or oxazepam** are often preferred when hepatic oxidative metabolism is impaired. Do not present this as an absolute rule.
- **CIWA-Ar is an adjunct**, not a diagnostic test or safe sole guide in complicated withdrawal; fixed medication cut-offs are protocol- and setting-dependent.
- **Symptom-triggered treatment is not automatically superior** in every setting; it requires reliable assessment and appropriate staffing.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q38a. How do you recognise and treat Wernicke encephalopathy?

Wernicke encephalopathy is a **medical emergency** and should be suspected early in a person with alcohol misuse or malnutrition who develops confusion or another mental-status change, ocular abnormalities, ataxia or gait disturbance, or another compatible presentation. The complete classic triad is often absent, so waiting for confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia together is unsafe.

Treatment should begin immediately and should not wait for imaging or diagnostic confirmation. The board-level regimen is **IV thiamine 300–500 mg three times daily for 3–5 days**. If the patient remains symptomatic, continue **300–500 mg IV daily for a further 3–5 days, or while clinical improvement continues**, and then move to oral thiamine.

Thiamine should be given **before or alongside carbohydrate where feasible**, but emergency glucose must never be delayed in a patient with hypoglycaemia. I would also check for and correct **hypomagnesaemia**, particularly when the response to thiamine is poor. Untreated Wernicke encephalopathy can progress to **Korsakoff syndrome**, which is why treatment should be empirical and urgent when suspicion is significant.

**Follow-up recall spine:** suspect early → do not wait for full triad → IV thiamine immediately → carbohydrate safely → correct magnesium → prevent Korsakoff.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating a patient with previous delirium tremens or withdrawal seizures as routine low-risk outpatient withdrawal.
- Treating classic withdrawal timing windows as rigid criteria.
- Following a CIWA-Ar score despite delirium or unreliable communication.
- Treating lorazepam/oxazepam as legally or universally mandatory in hepatic impairment.
- Requiring the full Wernicke triad before treatment.
- Using oral thiamine alone for suspected Wernicke encephalopathy.
- Delaying emergency glucose in hypoglycaemia.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Withdrawal timing:** Classic hour ranges remain useful orientation, but current management is severity- and history-led rather than clock-led.

**Wernicke recognition:** Historical teaching often overemphasised the complete triad. Current emergency practice requires a low threshold for diagnosis and immediate parenteral thiamine without waiting for all three classic features.
