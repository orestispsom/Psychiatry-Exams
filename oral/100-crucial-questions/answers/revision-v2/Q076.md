# Q76. How do you choose an antipsychotic?

## Recall spine

1. **Previous efficacy and tolerability**
2. **Metabolic vulnerability**
3. **EPS, prolactin and QT risk**
4. **Sedation, comorbidity and interactions**
5. **Adherence and formulation**
6. **Patient preference and priorities**
7. **Clozapine after two adequate failures**

## Model oral answer

Antipsychotic choice should be **individualised rather than based on a rigid first-line efficacy ranking**. I would integrate previous response, the patient’s adverse-effect vulnerabilities and physical comorbidity, interaction burden, adherence needs, available formulation and the patient’s own priorities. If schizophrenia remains inadequately responsive after two adequate antipsychotic trials, clozapine becomes the key evidence-based treatment.

I would first ask what has happened with previous antipsychotics: which drugs were effective, which adverse effects limited treatment, whether the trial was adequate and whether adherence was reliable. A previously effective and tolerated drug may be a strong choice unless the clinical context has changed.

The next step is to match the adverse-effect profile to the patient. **Metabolic risk** is especially important in someone with obesity, diabetes or other cardiometabolic vulnerability; clozapine and olanzapine are important high-metabolic-liability examples. If movement disorders are a concern, I would consider EPS vulnerability. Hyperprolactinaemia matters when there are sexual, reproductive, endocrine or bone-health concerns; risperidone, paliperidone and amisulpride are prolactin-elevating examples, whereas aripiprazole is relatively prolactin-sparing.

Mechanistically, the label “second-generation antipsychotic” should not be over-simplified. Many agents combine **D2 and 5-HT2A antagonism**, but the class is heterogeneous and partial agonists such as aripiprazole-type agents differ. Lower EPS liability is **drug- and dose-specific**, not a guaranteed property of every SGA.

I would also consider **QT risk, sedation and physical comorbidity**, together with the full medication list for pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions. The decision is not simply to choose the drug with the fewest adverse effects overall, because profiles differ and the relevant burden depends on the individual patient.

Adherence and formulation are equally important. If adherence is uncertain, treatment effectiveness cannot be judged properly. A **long-acting injectable** may be appropriate when the patient prefers it or when maintaining reliable exposure is a clinical priority. It should not be framed as a coercive option reserved for difficult patients.

Shared decision-making should include the adverse effects the patient is most concerned about, previous subjective experience of treatment, practical issues around dosing or formulation and the consequences of relapse. These factors often determine whether an otherwise pharmacologically reasonable choice will actually be sustainable.

Finally, apparent treatment resistance should not lead to endless serial switching. In schizophrenia, failure of **two adequate antipsychotic trials** should prompt consideration of clozapine rather than simply continuing an ordinary first-line sequence indefinitely.

The concise approach is therefore: previous response, adverse-effect vulnerability, physical and interaction risks, adherence/formulation, patient preference, then clozapine when genuine treatment resistance is established.

## Must-know board facts

- Many SGAs use **D2 + 5-HT2A antagonism**, but the class is mechanistically heterogeneous and includes **D2-family partial agonists**.
- Lower EPS liability is **drug- and dose-specific**, not a class guarantee.
- Clozapine and olanzapine are important examples of **high metabolic liability**.
- Risperidone, paliperidone and amisulpride are **prolactin-elevating** examples; aripiprazole is relatively prolactin-sparing.
- LAIs are appropriate when **preferred** or when reliable adherence/exposure is a clinical priority.
- In schizophrenia, clozapine is the key treatment after failure of **two adequate antipsychotic trials**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Giving a rigid ordinary first-line efficacy hierarchy instead of individualising treatment.
- Saying all SGAs work identically through D2/5-HT2A antagonism.
- Assuming every SGA has low EPS liability.
- Treating LAIs as inherently coercive.
- Continuing multiple ordinary antipsychotic trials after genuine treatment resistance is established.
