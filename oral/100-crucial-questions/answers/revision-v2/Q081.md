# Q81. What do you need to know about lithium prescribing and monitoring?

## Recall spine

1. **Indications**
2. **Serum level and 12-hour sampling**
3. **Baseline renal/thyroid/calcium assessment**
4. **Ongoing level and organ monitoring**
5. **Renal, thyroid and calcium adverse effects**
6. **Toxicity features**
7. **Interactions, dehydration and acute illness**

## Model oral answer

Lithium remains an important treatment for **acute mania, bipolar maintenance and antidepressant augmentation**, with evidence supporting reduction in suicide and self-harm risk. Safe prescribing depends on using the correct maintenance range, taking levels at the correct time, monitoring renal, thyroid and calcium function, and anticipating toxicity during interactions or dehydration. A serum level must always be interpreted with the patient’s clinical state and renal function.

For bipolar maintenance when initiating lithium, the standard NICE target is **0.6–0.8 mmol/L**. A range of **0.8–1.0 mmol/L** may be considered when there has been relapse while taking lithium or persistent subthreshold symptoms. I would not invent a separate acute-mania serum range here. Levels are conventionally sampled about **12 hours after the dose**, because inconsistent sampling makes serial interpretation unreliable.

Before treatment I would assess **renal function and U&Es, thyroid function, calcium, weight or BMI and pregnancy status when relevant**. An ECG is obtained when clinically indicated rather than automatically in every patient.

The ongoing NICE monitoring pattern is also a useful board anchor. Lithium levels are checked **every 3 months during the first year**. After that they are generally checked **every 6 months**, but remain **every 3 months in higher-risk groups**. Renal function and electrolytes including calcium, together with thyroid function, are generally checked **every 6 months**.

Common and important adverse effects include tremor, gastrointestinal symptoms and weight gain. Longer-term concerns include **hypothyroidism, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, hyperparathyroidism or hypercalcaemia and chronic renal risk**. Monitoring is therefore not only about the lithium number; it is surveillance for organ effects as well.

Toxicity may present with worsening or coarse tremor, gastrointestinal symptoms, **ataxia, dysarthria and confusion**, progressing in severe cases to seizures or coma. NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, diuretics and dehydration can increase lithium exposure. Intercurrent vomiting, diarrhoea or reduced fluid intake therefore deserves urgent attention.

A high lithium concentration alone should not be converted into an automatic dialysis rule. Decisions about extracorporeal treatment integrate the **serum level, renal function, clinical toxicity and expected elimination**. The EXTRIP framework recommends extracorporeal treatment when there is impaired kidney function with lithium above **4.0 mEq/L**, or when there is decreased consciousness, seizure or life-threatening dysrhythmia regardless of level. It suggests treatment when lithium is above **5.0 mEq/L**, when there is significant confusion, or when the expected time to fall below 1.0 mEq/L is more than **36 hours**. These are specialist toxicology criteria, not a one-number bedside rule.

The practical message is that lithium is highly effective but unforgiving of poor monitoring: know the maintenance targets, use 12-hour sampling, monitor renal/thyroid/calcium function, and act early when illness, dehydration or interacting drugs create a risk of accumulation.

## Must-know board facts

- Maintenance target when initiating: **0.6–0.8 mmol/L**.
- **0.8–1.0 mmol/L** may be considered with relapse or persistent subthreshold symptoms.
- Sample levels about **12 hours post-dose**.
- Levels: **3-monthly in year 1**, then generally **6-monthly**, or **3-monthly in higher-risk groups**.
- Renal/electrolytes including calcium and thyroid function: generally **6-monthly**.
- EXTRIP uses integrated criteria; do **not** teach “dialyse above X” as a single universal rule.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q81a. What do you do if a patient on lithium develops gastroenteritis, dehydration and has also taken an NSAID?

This is a high-risk situation for **lithium accumulation and toxicity**. I would **withhold lithium immediately** and arrange urgent assessment of the lithium level, U&Es and renal function, hydration and the patient’s neurological and general clinical state.

The patient needs acute medical review according to symptoms and severity; I would not wait for an arbitrary lithium concentration if the patient is clinically unwell. The NSAID and dehydration both increase concern, so the immediate task is to stop further lithium exposure, assess renal clearance and correct the acute medical problem safely.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Inventing an acute-mania lithium target that has not been established here.
- Interpreting a lithium level without checking sampling time, renal function and symptoms.
- Mapping toxicity severity directly to one serum concentration.
- Using one serum concentration as an automatic dialysis threshold.
