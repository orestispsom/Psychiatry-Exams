# Q25. How do you treat bipolar depression?

## Recall spine

1. **Confirm bipolar depression and assess mixed/manic risk**
2. **Use a guideline-specific bipolar-depression pathway**
3. **Match acute treatment to maintenance needs**
4. **Use antidepressants only cautiously and adjunctively**
5. **Avoid antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I**
6. **ECT for urgent or refractory illness**
7. **Separate international evidence from EU licensing**

## Model oral answer

Bipolar depression should not be treated as ordinary unipolar depression. I would first confirm the bipolar diagnosis and subtype, assess for current mixed or manic symptoms, suicide risk, psychosis or catatonia, review adherence and substance use, and consider previous treatment response and the patient’s longer-term maintenance needs.

The treatment sequence should be given with a **guideline context** rather than as a merged international list. In the NICE pathway, options include **quetiapine** or **fluoxetine combined with olanzapine** depending on current treatment and patient factors; **lamotrigine** may be considered in selected pathways. If the patient is already taking lithium or another maintenance treatment, I would first check adherence, dose/exposure and whether the existing maintenance strategy can be optimised before adding or changing acute treatment.

The choice should also anticipate long-term polarity. **Lamotrigine** is particularly relevant to depressive-polarity relapse prevention but is not an acute antimanic drug. Lithium may remain important when it is already part of an effective maintenance strategy, while quetiapine has both acute bipolar-depression and maintenance roles. The point is to connect the acute decision to the likely maintenance plan rather than treating the episode in isolation.

The main caution is the role of **antidepressants**. Antidepressant monotherapy is **not recommended in bipolar-I depression**. If an antidepressant is used, it should be selected cautiously alongside an antimanic or mood-stabilising strategy and with monitoring for switch or destabilisation. I would be particularly cautious in patients with previous antidepressant-induced mania, current mixed features or recent rapid cycling, and I would reassess the antidepressant if mania emerges.

**ECT** remains an important option for refractory bipolar depression and should be considered earlier when a rapid response is necessary because of severe suicidality, psychosis, catatonia or medical compromise from poor intake. Psychological intervention, attention to sleep and circadian regularity, substance management and relapse planning are useful adjuncts, but they do not replace an appropriate acute biological treatment when the episode is severe.

There is also an important distinction between **international treatment evidence and European licensing**. International guidelines include cariprazine and lurasidone-based strategies among options for bipolar-I depression. However, current European product indications should not be assumed to provide a generic bipolar-depression licence for these agents. They can be recognised as treatments with international evidence without being presented as routine EU-licensed bipolar-depression options.

The practical answer is therefore to confirm polarity and mixed-state risk, use a clearly identified treatment framework, connect acute treatment to maintenance needs, use antidepressants cautiously and not as bipolar-I monotherapy, and escalate to ECT when severity or refractoriness requires it.

## Must-know board facts

- **NICE-oriented bipolar-depression options:** quetiapine or fluoxetine+olanzapine are important acute options; lamotrigine may be considered in selected pathways. The exact sequence depends on what the patient is already taking.
- **Bipolar-I depression:** antidepressant monotherapy is **not recommended**; “contraindicated” is too broad as a universal regulatory statement.
- If an antidepressant is used, combine it with an appropriate antimanic strategy and monitor for **switch or mood destabilisation**.
- **Cariprazine and lurasidone:** have international bipolar-depression evidence, but international efficacy recommendations must not be converted automatically into a claim of routine EU bipolar-depression licensing.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating bipolar depression exactly like major depressive disorder.
- Merging NICE, international guideline and EMA licensing into one supposed universal hierarchy.
- Using antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar I.
- Calling antidepressant monotherapy universally “contraindicated” rather than not recommended/avoided in the treatment strategy.
- Presenting cariprazine or lurasidone as routine EU-licensed bipolar-depression treatments.
- Forgetting mixed features and switch risk.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Evidence versus licensing:** International bipolar-depression recommendations and European product licensing are not identical. State which authority or jurisdiction is being used rather than blending them.
