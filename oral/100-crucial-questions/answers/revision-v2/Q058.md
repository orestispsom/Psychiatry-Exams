# Q58. How do you distinguish Charles Bonnet syndrome from psychosis, delirium and neurodegenerative disease?

## Recall spine

1. **Significant visual impairment**
2. **Recurrent visual hallucinations**
3. **Insight usually preserved or developing**
4. **Exclude delirium and primary psychosis**
5. **Consider DLB/PD, epilepsy, drugs and focal neurological causes**
6. **Explain, reassure and optimise vision**

## Model oral answer

Charles Bonnet syndrome is characterised by **recurrent visual hallucinations in a person with significant visual impairment**, without a primary psychotic disorder or delirium explaining the phenomenon. The hallucinations are often vivid and formed, and insight is usually preserved or develops with explanation, but perfectly intact insight should not be treated as an absolute requirement at every moment. The main task is to recognise a visual-release phenomenon while excluding cognitive, neurological, medication-related and delirious causes.

The hallucinations are commonly **complex or formed**—for example people, faces, animals or scenes—but their precise form is supportive rather than diagnostic. They are typically visual rather than part of a broader psychotic syndrome, and significant visual impairment is central to the clinical context.

Insight is an important discriminator, but it needs nuance. Patients often recognise that the images are not real, yet initial uncertainty or partially fluctuating insight can occur. Persistent absent insight should make the clinician reconsider the diagnosis and look more carefully for another cause rather than automatically excluding Charles Bonnet syndrome from the outset.

The first major alternative is **delirium**. Delirium produces an acute fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually in the context of medical illness, medication effects, intoxication or withdrawal. A patient with visual hallucinations plus fluctuating inattention or reduced awareness should not be labelled as having uncomplicated Charles Bonnet syndrome.

The next concern is a **neurodegenerative or neurological syndrome**. Dementia with Lewy bodies or Parkinson disease dementia can include visual hallucinations, but the broader picture includes cognitive decline and may include cognitive fluctuations, parkinsonism and REM sleep behaviour disorder. Epilepsy, migraine, focal neurological disease, medications or substances can also produce visual phenomena and should be considered according to the presentation.

Primary psychosis is less likely when hallucinations are isolated to the visual modality in a visually impaired older adult without a broader psychotic syndrome, but the psychiatric examination should still assess delusions, thought disorder and other psychotic symptoms.

Management of uncomplicated Charles Bonnet syndrome is mainly **explanation and reassurance**. The patient should be told that the experience can occur in the context of impaired vision and does not, by itself, mean schizophrenia or dementia. Visual function should be optimised where possible, ophthalmological disease treated when feasible, and medications or intercurrent illness reviewed. Environmental or behavioural strategies may also help.

Routine antipsychotic treatment is **not standard care** for uncomplicated Charles Bonnet syndrome. Pharmacological evidence is limited and medication should be reserved for exceptional specialist circumstances rather than used reflexively.

The practical viva distinction is therefore visual impairment plus recurrent visual hallucinations, usually with retained or developing insight and without delirium or a broader psychotic or neurodegenerative syndrome, managed primarily through explanation, reassurance and optimisation of vision.

## Must-know board facts

- Preserved insight is **characteristic but not absolutely mandatory at every moment**; partial or developing insight can occur.
- Hallucinations are often **complex and formed**, but no single visual phenomenology is diagnostic.
- Routine antipsychotic treatment for uncomplicated Charles Bonnet syndrome is **not standard care**.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing schizophrenia from isolated visual hallucinations in a visually impaired older adult.
- Requiring perfectly intact insight as an absolute rule.
- Treating one hallucination phenotype, such as faces or Lilliputian figures, as required.
- Missing DLB or delirium when cognition or attention is abnormal.
- Recommending routine antipsychotic treatment.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Insight:** Older formulations can sound as though insight must be completely intact at all times. A current answer allows initial uncertainty or partial/fluctuating insight, while persistent absent insight remains a red flag for another diagnosis.
