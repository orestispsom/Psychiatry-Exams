# Q71. How do you assess and manage sexual dysfunction?

## Recall spine

1. **Define the affected sexual domain**
2. **Clarify onset, context and distress**
3. **Assess medical, psychiatric, relationship and medication causes**
4. **Investigate selectively**
5. **Treat reversible causes**
6. **Use psychosexual/relationship work and condition-specific treatment**

## Model oral answer

Sexual dysfunction should be assessed **biopsychosocially**, first defining whether the main difficulty is in desire, arousal, orgasm or pain/penetration, while recognising that these domains overlap. I would clarify onset, context and associated distress, then look systematically for relationship, psychiatric, medical, substance-related and medication causes before selecting treatment.

I would start with the phenomenology. Is the problem lifelong or acquired, generalised or situational, stable or fluctuating? I would ask about sexual desire, physiological and subjective arousal, orgasm, pain or penetration difficulties, and whether the problem occurs in all contexts or only with a particular partner or situation. Distress and functional impact matter because not every variation in sexual response is pathological.

The relationship and psychological context are important. I would explore communication, conflict, performance anxiety, mood and anxiety symptoms, trauma where relevant, body image and major life changes. At the same time, I would avoid assuming that a sexual problem is psychogenic simply because the patient has a psychiatric history.

Medical assessment depends on the presentation. Relevant contributors can include endocrine, neurological and other physical illness, and alcohol or substance use. Medication review is particularly important in psychiatry. **SSRIs can cause reduced desire, arousal and orgasmic dysfunction**, but the mechanism is multifactorial and drug-specific rather than a simple universal 5-HT2A explanation. **Prolactin-raising antipsychotics** can cause hypogonadal, sexual and reproductive effects.

Physical examination and further investigations should therefore be targeted to the symptom pattern and suspected cause. Prolactin or other endocrine tests are ordered when clinically indicated rather than as a universal panel.

Treatment begins by correcting reversible contributors where possible. That may mean treating a medical disorder, modifying a causative medication, addressing substance use or reviewing a psychiatric treatment that is producing unacceptable sexual adverse effects. Medication changes should consider psychiatric stability and local licensing rather than assuming that a commonly discussed alternative, such as bupropion, is a routine antidepressant option in every European jurisdiction.

Psychosexual or relationship-based interventions can be useful when anxiety, avoidance, communication or relationship factors are maintaining the problem. Condition-specific medical treatment can also be appropriate—for example, a **PDE5 inhibitor** in a suitable erectile-dysfunction presentation. PDE5 inhibitors augment the nitric-oxide–cGMP pathway in erectile tissue; they do not treat every domain of sexual dysfunction.

The overall approach is therefore to define the sexual problem precisely, assess it in medical, psychiatric and relational context, identify reversible causes and then use targeted psychosexual, medication-adjustment or medical treatment according to the mechanism.

## Must-know board facts

- **SSRI sexual dysfunction is multifactorial**; do not reduce it to one receptor mechanism.
- Prolactin-raising antipsychotics can produce **hypogonadism and sexual dysfunction**.
- **PDE5 inhibitors** inhibit phosphodiesterase-5 and augment NO–cGMP signalling; they are condition-specific treatment for erectile dysfunction, not a generic sexual-dysfunction drug.
- Medication-switch strategies must respect **jurisdiction and licensing**; bupropion is not a routine antidepressant option in every European setting.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Assuming every sexual problem is psychogenic.
- Ordering a universal endocrine panel regardless of phenotype.
- Attributing all SSRI sexual dysfunction to one receptor.
- Treating a PDE5 inhibitor as treatment for low desire, orgasmic dysfunction or pain.
- Importing a US antidepressant-switch option into European practice without licensing context.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older ICD-10 framing often placed sexual dysfunctions within psychiatric or “non-organic” categories. **ICD-11 places sexual dysfunctions under Conditions related to sexual health**, supporting a broader biopsychosocial rather than purely psychiatric framing.
