# Q15. What determines prognosis in schizophrenia?

## Recall spine

1. **Prognosis is multidimensional**
2. **Premorbid and baseline functioning**
3. **Duration of untreated psychosis**
4. **Negative symptoms and cognition**
5. **Adherence, engagement and substances**
6. **Family and social environment**
7. **Physical health and suicide**
8. **Probabilistic, not deterministic**

## Model oral answer

Prognosis in schizophrenia is **multidimensional, heterogeneous and probabilistic**. I would not reduce it to whether hallucinations and delusions remit. Symptomatic relapse and remission, occupational and social recovery, negative and cognitive disability, physical health and mortality, and suicide risk can follow different trajectories in the same patient.

A central distinction is between **symptomatic improvement and functional recovery**. Positive symptoms may respond while occupational, social or independent functioning remains impaired. Better premorbid and baseline functioning is therefore generally favourable, while greater illness burden, repeated admissions and persistent disability are associated with poorer functional outcomes.

**Duration of untreated psychosis**, or DUP, is an important prognostic correlate. Longer DUP is associated with poorer symptomatic and functional outcome. It is clinically useful because early detection and engagement are modifiable service targets, but I would describe DUP as an association rather than claim that it independently causes poor outcome in every patient.

Persistent **negative symptoms and cognitive impairment** are major determinants of long-term disability. It is also important to distinguish primary or enduring negative symptoms from secondary negative symptoms caused by active psychosis, depression, extrapyramidal adverse effects, sedation, substance use or environmental deprivation, because some secondary contributors are treatable.

**Adherence and engagement** strongly influence course. Poor adherence, disengagement and substance misuse increase relapse risk, while sustained treatment engagement and reduction of substance-related destabilisation are favourable. Family and social factors also matter: high expressed emotion is associated with relapse, while stable support, meaningful activity and rehabilitation can improve function.

Some classic prognostic teaching—such as acute onset, better premorbid function, fewer negative symptoms and better treatment engagement—remains useful as a set of **probabilistic correlates**, not as a deterministic scoring system. Outcome distributions vary greatly by cohort, treatment era and the definition of recovery, so the old “good/intermediate/chronic thirds” model should not be taught as current truth.

Prognosis also includes **physical health and mortality**. Schizophrenia is associated with a major excess-mortality gap and substantially shortened life expectancy, driven by both natural disease and non-natural causes including suicide. A broad contemporary context is often described as roughly a **10–20-year reduction in life expectancy**, but the exact figure varies by population and should not be treated as a fixed individual prediction. Suicide risk is particularly important early in illness and after destabilising events.

Overall, prognosis is something we formulate and try to modify. Earlier treatment, adherence, substance reduction, treatment of secondary negative symptoms, physical-health care, family work and rehabilitation all matter. Prognostic factors guide probability and treatment priorities; they do not determine destiny.

## Must-know board facts

- Prognosis has **symptom, functional, cognitive/negative-symptom, physical-health and mortality** dimensions.
- Longer **DUP** is associated with poorer symptomatic and functional outcomes.
- Better premorbid/baseline functioning, good engagement and fewer persistent negative symptoms are favourable correlates.
- Maintenance treatment, substance-use management, family/social support and rehabilitation influence modifiable outcome domains.
- Schizophrenia carries a **major excess-mortality gap**; exact mortality and suicide percentages should not be frozen into universal board rules.

## Examiner follow-up

### Which prognostic factors are actually modifiable?

Important modifiable targets include **duration of untreated psychosis at service level, adherence and engagement, substance use, treatment of active psychosis and secondary negative symptoms, physical-health risk, family environment and access to rehabilitation or meaningful occupation**. Baseline characteristics may help with formulation, but the clinical value of prognosis lies mainly in identifying factors we can change rather than predicting a fixed future.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Equating symptomatic remission with functional recovery.
- Quoting fixed “outcome thirds” as current truth.
- Treating one demographic feature as a dominant deterministic predictor.
- Presenting DUP as proven independent causation rather than a strong prognostic association.
- Giving one mortality or suicide percentage as though it applies to every cohort.
