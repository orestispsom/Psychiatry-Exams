# Q04. How do you assess suicidal intent and immediate suicide risk?

## Recall spine

1. **Suicidal ideation**
2. **Intent**
3. **Plan, preparation and access to means**
4. **Past suicidal behaviour**
5. **Mental state, substances and acute stressors**
6. **Protective factors and ability to collaborate**
7. **Dynamic formulation, disposition and safety plan**

## Model oral answer

I would assess suicide risk directly and specifically, but I would not pretend that I can predict an individual suicide. The aim is to establish current suicidal intent and capability, identify why danger may be elevated now, separate background from dynamic factors, and convert that formulation into an immediate safety and management plan.

I would begin by asking clearly about **current suicidal thoughts**: their frequency, persistence and controllability, whether the person wants to die, and whether there is current intent to act. I would then ask about a **plan and preparation**—method, timing, steps already taken, access to means, precautions against discovery and how lethal the person believes the method to be. Recent suicidal behaviour or rehearsal materially changes the assessment.

A crucial historical question is **previous attempts or self-harm**. I would establish what happened, the context and how similar the current situation is. I would also ask specifically about access to lethal means and any foreseeable near-term event that may increase danger.

The wider **mental state** matters because suicidal risk is often driven by dynamic syndromic factors. I would assess hopelessness, depression, psychosis, severe agitation, intoxication or withdrawal, substance misuse, impulsivity, acute interpersonal or other stressors, and any medical consequences of self-harm requiring treatment. I would also assess available supports and whether the person is able and willing to collaborate with a realistic safety plan.

At the same time, I would identify **protective factors and reasons for living**: relationships, responsibilities, future plans, beliefs, treatment engagement or other factors that genuinely reduce risk in this particular situation. Protective factors should not be used to cancel serious warning signs; they are part of the overall formulation.

The final step is a **dynamic risk formulation**, not a score. I would describe the person’s static or background vulnerabilities, the current modifiable drivers, likely short-term scenarios, what may worsen the situation and what interventions are likely to reduce danger. Global labels such as low, medium or high risk and numerical scores should not be used as if they predict suicide or determine discharge.

Disposition follows from that formulation. I would consider immediate safety, current mental state, intoxication or medical issues, engagement, available supports, access to means and whether the proposed safety plan can actually be implemented. The documentation should record the suicidal thoughts and intent, plan and means, past behaviour, dynamic drivers, protective factors, actions taken to reduce access to means, the safety plan and responsibility for follow-up. A bare statement such as “low risk” is not an adequate assessment.

## Must-know board facts

- Suicide risk scales and global risk categories do **not** provide reliable individual prediction and should not determine discharge or treatment decisions.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Asking only, “Are you suicidal?”
- Treating denial of current ideation as sufficient reassurance.
- Using low/medium/high categories as if they predict outcome.
- Listing risk factors without linking them to immediate management.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Risk formulation:** Older approaches often accumulated risk factors and assigned a categorical level of risk. Current practice places greater weight on an individualised, dynamic formulation that explains the present drivers of danger and directs management rather than claiming to predict suicide.
