# Q11. A 23-year-old presents with persecutory ideas, auditory hallucinations and social withdrawal. How would you approach the case?

## Recall spine

1. **Safety first** — risk, agitation, self-neglect, intoxication/withdrawal, delirium/catatonia, medical instability.
2. **Define the syndrome and course** — psychotic, negative, affective, disorganised and catatonic features.
3. **Collateral, substances and medications** — establish timing, exposure and functioning.
4. **Physical/neurological assessment** — investigate secondary causes selectively.
5. **Build the differential** — primary psychosis, mood-related psychosis, substance/medication-induced, medical/neurological.
6. **Engage and treat early** — early-intervention service, antipsychotic plus psychological/family care.
7. **Diagnose longitudinally** — first-episode psychosis is not automatically schizophrenia.

## Model oral answer

I would approach this as a **first episode of psychosis**, not as established schizophrenia. The first task is to determine whether there is an immediate safety or medical problem, then define the syndrome and its course, look actively for secondary causes, start appropriate early treatment, and keep the final diagnosis open until the longitudinal picture is clearer.

My immediate assessment would cover **risk to self and others**, severe agitation, self-neglect or inability to care for himself, intoxication or withdrawal, and signs of delirium, catatonia or other medical instability. The need for urgent containment or admission depends on clinical need and risk rather than on the diagnostic label alone.

I would then take a full psychiatric history and perform a detailed mental-state examination. I would establish the onset and evolution of the persecutory ideas, hallucinations and social withdrawal, and look systematically for other positive symptoms, disorganisation, negative symptoms, affective symptoms and catatonic features. Insight, change in social and occupational functioning, and the degree of behavioural disturbance are important. Collateral information is often essential, particularly for the timing of deterioration, substance use, functioning and possible previous episodes.

A careful **substance and medication history** is mandatory. I would establish what has been used, in what pattern and intensity, and how that exposure relates temporally to the psychosis. A positive toxicology result supports exposure but does not by itself prove that the substance caused the psychosis.

The assessment should also include a **physical and neurological examination**, followed by investigations directed by the history, examination and clinical suspicion of secondary causes. Routine structural brain imaging is not required in every otherwise uncomplicated first episode.

The principal differential is between a primary schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, a mood disorder with psychotic symptoms or schizoaffective presentation, substance- or medication-induced psychosis, and a medical or neurological cause. The distinction is usually made from the chronology, the wider syndrome and the course rather than from the content of a hallucination or delusion alone.

Where available, I would involve an **early-intervention-in-psychosis service without delay** and ensure multidisciplinary assessment. Once first-episode psychosis is established, treatment should combine an oral antipsychotic with psychological and family intervention. Antipsychotic choice should be shared with the patient, using lower-end licensed dosing principles and monitoring response, tolerability and engagement.

Finally, I would keep the diagnosis explicitly longitudinal. At presentation there may be enough evidence to identify psychosis, but not necessarily enough to determine the final disorder. The evolution of symptoms, mood episodes, substance relationship, functional course and persistence over time may change the diagnostic category.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q11a. There is heavy cannabis use. When would you call this substance-induced rather than a primary psychotic disorder?

A substance-induced formulation requires a plausible temporal relationship between cannabis intoxication or withdrawal and the psychosis, with symptoms exceeding ordinary intoxication or withdrawal effects. A primary psychotic disorder becomes more likely if psychosis clearly predates the relevant cannabis exposure, persists for a substantial period after cessation, or there have been previous psychotic episodes independent of cannabis. The assessment should include the pattern and intensity of use, the course during abstinence, collateral history and the wider longitudinal phenotype. There is no single universal abstinence interval that should be recited mechanically.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling the presentation schizophrenia immediately.
- Assuming cannabis causation from a positive urine test.
- Ordering routine structural brain imaging without a clinical indication.
- Treating psychological and family intervention as something reserved for chronic illness.
