# Q50. How do you diagnose and manage vascular cognitive impairment / vascular dementia?

## Recall spine

1. **Think in terms of the VCI spectrum**
2. **Establish a credible vascular relationship**
3. **Look for executive slowing and focal/gait clues**
4. **Use structural imaging, but not imaging alone**
5. **Do not require stepwise decline**
6. **Expect mixed Alzheimer/vascular pathology**
7. **Prioritise indicated vascular prevention, rehabilitation and function**

## Model oral answer

Vascular cognitive impairment is a **spectrum of cognitive disorder related to cerebrovascular disease**, with vascular dementia representing the dementia-level end of that spectrum. Diagnosis requires a credible relationship between the cognitive syndrome and vascular brain disease; neither a stepwise history nor white-matter change on imaging is sufficient by itself. Management is centred on appropriate vascular prevention, rehabilitation, function and safety.

The course is variable. A patient may deteriorate after a recognised stroke, show a classic **stepwise pattern**, or have a more gradual and progressive course, particularly with small-vessel disease. Stepwise decline is therefore a useful classic clue but not a diagnostic requirement.

The cognitive profile often differs from a purely amnestic Alzheimer presentation. **Executive dysfunction and slowed information processing** may be prominent, especially in subcortical or small-vessel disease. Focal neurological signs, gait disturbance and vascular risk factors support the diagnosis where present, but no single feature is obligatory.

I would establish the temporal relationship between vascular events and cognitive decline, obtain collateral history and assess functional consequences. Structural imaging should demonstrate a **cerebrovascular burden that is plausible for the clinical syndrome**. However, imaging abnormalities alone do not establish vascular dementia, because vascular changes may coexist with other pathology or may not explain the degree and pattern of impairment.

A major practical point is that **mixed pathology is common**. Alzheimer and vascular pathology should not be treated as mutually exclusive diagnoses. If the phenotype suggests both, the formulation should acknowledge both rather than forcing an artificial either/or label.

Management focuses on **optimising vascular risk factors and secondary stroke prevention according to standard cardiovascular and cerebrovascular indications**, together with treatment of relevant medical comorbidity. Antiplatelet or statin treatment should not be prescribed merely because a cognitive syndrome has been labelled “vascular”; each intervention still requires its ordinary vascular indication. Rehabilitation, mobility, communication, cognition, daily function and safety should be addressed according to the deficits present.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors or memantine should **not** be presented as routine treatment for pure vascular dementia. They may become appropriate when there is a comorbid or mixed neurodegenerative indication, such as suspected Alzheimer disease, PDD or DLB, but the treatment backbone of vascular cognitive impairment remains prevention of further indicated vascular injury and maximisation of function.

The key viva message is therefore to recognise the broader VCI spectrum, prove a clinically credible vascular contribution, avoid requiring a stereotyped stepwise course, consider mixed disease, and make evidence-based vascular prevention and rehabilitation central to management.

## Must-know board facts

- **Stepwise decline is classic but not required**.
- **VCI is broader than vascular dementia**.
- White-matter change or other vascular imaging findings are **not sufficient by themselves** to diagnose vascular dementia.
- Antiplatelets, statins and blood-pressure treatment follow **ordinary cardiovascular/cerebrovascular indications**; they are not automatically prescribed because cognition is labelled vascular.
- AChE inhibitors/memantine are **not routine for pure vascular dementia**; consider them when a comorbid Alzheimer/PDD/DLB indication is suspected.

## Examiner follow-up

### Q50a. What is CADASIL and what imaging pattern should make you think of it?

CADASIL is a hereditary small-vessel arteriopathy associated with **NOTCH3** pathogenic variants. In the right phenotype and family history, MRI involvement of the **anterior temporal poles and external capsules** is characteristic and can strongly support suspicion, but those imaging locations are **not pathognomonic**. Diagnosis integrates the clinical and family picture with appropriate genetic testing rather than being made from one MRI sign.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Diagnosing vascular dementia solely from white-matter changes.
- Treating gradual progression as evidence against VCI.
- Treating Alzheimer and vascular disease as mutually exclusive.
- Prescribing antiplatelets or statins solely because dementia is called vascular.
- Using cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine routinely for pure vascular dementia.
- Calling CADASIL anterior-temporal/external-capsule MRI changes pathognomonic.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Conceptual update:** Older descriptions often emphasise a stepwise course. Current vascular cognitive impairment includes post-stroke, stepwise, gradual and mixed trajectories.
