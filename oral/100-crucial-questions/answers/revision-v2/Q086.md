# Q86. How do you approach psychopharmacology in pregnancy and the reproductive period?

## Recall spine

1. **Maternal illness and relapse risk**
2. **Fetal/neonatal drug risk**
3. **Avoid reflexive abrupt discontinuation**
4. **Use the minimum effective regimen**
5. **Drug-specific reproductive evidence**
6. **Perinatal specialist input when high risk**
7. **Breastfeeding decisions are drug-specific**

## Model oral answer

Psychopharmacology in pregnancy is a **risk–benefit problem, not a simple drug-avoidance exercise**. Untreated severe psychiatric illness also carries important maternal and fetal risks, so an effective medication should not be reflexively stopped simply because pregnancy occurs. I would compare relapse risk with drug-specific fetal and neonatal risks, simplify to the minimum effective regimen where possible, and involve specialist perinatal care when the decision is high risk.

The first step is to establish the severity and recurrence pattern of the psychiatric disorder, previous response to treatment and the consequences of relapse. Abrupt discontinuation can itself create clinical risk. The aim is therefore informed shared decision-making rather than assuming that no medication is always safer.

**Valproate** is the clearest major restriction in bipolar treatment: it is contraindicated during pregnancy, with stringent reproductive regulation outside pregnancy as well. This should be addressed before conception whenever possible rather than discovered only after pregnancy is established.

**Lithium** should not be described through the old simplistic rule that it is forbidden because it causes Ebstein anomaly. Current evidence supports a **small increased cardiac-malformation risk**, lower than historic teaching suggested. For a patient with substantial relapse risk, selected continuation can therefore be considered under specialist monitoring rather than imposing an automatic prohibition.

**SSRIs** can be used when clinically indicated. Counselling should frame potential neonatal adaptation effects and the possible **small absolute risk of persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn** proportionately, alongside the risks of untreated maternal illness. The discussion should not be converted into alarmist percentage recitation.

**Lamotrigine** requires awareness that clearance can change during pregnancy and again postpartum, so exposure may not remain stable even when the prescribed dose does. Lithium pharmacokinetics also change during pregnancy. Dose adjustment should follow clinical state and, where appropriate, therapeutic monitoring rather than an automatic pregnancy or delivery formula.

Breastfeeding is similarly **drug-specific**. **Sertraline is commonly preferred when an SSRI is needed during breastfeeding because infant exposure is low.** Lithium can enter breast milk substantially; if it is considered, the decision requires specialist risk assessment and infant monitoring rather than one simple relative-infant-dose cut-off. Antipsychotics and benzodiazepines likewise require product- and infant-specific assessment.

The overall principle is to preserve maternal psychiatric stability while minimising avoidable reproductive risk: plan before conception where possible, avoid unnecessary polypharmacy, do not abruptly stop effective treatment by reflex, use specialist input for high-risk drugs or severe illness, and revisit the plan during pregnancy, postpartum and breastfeeding as the clinical context changes.

## Must-know board facts

- Pregnancy prescribing is **risk–benefit**, including the risk of untreated maternal illness.
- **Valproate is contraindicated during pregnancy** for bipolar disorder.
- Lithium carries a **small increased cardiac-malformation risk**, not the old simplistic “forbidden because of Ebstein anomaly” rule.
- Pregnancy can alter **lithium and lamotrigine pharmacokinetics**; dose changes are individualised rather than automatic.
- **Sertraline** is commonly preferred among SSRIs in breastfeeding because infant exposure is low.
- Lithium during breastfeeding requires **specialist individual risk assessment and infant monitoring** if considered; do not use one RID threshold as a universal prohibition.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Treating pregnancy as an automatic indication to stop all psychotropics abruptly.
- Describing lithium through simplistic historic Ebstein-anomaly teaching.
- Presenting reproductive risk without balancing the risk of severe untreated maternal illness.
- Automatically increasing or reducing lithium/lamotrigine doses according to one pregnancy formula.
- Using one relative-infant-dose cut-off as a universal breastfeeding rule.
- Turning the answer into an exhaustive drug-by-drug pregnancy catalogue.
