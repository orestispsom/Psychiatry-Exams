# Q28. How do you recognise and manage panic disorder and agoraphobia?

## Recall spine

1. **Recognise the panic attack**
2. **Establish recurrent unexpected attacks plus concern or behavioural change**
3. **Exclude medical, substance and medication causes**
4. **Assess agoraphobia separately**
5. **Use CBT with interoceptive and situational exposure**
6. **Use SSRI/SNRI pharmacotherapy when indicated**

## Model oral answer

Panic disorder is characterised by **recurrent unexpected panic attacks**, followed by persistent concern about further attacks or their consequences and/or maladaptive behavioural change. A panic attack itself is a transdiagnostic phenomenon, so one or even several attacks do not establish panic disorder. Agoraphobia should now be assessed as a separate diagnosis rather than treated simply as a panic-disorder specifier.

A panic attack is an abrupt surge or crescendo of intense fear or discomfort accompanied by prominent bodily and cognitive symptoms. These may include palpitations, chest discomfort, dyspnoea, dizziness, tremor, sweating, paraesthesiae and fears of losing control or dying. In panic disorder, I would clarify whether attacks are genuinely **unexpected**, how often they recur, whether the patient has developed anticipatory anxiety, and whether fear of further attacks has changed behaviour or functioning.

I would also assess the wider context because similar symptoms can occur in other psychiatric disorders and in medical or substance-related states. The history should include medication and substance exposure, intoxication or withdrawal where relevant, and clinical features suggesting cardiac, respiratory, endocrine or neurological causes. Investigations should be guided by the presentation rather than ordered as a universal battery.

**Agoraphobia** is assessed independently. The central problem is marked, persistent fear or avoidance of situations in which escape may feel difficult or help may be unavailable if incapacitating symptoms develop. Panic symptoms are common in agoraphobia but are not required. Panic disorder and agoraphobia can therefore coexist when both are independently present, but panic that occurs only in feared agoraphobic situations does not automatically establish panic disorder.

Treatment should combine explanation of the panic cycle with interventions that reverse avoidance. **CBT** is central and should include cognitive work together with **interoceptive exposure** to feared bodily sensations and **graded situational exposure** when agoraphobic avoidance is present. Interoceptive exposure deliberately evokes feared bodily sensations so that catastrophic interpretations and avoidance can be tested and weakened, rather than simply reassuring the patient that the sensations are harmless.

When medication is indicated or preferred, **SSRIs** are core long-term pharmacotherapy, with **SNRIs** also evidence-based options. Benzodiazepines are **not recommended as routine long-term treatment** because of tolerance, dependence and poorer long-term management considerations; they should not become the centre of the treatment plan.

The practical approach is therefore to distinguish panic attacks from panic disorder, exclude important secondary causes, assess agoraphobia independently, and treat the maintaining cycle of catastrophic interpretation and avoidance with exposure-based CBT, with SSRI or SNRI treatment when appropriate.

## Must-know board facts

- **Panic attacks are transdiagnostic** and are not synonymous with panic disorder.
- **DSM panic disorder:** after recurrent unexpected panic attacks, at least **1 month** of persistent concern about further attacks/consequences or maladaptive behavioural change.
- **DSM agoraphobia:** fear/anxiety involving at least **2 situation domains** and persisting for at least **6 months**. These are DSM-specific anchors; ICD-11 timing should not be silently replaced by DSM wording.
- **Current ICD-11:** panic disorder and agoraphobia are independent diagnoses.
- **Interoceptive exposure:** targets catastrophic interpretation and avoidance of bodily sensations by deliberately evoking them within treatment.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Calling every panic attack panic disorder.
- Missing cardiac, endocrine, substance or withdrawal presentations.
- Treating agoraphobia only as a complication or specifier of panic disorder.
- Making benzodiazepines the centre of long-term treatment.
- Calling long-term benzodiazepines universally “contraindicated” rather than not recommended for routine long-term management.

## Exam answer vs current practice

**Classification update:** Older “panic disorder with/without agoraphobia” architecture is historical. Current ICD-11 treats panic disorder and agoraphobia as separate diagnoses that may coexist.
