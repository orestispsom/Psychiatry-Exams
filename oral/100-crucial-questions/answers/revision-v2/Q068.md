# Q68. How do you classify and formulate personality disorder?

## Recall spine

1. **Identify enduring personality dysfunction**
2. **Rate severity**
3. **Describe ICD-11 trait domains**
4. **Use borderline qualifier when appropriate**
5. **Formulate development and interpersonal patterns**
6. **Assess comorbidity, strengths and risk**
7. **Keep ICD-11 and DSM frameworks separate**

## Model oral answer

Personality disorder is an **enduring and pervasive maladaptive pattern affecting self and interpersonal functioning**, arising by adolescence or early adulthood and producing clinically important dysfunction. In current ICD-11, the main framework is **dimensional rather than categorical**: first establish personality disorder, then rate severity and describe the dominant trait domains. A formulation is still essential because the diagnosis alone does not explain how the person’s difficulties developed or are maintained.

The first task is to establish that the pattern is persistent, cross-situational and not simply a state effect of an acute mood episode, psychosis, substance use or another disorder. I would examine identity, self-direction, relationships, emotional regulation, behaviour and the effect on work, intimacy and broader social functioning.

ICD-11 then grades personality-disorder **severity as mild, moderate or severe**. Severity is the organising diagnostic variable because it reflects the extent of dysfunction and risk more directly than simply assigning a traditional type label.

The personality style can then be described using five trait domains: **Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition and Anankastia**. An optional **Borderline Pattern qualifier** can also be applied. These traits should be used descriptively and proportionately rather than turning the answer back into a checklist of old categorical diagnoses.

That is distinct from the DSM-5-TR Section II system, which still retains the traditional named personality disorders and the familiar Cluster A, B and C grouping. In a viva I would state which classification system I am using rather than mixing an ICD-11 dimensional diagnosis with DSM categorical criteria as though they were one system.

The diagnosis should then be embedded in a **biopsychosocial and developmental formulation**. I would consider temperament, attachment and relationship history, adversity or trauma where relevant, learned schemas and coping strategies, recurrent interpersonal patterns, strengths and protective factors. I would also assess comorbid mood, anxiety, substance-use, neurodevelopmental and other psychiatric disorders and include self-harm, suicide, aggression, vulnerability and safeguarding in the risk formulation where relevant.

The practical value of the dimensional system is that two people with similar traditional labels may differ greatly in severity and trait configuration. Treatment planning should therefore follow the actual pattern of dysfunction, risk, comorbidity and goals rather than relying on a label alone.

## Examiner follow-ups

### Q68a. How do you distinguish avoidant personality disorder from social anxiety disorder?

An avoidant personality pattern is **pervasive across self-concept, relationships and interpersonal functioning**, whereas social anxiety disorder is organised around fear of scrutiny and negative evaluation in social situations. There is substantial overlap and they can coexist.

“Avoidant personality disorder” remains DSM and legacy terminology. In ICD-11, a similar phenotype is represented dimensionally, often through **Negative Affectivity and Detachment** rather than a separate avoidant category.

### Q68b. How do you distinguish OCPD from OCD?

An anankastic or OCPD pattern is a pervasive personality style characterised by **perfectionism, rigidity, order, control and overconscientiousness**. OCD is a separate obsessive-compulsive and related disorder defined by obsessions and/or compulsions.

The common shorthand that OCPD is ego-syntonic and OCD ego-dystonic is a **useful tendency, not an absolute discriminator**. In ICD-11 there is no separate OCPD/anankastic personality-disorder category; **Anankastia is a trait qualifier**, while OCD remains a separate disorder.

## Must-know board facts

- ICD-11 personality disorder: **mild / moderate / severe**.
- Five trait domains: **Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, Anankastia**.
- An optional **Borderline Pattern qualifier** may be added.
- Avoidant-pattern pathology is a pervasive personality/self-interpersonal pattern; social anxiety is centred on **scrutiny and negative evaluation**.
- OCPD/anankastic style versus OCD: **pervasive perfectionism/control** versus **obsessions and/or compulsions**; ego-syntonic/ego-dystonic is only a tendency.

## Common traps / examiner traps

- Mixing ICD-11 dimensional severity/traits with DSM categorical criteria as though they were one system.
- Treating traditional Cluster A/B/C groupings as current ICD-11 structure.
- Using ego-syntonic versus ego-dystonic as the sole OCD/OCPD discriminator.
- Diagnosing personality disorder from behaviour occurring only during an acute mood, psychotic or substance-related state.

## Exam answer vs current practice

Older ICD-10 and DSM-style teaching is primarily categorical. **ICD-11 is organised around dimensional severity plus trait qualifiers**, while DSM-5-TR Section II continues to retain named categorical diagnoses.
